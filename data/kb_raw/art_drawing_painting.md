# concepts(id|name|definition|category)
CO1|Mark Making|any visible trace left by tool on surface; foundation of all visual art; carries energy, intention, and character of the hand that made it|foundation
CO2|Observation|active seeing; analyzing visual reality for shape, value, edge, color, proportion, spatial relationship; learned skill, not innate talent|foundation
CO3|Abstraction|simplifying or distilling visual information; reducing to essential structures; all representation involves degrees of abstraction from reality|foundation
CO4|Representation|depicting recognizable subjects from observed or imagined reality; mimesis; naturalistic to stylized spectrum|foundation
CO5|Expression|conveying emotion, energy, or concept through visual means; not merely depicting but communicating feeling; brushwork, color, distortion, emphasis|foundation
CO6|Picture Plane|flat surface of drawing or painting; two-dimensional field on which illusion of three-dimensional space is constructed|foundation
CO7|Illusionistic Space|creating the appearance of depth and volume on flat surface through perspective, value, color, overlap, scale|foundation
CO8|Ground|prepared surface receiving marks; paper, canvas, panel, wall; ground color and texture affect all subsequent marks|foundation
CO9|Negative Space|area around and between subjects; shapes formed by the absence of the subject; equally important as positive shapes; powerful compositional tool|foundation
CO10|Positive Space|area occupied by the subject itself; the figure, object, or form depicted|foundation
CO11|Gesture|essential movement, energy, and rhythm of a form or pose captured through rapid, flowing marks; the life and action beneath surface detail|foundation
CO12|Contour|edge or outline defining the boundary between form and space; outer contour (silhouette) and inner contour (surface topology)|foundation
CO13|Mass|sense of weight, volume, and three-dimensionality of a form; created through value modeling, overlap, and foreshortening|foundation
CO14|Plane Change|point where surface direction shifts; creates edges (hard or soft) where light behavior changes; basis of constructive drawing and painting|foundation
CO15|Visual Weight|perceived heaviness of an element in composition; influenced by value (dark = heavy), size, color saturation, detail density, isolation, position|foundation
CO16|Unity|quality of all elements working together as coherent whole; nothing can be added or removed without weakening the work; coherence|foundation
CO17|Variety|differences and contrasts within unity; prevents monotony; engages the eye; must serve unity (variety without unity = chaos)|foundation
CO18|Optical Mixing|colors placed adjacent (not physically mixed) blend in the eye at distance; Impressionist and Pointillist technique; more luminous than palette mixing|foundation
CO19|Local Color|actual inherent color of an object independent of lighting conditions; green of grass, red of apple; modified by light, atmosphere, and reflected color in reality|foundation
CO20|Temperature Shift|warm-cool color variation across form; light source usually warm → shadow cool (or reverse); creates vibrating color and spatial depth|foundation
CO21|Economy|achieving maximum effect with minimum means; selective inclusion; knowing what to leave out; Matisse: "exactitude is not truth"|foundation

# elements(id|name|definition|properties|function)
EL1|Line|continuous mark with length and direction; defines edges, creates contour, implies movement; most fundamental element|weight (thick/thin); quality (smooth/rough, confident/tentative); direction (horizontal=calm, vertical=stable, diagonal=dynamic, curved=organic)|defines shape; creates movement; establishes rhythm; separates areas; describes contour; implies texture; carries expressive energy; calligraphic line has thick-thin variation
EL2|Shape|enclosed two-dimensional area defined by edge (line, value change, color change); geometric or organic|geometric (circle, square, triangle) = order, stability, human-made; organic (irregular, curvilinear) = natural, living; positive and negative shapes|organizes composition; creates pattern; defines objects; negative shapes as important as positive; simplified shapes underlie all complex forms; shapes interlock in strong compositions
EL3|Form|three-dimensional shape; has volume, mass, depth; created on 2D surface through value, perspective, and overlap|sphere, cube, cylinder, cone = four basic forms underlying all complex objects; forms have planes that catch light differently|creates illusion of 3D; provides structure for value mapping; all complex objects reducible to basic forms; understanding form = understanding how light behaves on surfaces
EL4|Value|relative lightness or darkness of a tone; independent of color; the most important element for creating illusion of form and space|scale from white (highest) to black (lowest); typically mapped on 5-10 step scale; squinting simplifies values; value structure = composition skeleton|creates form (light/shadow); establishes depth (atmospheric perspective); creates mood (high-key = light/airy; low-key = dark/dramatic); organizes composition; value does more work than color in most images
EL5|Color|visual perception of wavelength; described by hue, value, and chroma (saturation); most complex element; most seductive (can distract from value structure)|hue (position on color wheel); value (light/dark of color); chroma/saturation (intensity/purity); temperature (warm/cool)|creates mood; establishes light; defines space (warm advances, cool recedes); describes form (color shifts across surface); identifies objects; triggers emotional response; unifies or divides composition
EL6|Texture|surface quality (visual or tactile); actual texture (impasto, rough canvas) or implied texture (rendered appearance of bark, fabric, skin)|actual (physical surface); implied (depicted surface); coarse/fine; regular/irregular; hard/soft|adds sensory richness; creates variety; establishes material identity; creates focal areas (texture attracts eye); rough textures advance, smooth recede; contrast of textures creates interest
EL7|Space|sense of depth and distance on picture plane; created through overlap, size change, value change, color change, perspective, placement|shallow (compressed depth; near picture plane) vs deep (receding into illusionistic distance); open vs closed; positive vs negative|establishes environment; creates relationship between objects; determines viewer's position; atmospheric perspective = value and color shifts with distance; position on picture plane (higher = farther in landscape)
EL8|Edge|boundary between two areas; transition from one shape/value/color to another; controls focus and form|hard (sharp, sudden transition); soft (gradual, blended transition); lost (disappears into adjacent area); found (reappears after being lost)|hard edges attract eye (focal point); soft edges recede; lost edges create mystery and connection; found edges define key forms; edge variety essential — all hard = rigid/cutout; all soft = unfocused/blurry; lost-and-found edges create life

# principles(id|name|definition|mechanism|application)
PR1|Contrast|juxtaposition of opposing elements; light/dark, large/small, rough/smooth, warm/cool, busy/quiet|creates visual interest; directs attention; establishes hierarchy; prevents monotony; greatest contrast = focal point|value contrast strongest tool for focal point; color contrast for vibrancy; texture contrast for tactile interest; contrast of detail (rendered vs simplified) directs attention; simultaneous contrast: adjacent colors/values affect each other's perception
PR2|Balance|distribution of visual weight creating sense of equilibrium; symmetrical or asymmetrical|symmetrical: formal, stable, static, dignified; asymmetrical: dynamic, natural, more interesting; radial: from center point|large dark shape balanced by small bright one; heavy form near center balanced by lighter form far from center; color intensity balances area; visual weight considers position (elements near edge have more leverage)
PR3|Rhythm|repetition of elements creating visual movement and pattern; regular or irregular|repetition creates expectation; variation within repetition creates interest; rhythm directs eye through composition; staccato (sharp, interrupted) vs legato (smooth, flowing)|repeated shapes at varying scales; color echoed across composition; directional brushstrokes creating flow; architectural repetition (windows, columns); natural rhythm (waves, leaves, folds)
PR4|Emphasis (Focal Point)|drawing viewer's attention to most important area; hierarchy of visual importance|created by: greatest value contrast; sharpest edges; most saturated color; greatest detail; convergence of lines; isolation; human faces and eyes naturally attract|primary focal point: where story centers; secondary focal points: support primary; avoid equal emphasis everywhere (no hierarchy = confusion); avoid edges and corners for focal point; every element should lead toward or support focal area
PR5|Proportion|size relationships between parts and between parts and whole; comparative measurement; relational|correct proportion creates believability; distorted proportion creates expression or unease; golden ratio and root rectangles historically used for pleasing proportions|sight-size method: hold measuring tool at arm's length; comparative measurement: use one unit (head height) to measure all others; proportional dividers; common error: drawing what you know (symbols) rather than what you see (proportions)
PR6|Movement|directing viewer's eye through the composition; visual path; flow|created by: directional lines; overlapping shapes; value gradients; gesture; placement of elements; gaze direction of figures|eye enters composition (usually at focal point or corner entry); travels through via directional elements; rests at focal point; continues to explore; eventually returns; avoid leading eye out of picture
PR7|Harmony|elements sharing common qualities; relatedness; family resemblance|limited palette; related shapes; consistent edge quality; analogous colors; similar textures; repeated motifs|harmony without variety = monotony; variety without harmony = chaos; dominant element (color, shape, value range) unifies; subordinate variations within dominance create interest
PR8|Gradation|gradual transition from one state to another; value gradation, color gradation, size gradation, edge gradation|creates smooth transitions; models curved surfaces; atmospheric depth; transitions between areas; prevents jarring jumps|value gradation across form (core shadow to highlight); color gradation (warm to cool across landscape); size gradation (large foreground to small background); atmosphere: value and saturation decrease with distance

# color(id|name|definition|properties|application)
CL1|Hue|pure color identity on spectrum/color wheel; red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet; identified by dominant wavelength|12-hue wheel: 3 primaries + 3 secondaries + 6 tertiaries; warm hues (red-yellow) vs cool hues (blue-violet); warm/cool relative within any hue (warm red vs cool red)|hue identifies color family; limited hue range = harmony; complementary hues = maximum contrast; hue shift across form indicates light quality; local color = hue identity of object
CL2|Value (of Color)|lightness or darkness of a color; pink = high-value red; maroon = low-value red; independent of hue|every color has inherent value (yellow lightest, violet darkest); tints (add white) raise value; shades (add black/complement) lower; value structure must work in grayscale|most important property; painting can succeed with wrong hue if values are right; cannot succeed with right hues and wrong values; squint to check value relationships; value study before color painting
CL3|Chroma (Saturation)|intensity or purity of color; high chroma = vivid; low chroma = muted/neutralized; fully desaturated = gray|neutralized by adding complement, black, gray, or earth tones; distance from neutral gray; prismatic colors have highest chroma; earth colors are low chroma|full chroma throughout = garish; selective high chroma at focal point; atmospheric perspective reduces chroma with distance; shadows generally lower chroma than light areas (with exceptions); high chroma attracts eye (use at focal point)
CL4|Color Temperature|relative warmth (toward red/orange/yellow) or coolness (toward blue/green/violet) of a color|warm advances spatially, cool recedes; warm light → cool shadows (most common); cool light → warm shadows; every color has warmer and cooler variants|temperature variation across form creates vibration and life; warm light: highlight = warm, shadow = cool; cool light (overcast): highlight = cool, shadow = warm; warm/cool contrast more subtle and effective than complementary contrast for form
CL5|Complementary Colors|colors opposite on color wheel; red-green, blue-orange, yellow-violet; maximum hue contrast|mixed together → neutral gray (pigment) or white (light); placed adjacent → vibrate optically; each intensifies the other; afterimage effect|vibrating edges where complements meet; shadows contain complement of light color (orange light → blue-violet shadow); mix complement to neutralize (better than adding black); complementary accent against dominant color = powerful focal point
CL6|Analogous Colors|colors adjacent on color wheel; share common hue component; inherently harmonious|easy harmony; limited contrast; monochromatic is extreme case (one hue, varied value and chroma); temperature range within analogous is subtle|most natural color schemes; landscape often analogous (greens, yellow-greens, blue-greens); harmonious but may lack energy; add small complementary accent for vibrancy
CL7|Color Mixing (Subtractive)|combining pigments; each pigment absorbs (subtracts) wavelengths; resulting color is what's not absorbed; primaries: red, yellow, blue (traditional) or magenta, yellow, cyan (process)|more mixing → duller (more wavelengths absorbed); clean color = fewer pigments; two-pigment mixes cleaner than three; mud results from over-mixing or mixing across full color wheel|mix on palette with palette knife or brush; premix major color notes before painting; avoid over-mixing (leave slight variation in stroke); understand each pigment's bias (warm red vs cool red) for clean secondaries
CL8|Color Mixing (Additive)|combining light; overlapping colored lights; primaries: red, green, blue (RGB); more mixing → brighter; relevant for digital art, stage lighting, understanding light|mixing all primaries → white; relevant for understanding how light works (vs pigment); digital painting operates in additive space on screen, viewed as additive|understanding additive mixing explains why shadows aren't simply darker versions of light color; light is additive; pigment is subtractive; complementary pairs in additive = cyan/red, magenta/green, yellow/blue
CL9|Color Key|overall value and chroma range of a painting; high-key (mostly light values), low-key (mostly dark), full-range; and chroma equivalent|establishes mood; high-key = airy, optimistic, ethereal; low-key = dramatic, mysterious, somber; limited chroma key = subtle, sophisticated; high chroma key = energetic, naive|decided before painting; affects palette selection; key should be consistent (avoid drifting between keys); limited value range within key creates harmony; expanding range at focal point creates emphasis within key
CL10|Color Harmony|pleasing or coherent color relationships; based on color wheel position, value relationship, temperature relationship|complementary, analogous, triadic, split-complementary, tetradic; dominant-subordinate relationship; mother color (one color mixed into all others)|one dominant temperature (warm or cool); one dominant value range; accents of opposite; limited palette forces harmony; masterful colorists often use surprisingly few colors; color harmony serves composition and mood (not decorative end in itself)
CL11|Gamut Mapping|limiting palette to intentional subset of full color range; choosing which colors you will and won't use|creates unity; prevents garish combinations; mimics the color character of specific light conditions|Munsell-based gamut mapping: plot intended colors in 3D color space; limiting palette (e.g., Zorn palette: yellow ochre, ivory black, cadmium red, white) creates inherent harmony; gamut mask technique (James Gurney)
CL12|Simultaneous Contrast|adjacent colors affect each other's appearance; gray appears warmer next to cool, cooler next to warm; complementary halos|colors appear more saturated adjacent to their complement; values appear lighter against dark background and darker against light; Chevreul's law|exploit for luminosity (place complement in shadow → light appears more vivid); be aware when judging color in isolation vs in context; test color relationships on painting surface not on palette; studio lighting must be neutral or context misleads

# drawing(id|name|definition|method|application)
DR1|Gesture Drawing|rapid capture (30 seconds to 5 minutes) of essential movement, rhythm, and energy of subject; longest axis first; flowing continuous line|start with line of action (C-curve or S-curve of primary movement); work inside-out from major gesture to secondary rhythms; proportion approximate; detail omitted|foundation of all figure and animal drawing; warm-up exercise; captures what static careful drawing misses; trains eye-hand coordination and rapid observation; gesture exists in still life and landscape too (the energy of how forms relate and move)
DR2|Contour Drawing|following the edge of form with the eye and simultaneously with drawing tool; slow, careful observation of edges|blind contour: never look at paper (trains observation); modified contour: occasional glances at paper; cross-contour: lines following surface topology across form|develops observational intensity; creates sensitive line quality; blind contour especially valuable for breaking symbol-drawing habits; cross-contour describes form and surface direction
DR3|Constructive Drawing|building form from basic geometric solids (sphere, cube, cylinder, cone); establishing structure before surface|block in major forms as simple solids; find center lines, cross-sections; establish proportion with geometric measurement; add complexity over structure|figure drawing (head = sphere + jaw block; torso = modified cylinder; limbs = tapered cylinders); architectural drawing; product design; enables drawing from imagination because structure is understood
DR4|Value Drawing|creating illusion of form and light through tonal gradation; no line (or line subordinated to value); chiaroscuro|map light pattern: light, halftone, core shadow, reflected light, cast shadow; squint to simplify into 2-3 values; build gradually from light to dark (additive) or carve light from dark (reductive)|most effective method for creating convincing 3D form on 2D surface; value drawing skill transfers directly to painting; charcoal and graphite primary media; eraser as drawing tool (subtractive method)
DR5|Line Quality / Calligraphic Line|variation in line weight, speed, darkness, and character; thick-thin, fast-slow, dark-light within single stroke|press harder for thick/dark; lighter for thin/light; fast marks = energy; slow marks = precision; overlap marks at corners; taper at ends; line weight variation describes form (thicker at shadow side)|creates visual interest; describes form without value modeling (thick at shadow, thin at light); conveys material (heavy line = heavy object; light line = delicate); calligraphic quality distinguishes accomplished drawing from mechanical
DR6|Measuring and Proportion|comparative measurement techniques for achieving accurate size relationships|sight-size: hold pencil at arm's length, measure apparent size, transfer 1:1 to drawing surface; comparative: use one measurement as unit (head = 1 unit), measure everything relative to it; triangulation: check angles between landmark points|most common drawing error is incorrect proportion; measuring combats drawing from symbols (what you think vs what you see); check proportions by measuring negative spaces; flip drawing upside down or view in mirror to check
DR7|Envelope and Scaffolding|beginning drawing with largest simple containing shapes before refining interior detail; work large to small, simple to complex|draw bounding shape (envelope) of entire subject; subdivide into major masses; locate key points within scaffold; refine proportions within established framework|prevents detail fixation (starting with eye when head isn't placed); ensures proportional accuracy at every stage; enables corrections early (moving a line is easy, moving a finished eye is hard); architectural approach to organic subjects
DR8|Thumbnail Sketch|small (2-5 cm), rapid sketch exploring composition, value pattern, and design before committing to full work|3-5 minutes per thumbnail; work in 2-3 values (no detail); try multiple compositions; explore portrait vs landscape, focal point placement, value distribution|essential pre-planning; prevents major compositional errors; tests ideas cheaply; do 5-10 before selecting; thumbnail should read at arm's length as abstract value pattern; time invested here saves hours correcting full-size work
DR9|Hatching and Cross-Hatching|building value through parallel lines (hatching) or intersecting parallel lines (cross-hatching)|line spacing controls value (closer = darker); line direction can follow form (contour hatching) or be systematic (mechanical hatching); cross-hatching layers at angles|pen and ink primary technique; also pencil, charcoal; contour hatching describes form while building value; cross-hatch at consistent angles (don't scribble); optical gray: fine hatching at distance reads as smooth tone
DR10|Stippling|building value through dots; density and size of dots create tonal gradation|closer dots = darker value; larger dots = darker value; extremely time-intensive; produces unique luminous quality|pen and ink technique; pointillist quality; very slow but meditative; cannot be rushed; best for final rendering not sketching; value range limited by paper white and ink density
DR11|Schematic / Diagrammatic Drawing|simplified structural drawing for understanding rather than depiction; anatomical diagrams, perspective constructions, compositional schemas|reduce subject to essential geometric relationships; annotate; show underlying structure that finished drawing conceals|learning tool; planning tool; understanding anatomy, perspective, composition; not an end product but enables better final work; draw what you need to understand, not what would look good

# painting(id|name|definition|method|application)
PA1|Underpainting|first layer establishing value structure and/or color temperature before subsequent layers; monochromatic (grisaille, verdaccio) or color|thin paint (diluted); establish major shapes and values; let dry before overpainting; warm underpainting (burnt sienna) for cool subject; cool underpainting (blue-gray) for warm|alla prima can skip underpainting; indirect painting depends on it; value established in underpainting simplifies color decisions; shows through subsequent transparent layers enriching color; error correction easier at this stage
PA2|Blocking In|covering entire canvas with approximate colors and values in first painting pass; establishing major relationships before refining any area|mix major color notes on palette; apply broadly with large brush; cover white canvas quickly (white canvas distorts value judgment); work all areas simultaneously; no detail|prevents piecemeal painting (finishing one area before starting others); establishes relationships that inform all subsequent decisions; resist the temptation to refine too early; ugly stage is necessary — trust the process
PA3|Alla Prima (Direct Painting)|completing painting in single session; wet-into-wet; no underpainting or layering; paint applied at full thickness from start|mix accurate color notes before applying; work from dark to light or general to specific; each stroke placed deliberately with correct value, color, temperature; minimal blending on canvas|freshness; spontaneous quality; bold brushwork preserved; demands decisive color mixing and confident paint application; ideal for plein air (changing light limits time); time constraint forces essential decisions (what matters most?)
PA4|Glazing|applying thin transparent paint layer over dried opaque layer; light passes through glaze, reflects off opaque layer beneath, passes through glaze again|thin paint with medium (linseed oil + solvent for oil; water for watercolor/acrylic); dark over light; transparent pigments only (not opaque); each layer must dry completely|creates luminous depth impossible with direct mixing; deepens shadows without losing transparency; modifies color without covering; Old Master technique; slow (drying time between layers); builds depth over many sessions
PA5|Scumbling|applying thin opaque or semi-opaque light paint over darker dried layer; dry-brush technique; opposite of glazing|stiff brush, little paint, dragged across dry surface; light catches raised texture; creates optical mixing of layers; broken color; atmospheric haze effect|creates atmospheric effects (haze, glow, softness); warm scumble over cool = vibrating luminosity; useful for suggesting distance, atmosphere, soft light; textural interest; corrective technique (modify area without repainting)
PA6|Impasto|thick paint application; visible brushwork or palette knife marks; three-dimensional surface texture|apply with brush or palette knife; paint used thickly, sometimes extruded directly from tube; texture catches real light creating literal 3D surface|draws eye (tactile interest → focal point); creates energy; captures light (real light, not depicted light); textural contrast with thin areas; expressive mark-making; Van Gogh, Frank Auerbach, Lucian Freud
PA7|Wet-Into-Wet|applying paint into still-wet paint; colors blend on canvas surface; soft transitions|work quickly before paint sets; add wet color adjacent to or over wet color; blending occurs naturally; control blending with pressure and brush angle|soft edges; atmospheric effects; cloud painting; skin tones; gradations; oil paint stays wet longest (hours to days); acrylic dries fast (retarder medium extends working time); watercolor is inherently wet-into-wet
PA8|Color Mixing on Palette|premixing colors before applying to canvas; controlled; predictable; clean color strings|mix with palette knife or brush on flat palette; create value strings (same hue at 3-5 values); premix all major notes before painting; isolate clean mixing area|enables accurate color placement; color strings for organized approach; premixing prevents over-manipulation on canvas (mud); palette organization reflects painting organization; limited palette forces color understanding
PA9|Plein Air Painting|painting outdoors directly from nature; en plein air; Impressionist tradition; captures natural light, atmosphere, and color|portable setup (pochade box, limited palette, small canvas); limited time (2-3 hours before light changes significantly); simple composition; focus on light effect; notan (value pattern) decided first|trains observation of natural light and color; light temperature changes constantly (work fast); simplify ruthlessly (you cannot paint everything you see); concentrate on light effect not objects; translate what you see, don't copy; each session = one light condition
PA10|Color String|premixed sequence of one color at graduated values from dark to light; 3-5 steps; organized on palette|e.g., background color string: darkest → medium → lightest; flesh color string: shadow → halftone → light → highlight; each step mixed with appropriate modifiers (add white, add complement, etc.)|organized palette; speeds painting; ensures value control within each color; industrial-strength color preparation before painting; enables accurate color placement with confidence; Richard Schmid approach
PA11|Lost and Found Edges|selectively sharpening some edges and softening or losing others; key to creating focus and atmosphere in painting|hard edges at focal point (maximum contrast and detail); soft edges in transitions and secondary areas; lost edges where values or colors merge (shadow into background, turning form into space)|most powerful compositional tool in painting; creates visual hierarchy; directs eye; prevents the cut-out look of uniformly hard edges; creates atmosphere and mystery; Sargent, Zorn, Sorolla mastered this
PA12|Broken Color|applying distinct strokes of different colors side by side without fully blending; colors mix optically at viewing distance|individual strokes visible; each stroke slightly different hue, value, or temperature from neighbors; viewed close: mosaic; viewed distant: unified color|more luminous than premixed flat color (optical mixing preserves chroma); creates vibration and life; Impressionist and Post-Impressionist technique; requires understanding of color relationships; bold, confident stroke placement

# figure(id|name|definition|proportions_or_structure|application)
FG1|Proportions (Idealized Adult)|classical proportional system for human figure|total height ≈ 7.5-8 heads (heroic = 8; actual average closer to 7.5); head = 1 unit; chin to nipple = 1; nipple to navel = 1; navel to pubic = 1; pubic symphysis = halfway point; arm span ≈ height; hand ≈ face length; foot ≈ forearm (elbow to wrist)|starting framework, not absolute rule; actual proportions vary by individual, age, sex, ethnicity; measuring from life always supersedes formula; useful for checking and for drawing from imagination
FG2|Skeleton (Structural Landmarks)|bony landmarks visible or palpable through surface; anchor points for figure construction|clavicle; acromion process; scapula spine; iliac crest; anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS); greater trochanter; patella; malleoli; rib cage; sternum; vertebral spinous processes|skeletal landmarks don't move with muscle contraction (unlike soft tissue); provide reliable reference points; understanding skeletal structure explains surface form; gesture of spine and pelvis = gesture of figure
FG3|Major Masses|simplification of figure into large interconnected volumes; head, rib cage, pelvis as primary masses connected by spine|head ≈ egg/sphere + jaw wedge; rib cage ≈ barrel/egg; pelvis ≈ bucket/butterfly; these three masses tilt, twist, and shift relative to each other; limbs attach to and swing from these masses|figure drawing begins with these three masses in correct relationship; contrapposto = opposing tilt of rib cage and pelvis; all poses reducible to arrangement of three masses; understand masses before surface anatomy
FG4|Contrapposto|weight shift producing asymmetric balance; weight on one leg; pelvis tilts; spine curves to compensate; shoulders counter-tilt; creates dynamic equilibrium|weight-bearing side: hip rises, shoulder drops; free side: hip drops, shoulder rises; S-curve through torso; Greek invention (Polykleitos' Doryphoros); creates life in standing figure|most natural standing pose; immediately more interesting than symmetric pose; understanding contrapposto = understanding how weight distributes through figure; exaggeration of contrapposto = more dynamic pose; every standing figure uses some degree
FG5|Muscle Groups (Surface Anatomy)|muscles visible at surface creating form; organized by function and location|torso: pectoralis major, rectus abdominis, external oblique, latissimus dorsi, trapezius, erector spinae; arm: deltoid, biceps, triceps, forearm flexors/extensors; leg: quadriceps, hamstrings, gluteals, gastrocnemius, tibialis anterior|surface anatomy explains the bumps, ridges, and hollows of figure; know origin, insertion, and action of major muscle groups; muscles change shape dramatically with action (flexed vs extended); fat layer modifies visibility (leaner = more visible anatomy)
FG6|Head Construction|systematic method for drawing the head from any angle; Loomis, Reilly, Asaro head methods|Loomis: sphere (cranium) + block (jaw); center line follows curve of sphere; brow line at equator; face divided into thirds (hairline-brow-nose base-chin); eye line at center of head (not center of face); ear between brow and nose base|most common error: face too large relative to cranium (eyes too high); head is a sphere, not a flat oval; all features wrap around curved surface; master the basic head before portraits; planes of the head (Asaro head) teach light behavior
FG7|Hands and Feet|most complex appendage forms; require separate study; hands especially expressive and revealing of character|hand: palm ≈ square; fingers ≈ palm length; middle finger longest; thumb opposes; draw as mitten first then separate fingers; foot: wedge shape; arch creates inner curve; toes shorter than expected; ankle bones asymmetric (inner higher)|draw hands as shapes first (wedge, block, mitten) before articulating fingers; observe your own hand constantly; feet carry weight (contact with ground must be convincing); shoes simplify foot form; hands and feet are secondary portraits revealing age, labor, character
FG8|Drapery and Cloth|fabric folds over and around the body; cloth has its own logic based on support points, tension, and fabric weight|seven types of folds: pipe, zigzag, spiral, half-lock, diaper, drop, inert; cloth hangs from support points (shoulder, breast, hip); tension between support points creates folds; heavier fabric = fewer, larger folds; lighter = more, smaller|cloth reveals form beneath (taut fabric) and conceals (loose fabric); fold lines radiate from support points; understand the body beneath the cloth first; fabric type determines fold character (silk vs burlap vs wool); shadow pattern of folds follows same light logic as body
FG9|Foreshortening|apparent compression of form oriented toward or away from viewer; limb pointing at viewer appears shorter than its actual length|draw what you see (apparent shape), not what you know (actual shape); foreshortened forms become rounder (cylinder viewed end-on → circle); overlap is primary depth cue in foreshortening; value modeling supports compression|most common error: drawing the known length rather than the seen length; trust measurement over knowledge; envelope the foreshortened form first, then detail; extreme foreshortening = extreme overlap
FG10|Weight and Balance|depicting the figure in convincing gravitational relationship with ground; center of gravity must fall over base of support|plumb line from pit of neck should fall within base of support (feet, or feet + prop); leaning figures compensate (extend limb, shift mass); sitting figures redistribute weight; contact points must be convincing|figure that doesn't look grounded feels wrong even if every muscle is rendered perfectly; check plumb line in every figure drawing; feel the weight in your own body to understand the pose; weight = compressed forms at contact, stretched forms pulling away

# composition(id|name|definition|method|application)
CM1|Rule of Thirds|divide picture plane into 3×3 grid; place focal point at intersection; avoids centered, static placement|imaginary grid divides canvas into nine zones; four intersection points = strong positions for focal point; horizon on upper or lower third (not center)|starting guideline (not rule); more dynamic than centered; provides quick compositional check; can be transcended once understood; many masterworks violate it successfully
CM2|Golden Section / Golden Ratio|φ ≈ 1.618; ratio appearing in nature and considered aesthetically pleasing; rectangle, spiral, division of line|divide line so that whole:larger = larger:smaller (≈ 1.618:1); golden rectangle; golden spiral; Fibonacci sequence approximates|historically used in architecture, painting, composition; divisional tool (not prescriptive grid); creates asymmetric but harmonious division; present in many masterworks (debated whether intentional or retrospective analysis)
CM3|Value Pattern (Notan)|organization of light and dark masses into clear, bold pattern; Japanese concept: harmony of light and dark|simplify entire composition to 2-3 values; dominant light or dominant dark; pattern should read as abstract design independent of subject|most important compositional decision; thumbnail in 2 values tests notan; strong notan = strong painting regardless of rendering; weak notan = weak painting regardless of skill; squinting at any stage reveals notan
CM4|Leading Lines|linear elements directing viewer's eye through composition toward focal point|roads, rivers, fences, edges, implied lines (gaze direction), perspective convergence; S-curve particularly pleasing; avoid leading lines pointing out of frame|provide visual pathway; create depth (converging lines); connect elements; should lead to focal point not away; avoid arrow-straight lines pointing at corners; curved lines more natural than straight
CM5|Framing (Within Image)|using elements within scene to frame subject; doorway, archway, tree branches, window|foreground elements create frame around subject; darkening frame vignettes toward focal point; frame creates sense of depth (foreground layer)|natural depth creation; directs attention inward; creates sense of looking through to subject; dark frame + light subject = strong value contrast at focal point; avoid overly symmetrical framing (becomes gimmicky)
CM6|Grouping and Clustering|organizing multiple elements into groups rather than scattering evenly; clusters create hierarchy and interest|3-5 elements group naturally; odd numbers more dynamic than even; overlapping elements unify groups; spacing between groups creates visual breathing room|avoid checkerboard distribution (evenly spaced = boring); cluster trees, figures, objects; vary group sizes; larger group = primary; smaller = secondary; isolation of single element creates emphasis through contrast
CM7|Counterpoint|secondary element opposing or balancing primary element; visual dialogue between areas|warm vs cool; light vs dark; detailed vs simplified; geometric vs organic; large shape vs small accent; horizontal calm vs diagonal energy|creates tension and visual interest; prevents one-note composition; counterpoint area is subordinate to dominant; too much counterpoint = chaos; too little = monotony; like musical counterpoint — related but independent voices
CM8|Cropping|deciding what to include and exclude; edges of composition; relationship of subject to frame|tight crop = intimate, intense, confrontational; loose crop = environmental, contextual; asymmetric crop = dynamic; cutting subject at edge implies continuation beyond frame|what you leave out defines the composition as much as what you include; Degas: radical cropping from Japanese prints; cutting figures at limb joints feels amputative (cut between joints); crop from reference photo to strengthen composition before painting
CM9|Entry and Exit Points|where viewer's eye enters and leaves the composition; controlled by edge treatment|soft edges at borders allow gentle entry; hard edges near border risk eye exiting; corners are natural exit points (avoid placing high contrast there); implied lines directing eye inward from edges|viewer's eye typically enters at area of highest contrast; should be guided through composition and returned; strong compositions have no obvious exit; barrier elements near edges keep eye in picture; vignetting (darkening edges) is traditional solution
CM10|Figure-Ground Relationship|relationship between subject (figure) and background (ground); clarity of separation; intentional ambiguity|clear figure-ground: subject distinct from background; ambiguous: figure and ground switch (Escher, Rubin vase); interlocking: figure and ground equally important as shapes|strong figure-ground contrast creates readability; too little = subject lost; consider negative space as shape (not just leftover space); vary edge quality between figure and ground (some edges hard, some lost)

# perspective(id|name|definition|method|application)
PE1|One-Point Perspective|all parallel lines receding into depth converge at single vanishing point on horizon line (eye level)|establish horizon line (eye level); place vanishing point; all depth lines converge to VP; verticals remain vertical; horizontals remain horizontal|interior views; corridors; roads; tunnels; looking straight down railroad tracks; simplest perspective system; creates strong depth with single VP
PE2|Two-Point Perspective|two vanishing points on horizon line; used when viewing corner of object; no surface parallel to picture plane|establish horizon; two VPs (widely spaced, often off-canvas); vertical edges remain vertical; horizontal edges converge to left or right VP; each wall plane has its own VP|buildings at angle; most common for architectural subjects; cube viewed from corner; VPs too close together → distortion; keep VPs well-separated
PE3|Three-Point Perspective|three vanishing points; third VP above (looking up: worm's eye) or below (looking down: bird's eye); no edges parallel to picture plane|horizon line with 2 VPs + third VP above or below; all three axes converge to their respective VP; creates dynamic, dramatic viewpoint|tall buildings viewed from below; aerial views looking down; dramatic angles; comic book perspective; most dynamic but most distortion-prone
PE4|Atmospheric (Aerial) Perspective|distant objects appear lighter in value, lower in contrast, cooler in color temperature, and less detailed than near objects|value: compress value range toward middle with distance; color: shift toward blue-gray with distance; edge: softer with distance; detail: less with distance|creates convincing depth without linear perspective; essential for landscape painting; works at any scale (even across a room); Leonardo codified; amount of atmospheric effect depends on conditions (humid = more; dry/high altitude = less)
PE5|Foreshortening (Perspective on Form)|forms oriented toward/away from viewer appear compressed along their length; circles become ellipses; rectangles become trapezoids|draw the apparent shape (what you see) not the known shape (what you know); near end of foreshortened form appears larger than far end; overlap establishes which end is nearer|most difficult perspective concept to internalize; figure drawing: arms pointing at viewer, legs receding; still life: ellipses on cylinders; architecture: columns, arches; trust your eye and measurement over your knowledge
PE6|Ellipses|circles in perspective; become narrower ellipses as angle to viewer decreases; round at 90° (looking straight down), narrowest near eye level, hidden at 0°|minor axis of ellipse points toward VP; width doesn't change with tilt (only apparent height); no corners or points (common error); consistently curve (no flat spots)|every cylindrical object: cups, wheels, plates, columns, hats; ellipses must be rounder at bottom of cylinder and narrower at top (when eye level is above); most common error: pointed ends (football shape instead of ellipse)
PE7|Measuring in Perspective|determining proportional sizes of objects at different depths; objects diminish predictably with distance|measuring line: establish known size at picture plane; project measurement lines to VP; any depth point can be measured proportionally; diagonals of rectangles find midpoints (for spacing fence posts, windows)|consistent scaling prevents size errors; evenly-spaced objects (fence posts, columns) require perspective diminution; the diagonal method: divide rectangle in half by crossing diagonals; transfer half-width to find next spacing
PE8|Eye Level / Horizon Line|horizontal line at viewer's eye height; all vanishing points for horizontal planes sit on this line; determines viewer's position|sitting = low horizon; standing = middle; elevated = high horizon; horizon always at eye level regardless of head tilt; objects above eye level: see bottom; objects below: see top|the most important single line in any perspective setup; determines viewer's relationship to scene; low horizon = subject looming above (dramatic, monumental); high horizon = looking down (map-like, God's-eye); eye level in figure drawing = where on the model the viewer's eyes align

# techniques(id|name|definition|medium|purpose)
TK1|Chiaroscuro|dramatic contrast between light and dark; strong directional light creating deep shadows and bright highlights; theatrical lighting|all (originated in drawing and painting)|creates dramatic mood; strong three-dimensional form; Caravaggio, Rembrandt; tenebrism = extreme version; single light source simplifies and dramatizes
TK2|Sfumato|extremely gradual, imperceptible transitions between tones and colors; no visible brushstrokes or hard edges; smoky, hazy quality|oil paint (slow drying enables)|creates atmospheric softness; mystery; spatial depth; Leonardo da Vinci's signature technique (Mona Lisa); achieved through many thin transparent layers; opposite of hard-edged approach
TK3|Grisaille|painting executed entirely in gray monochrome; establishes value structure before color; or as finished work (stone or bronze simulation)|oil, acrylic, watercolor|separates value decisions from color decisions; underpainting technique; trompe l'oeil simulation of stone sculpture; educational exercise isolating value
TK4|Verdaccio|underpainting in green-gray tones (terre verte + white); traditional fresco and oil technique; particularly for flesh|oil, tempera, fresco|green underpainting complements warm flesh tones; shadows retain cool green; warm flesh glazed over provides optical complexity; Italian Renaissance technique
TK5|Wipe-Out / Reductive Drawing|covering surface with tone (charcoal, thinned paint) then erasing/wiping to create lights; drawing by subtraction|charcoal on paper; thinned oil paint on toned canvas|trains seeing light (not edges); sculptural approach (carving form from darkness); fast value study; historical technique for oil painting underdrawing; meditative process
TK6|Sgraffito|scratching through upper layer to reveal layer beneath; creates texture and line through removal|oil paint, acrylic, clay (pottery), mixed media|textural contrast; linear detail in painted surface; reveals underpainting color; adds graphic quality; tool marks create unique texture
TK7|Toned Ground / Imprimatura|applying thin transparent or semi-transparent color over white ground before painting; establishes middle value starting point|oil, acrylic; applied thinly so white ground shows through|eliminates intimidating white canvas; provides middle value to judge against; warm or cool imprimatura sets color key; transparent ground lets underlying white add luminosity
TK8|Sight-Size Method|positioning oneself so that subject and drawing/painting appear same size when viewed from standing position at specific distance|all|enables direct comparison of proportions, values, and edges between subject and artwork; most accurate method for representational accuracy; traditional atelier method; requires specific setup (subject beside easel, viewed from distance)
TK9|Comparative Measurement|using one body part or object as unit to measure proportional relationships; holding tool at arm's length, one eye closed, arm locked straight|all drawing|doesn't require sight-size setup; works at any scale; portable; essential for plein air and life drawing; use head-height as unit for figure; check angles by aligning pencil with observed angle
TK10|Massing|simplifying complex subjects into large simple shapes of unified value; seeing forests not trees; grouping elements|all|prevents detail addiction; creates strong compositional structure; essential first step; shadows massed as single shape; light areas massed; detail added within established mass structure; squinting reveals masses
TK11|Alla Prima Portrait|painting portrait in single session from life; typically 2-4 hours; demands efficiency and decisiveness|oil paint primarily|most challenging test of painting skill; integrates drawing, value, color, edge, likeness simultaneously; forces economy; fresh, lively result; demands preparation (color mixing, composition decisions before session)
TK12|Color Spotting|placing accurate spots of mixed color on canvas; each spot correct in hue, value, chroma, temperature, and shape; minimal blending; mosaic-like construction|oil, acrylic|builds image from discrete color notes; prevents muddy over-blending; each note placed with conviction; Joaquín Sorolla, Anders Zorn, John Singer Sargent; requires decisive premixing; freshest possible paint surface

# observation(id|name|definition|method|common_error)
OB1|Seeing Shape|perceiving the 2D silhouette of 3D forms; seeing contours, not objects; drawing the shape of the shadow, not the concept of shadow|squint to simplify values; look for abstract shapes in negative space; turn reference upside down (defeats object recognition); draw the shape of the space between objects|drawing symbols instead of shapes (generic eye shape instead of the specific shape of this eye from this angle in this light); naming what you see activates symbol system — describe shapes and values instead
OB2|Seeing Value|perceiving relative lightness and darkness independent of color; separating luminance from hue; value structure|squint hard (reduces color information, reveals value structure); use value scale (hold against subject); photograph subject in black and white; compare relationships (is this darker or lighter than that?)|confusing color with value (yellow and blue can be same value); making shadows too light (shadow is dark even if beautiful); not squinting (too much detail obscures value pattern); judging value in isolation instead of relationship
OB3|Seeing Color|perceiving actual color (hue, value, chroma, temperature) rather than assumed local color; seeing how light transforms color|ask four questions: what hue? what value? what chroma? warm or cool? compare to known reference; hold brush/chip against subject to compare; mix then compare; isolate color from context (view through hole in gray card)|painting from knowledge of local color rather than observation (painting grass green when it's actually warm gray-green in this light); ignoring color temperature shifts; not seeing reflected color in shadows; not seeing warm/cool variation within single surface
OB4|Seeing Edges|perceiving transitions between areas; hard edges vs soft vs lost; edge quality varies across entire image|squint to see which edges disappear (those are soft/lost); compare edge sharpness across image; note that edges are hardest at focal point and softest in periphery|making all edges equally hard (cutout look); ignoring lost edges (missing the magic of forms merging); hard edges everywhere from working too close (step back frequently)
OB5|Seeing Proportions|perceiving accurate size relationships between parts; comparative measurement; relationships not absolutes|measure with pencil at arm's length; compare part to part (how many eye-widths across is the nose?); check angles; use plumb line for verticals; check negative space shapes|drawing what you know instead of what you see (eyes too high on head; hands too small; torso too short); not measuring (trusting estimation over observation); working too close (can't see whole simultaneously with part)
OB6|Seeing Light|perceiving how light creates form; light side, shadow side, core shadow, reflected light, highlights, cast shadows; the language of light|identify light direction; map shadow shapes (shadow = single connected mass); note value of reflected light (never as light as direct light); observe edge of shadow (hard for direct light, soft for diffused)|reflected light too bright (should never approach value of light side); cast shadows too uniform (they have soft edges far from object, hard near contact); not observing how light direction unifies all objects in scene
OB7|Active vs Passive Seeing|passive: recognizing objects (that's a chair); active: analyzing visual properties (that shape is darker than adjacent shape, warmer, with a soft edge)|constantly compare (is this darker or lighter? warmer or cooler? harder or softer?); verbal analysis slows passive recognition and engages analytical seeing; draw what you see not what you know; frequent measurement|default mode is passive recognition; brain categorizes and stops seeing; antidote is directed questions; turn reference upside down; draw negative spaces; any technique that disrupts object recognition enhances observation

# failure_modes(id|topic|mode|cause|consequence|prevention)
FM1|drawing|symbol drawing|drawing conceptual knowledge of object rather than observed visual reality; brain substitutes symbol for perception|generic, flat, unconvincing images; all eyes look the same; proportions wrong; no individual character|observation exercises (blind contour, upside-down drawing, negative space drawing); draw what you see, not what you know; constant measurement; question every mark
FM2|drawing|proportion errors|insufficient measurement; working too close; starting with detail before establishing whole; drawing from knowledge rather than observation|figure looks wrong but artist can't identify why; head too small/large; limbs wrong length; features misplaced|measure constantly; step back frequently; establish whole before refining parts; check by viewing in mirror (reversal reveals errors); compare negative spaces

FM11|process|premature detail|rendering detail before establishing structure; drawing eyelashes before head proportions; painting texture before value structure|beautifully rendered details in wrong places; cannot be corrected without erasing finished work; proportion and value errors locked in under detail|work general to specific; large shapes first, refinement last; structure before surface; resist the seductive pull of detail until foundation is secure; ask: is the big picture working?

DI5|Direct Painting|Indirect Painting|direct (alla prima): one session, wet-into-wet, immediate; freshness; limited time; no layering; indirect: multiple sessions, underpainting → glazing → finishing; luminous depth; unlimited revision; slow; direct captures impression; indirect builds complexity; both valid; different results

DI7|Local Color|Observed Color|local color: inherent color of object (green leaf, red apple); what we know; observed color: actual color under specific lighting conditions; what we see; green leaf in sunset = warm orange; local color is starting point; observed color is reality; painting observed color = painting light; painting local color = painting things
DI8|Finished|Unfinished|finished: all areas resolved to consistent level; edges, values, colors complete; nothing jars; unfinished: varying levels of resolution; some areas fully rendered, others suggested or bare; strategic incompleteness is itself a powerful technique; Rembrandt's late work; suggestion engages viewer's imagination; "finished" is a decision not a state
DI9|Realism|Abstraction|realism: faithful representation of visual reality; verisimilitude; technical skill demonstrated through accuracy; abstraction: reducing, simplifying, or departing from visual reality; emphasizing formal elements (shape, color, line) over representation; all representation involves abstraction (choosing what to include); continuum not binary; abstract principles strengthen realism
DI10|Talent|Skill|talent: innate aptitude; starting advantages; eye-hand coordination; visual memory; sensitivity; varies by individual; skill: developed ability through deliberate practice; measurable improvement; teachable and learnable; skill can compensate for modest talent; talent without skill produces nothing; most of what we call "talent" is actually accumulated skill from years of practice
