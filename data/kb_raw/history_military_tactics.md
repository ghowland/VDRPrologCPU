# concepts(id|name|definition|category)
CO1|Tactics|employment of forces in battle or engagement; arrangement, maneuver, and application of combat power at point of contact|foundation
CO2|Strategy|planning and directing of large-scale military operations; campaign and war level; selects when, where, and why to fight|foundation
CO3|Operational Art|link between strategy and tactics; sequencing of battles and engagements within a campaign to achieve strategic objectives|foundation
CO4|Logistics|procurement, maintenance, transport of materiel, personnel, and facilities; determines what is tactically possible|foundation
CO5|Command and Control (C2)|exercise of authority over forces; communication of orders; integration of information for decision-making|foundation
CO6|Intelligence|collection, analysis, dissemination of information about enemy forces, terrain, weather|foundation
CO7|Terrain|physical environment of battle; governs movement, observation, fields of fire, cover, concealment, obstacles (OAKOC)|foundation
CO8|Concentration of Force|massing combat power at decisive point; achieving local superiority even if globally outnumbered|principle
CO9|Economy of Force|allocating minimum essential combat power to secondary efforts; accepting risk elsewhere to concentrate at decisive point|principle
CO10|Surprise|striking enemy at time, place, or manner for which they are unprepared; multiplies combat power|principle
CO11|Maneuver|movement to gain positional advantage relative to enemy; creates dilemma; threatens enemy from unexpected direction|principle
CO12|Unity of Command|single commander with authority to direct all forces toward common objective; prevents conflicting orders|principle
CO13|Security|never permitting enemy to acquire unexpected advantage; protect force, deny information, maintain freedom of action|principle
CO14|Offensive|seize, retain, exploit initiative; dictate tempo; determine course of action|principle
CO15|Simplicity|clear, uncomplicated plans and orders; reduces friction and misunderstanding in fog of war|principle
CO16|Mass|synchronize effects of combat power at decisive place and time; not mere numbers but concentration of effect|principle
CO17|Friction (Clausewitz)|accumulation of difficulties, uncertainties, and chance in war; makes the simple difficult; fog of war|theory
CO18|Center of Gravity|source of power providing moral or physical strength, freedom of action, or will to fight; Clausewitz concept|theory
CO19|Decisive Point|geographic place, key event, or critical factor where applying force achieves disproportionate advantage|theory
CO20|OODA Loop (Boyd)|Observe-Orient-Decide-Act cycle; faster cycle tempo = initiative; disrupt enemy's OODA → paralysis|theory
CO21|Tempo|rate and rhythm of military operations relative to enemy; control tempo = dictate engagement|theory
CO22|Culminating Point|moment when attacker's combat power no longer exceeds defender's; extending past this invites defeat|theory
CO23|Lines of Communication (LOC)|routes connecting force to its supply base; protection of own LOC and interdiction of enemy's LOC|logistics
CO24|Interior Lines|operating from central position; able to reinforce faster than enemy operating on exterior; shorter distances|principle
CO25|Exterior Lines|enveloping or surrounding enemy from multiple directions; risk of defeat in detail if not coordinated|principle
CO26|Force Multiplier|factor (technology, terrain, training, morale, surprise) increasing combat effectiveness beyond raw numbers|concept
CO27|Attrition|wearing down enemy through continuous losses; exchange ratios determine outcome; material-intensive|approach
CO28|Annihilation|destroying enemy force entirely in decisive battle; seeking Cannae-type encirclement; single battle decides campaign|approach
CO29|Exhaustion|protracted operations depleting enemy's will and resources without seeking decisive engagement; Fabian strategy|approach
CO30|Asymmetric Warfare|conflict between forces of significantly different military capabilities; weaker side uses unconventional methods|approach
CO31|Combined Arms|integration of different combat arms (infantry, armor, artillery, air) to create synergistic effect where each compensates for others' vulnerabilities|principle
CO32|Fire and Movement|one element suppresses enemy (fire) while another advances to new position (movement); alternating roles|principle
CO33|Envelopment|attacking one or both flanks while fixing enemy frontally; single or double envelopment|maneuver
CO34|Turning Movement|maneuver around enemy position to threaten rear or LOC; forces enemy to abandon position or fight at disadvantage|maneuver
CO35|Frontal Attack|direct assault against enemy front; highest casualties; used when overwhelming superiority or no alternative|maneuver
CO36|Penetration|concentrating force to break through enemy line at narrow point; then exploiting breach to attack from rear|maneuver
CO37|Infiltration|passing small units through gaps in enemy lines to attack from rear; avoids main defenses|maneuver
CO38|Defense in Depth|successive defensive positions trading space for time; attritional; dissipates attacker's momentum through multiple lines|maneuver
CO39|Mobile Defense|defensive maneuver trading space; retain small force forward; counterattack with mobile reserve to destroy attacker at point of maximum vulnerability|maneuver
CO40|Ambush|surprise attack from concealed position on moving or halted enemy; devastating when combined with terrain and restricted routes|maneuver
CO41|Siege|encirclement and isolation of fortified position; starvation, assault, or negotiation to compel surrender|maneuver
CO42|Pursuit|following and destroying retreating enemy before they can reconstitute; most destructive phase of battle if executed|maneuver
CO43|Withdrawal|disengagement from enemy; most difficult tactical operation; requires covering force, deception, and timing|maneuver
CO44|Delay|trading space for time while inflicting maximum damage; successive positions; not intended to hold permanently|maneuver
CO45|Raid|swift penetration of enemy area for specific objective (destroy, capture, gather intelligence); planned withdrawal after|maneuver
CO46|Feint|attack or demonstration designed to divert enemy attention and reserves from main effort|deception
CO47|Deception (Military)|deliberately misleading enemy about own intentions, capabilities, or disposition of forces|deception
CO48|Interdiction|disrupting enemy movement, supply, and communication before they reach the battle area|operational
CO49|Encirclement|surrounding enemy force to prevent escape or reinforcement; objective is annihilation or surrender|maneuver
CO50|Exploitation|advancing rapidly after successful penetration or breakthrough to prevent enemy from reconstituting defense|maneuver

# formations(id|name|era|description|strengths|weaknesses)
FM1|Phalanx (Sumerian)|c. 2500 BC|close-order infantry block with overlapping shields and spears; earliest known formation|mass shock; mutual protection; simple command|rigid; vulnerable on flanks and rear; depends on cohesion; poor on broken terrain
FM2|Phalanx (Greek Hoplite)|c. 700–338 BC|8-deep ranks of heavy infantry with aspis (shield) and doru (spear); push of shields (othismos)|frontal shock; mutual protection via overlapping shields; citizen-soldier morale|flanks exposed; pursuit difficult; restricted to flat terrain; inflexible
FM3|Phalanx (Macedonian Sarissa)|c. 359–168 BC|16-deep pike formation; 5-6m sarissa; lighter shield (pelta); created by Philip II|5 ranks of pike points project beyond front rank; devastating frontal engagement; pins enemy in place|flanks and rear extremely vulnerable; requires combined arms support (cavalry, hypaspists); terrain-dependent
FM4|Roman Manipular Legion|c. 340–107 BC|three lines (hastati, principes, triarii); maniples of 120 men in checkerboard (quincunx) pattern|tactical flexibility; fresh lines in reserve; gaps allow passage of lines; adaptable to terrain|requires extensive training; complex command; vulnerable to cavalry if disordered
FM5|Roman Cohort Legion (Marian)|107 BC – 3rd century AD|10 cohorts of 480 men; standardized equipment; professional soldiers; eliminated class distinctions|uniform training; interchangeable cohorts; integrated with auxiliary cavalry, archers, engineers|expensive to maintain; dependent on logistics and frontier infrastructure; vulnerable during political instability
FM6|Testudo (Tortoise)|Roman|interlocking shields overhead and on all sides; forms mobile shell|protection against missiles; enables approach to walls and fortifications|extremely slow movement; no offensive capability while formed; limited visibility
FM7|Shield Wall|c. 5th–11th century AD|overlapping shields forming continuous barrier; used by Anglo-Saxons, Vikings, Franks|strong frontal defense; mutual protection; simple to form|static; flanks vulnerable; limited offensive power; breaks down in prolonged melee
FM8|Wedge (Svinfylking)|Germanic/Viking|triangular formation with strongest warriors at point; designed to penetrate shield walls|concentrates force at apex; psychological impact; breaks enemy line|exposed flanks of wedge; if apex fails, formation collapses; requires elite warriors at point
FM9|Cavalry Wedge/Rhombus|Hellenistic/medieval|mounted formation for shock charge; wedge (Macedonian) or rhombus (Thessalian)|concentrated impact at point of contact; easy to change direction (rhombus)|requires space; vulnerable to pikes and prepared infantry; horses refuse obstacles
FM10|Heavy Cavalry Charge (Knightly)|c. 11th–15th century|armored knights with couched lance in stirrups; massed charge at full gallop|devastating shock against unprepared infantry; psychological impact; prestige|defeated by disciplined pike/spear formations, stakes, terrain obstacles; expensive; limited endurance
FM11|Schiltron|c. 13th–14th century|Scottish circular or rectangular formation of spearmen; hedgehog defense against cavalry|effective anti-cavalry defense; all-round protection|static; vulnerable to archery and combined arms; cannot advance effectively
FM12|Pike Square (Swiss)|c. 14th–16th century|dense block of pikemen (5m pikes) in square; aggressive offensive use; halberdiers at corners|offensive shock; defeated mounted knights; maintained cohesion on advance; self-sufficient|vulnerable to firearms; flanks and corners exposed to fire; required extensive drilling and cohesion
FM13|Tercio (Spanish)|c. 1534–1640s|combined pike and shot formation; pikemen in center, arquebus/musket sleeves on flanks; ~3000 men|combined arms at formation level; pike protects shot, shot provides firepower; dominated European battlefields for century|large, slow, inflexible; vulnerable to more maneuverable linear formations; costly to maintain
FM14|Line Infantry|c. 1690–1860s|thin linear formation (2-3 ranks deep); maximizes musket firepower along front|maximum simultaneous fire from all ranks; simple volley fire drill|thin, fragile; flanks exposed; requires flat terrain and tight discipline; vulnerable to cavalry on flanks
FM15|Column|c. 1790–1860s|deep formation for movement and shock; French Revolutionary/Napoleonic armies|rapid movement; shock at point of contact; easier command; lower training requirements|narrow frontage limits firepower; vulnerable to artillery and volley fire; higher casualties from fire
FM16|Square|c. 18th–19th century|infantry formed in hollow square facing all directions; bayonets outward|highly effective against cavalry from any direction; all-round defense|static; dense target for artillery; cannot advance while formed; vulnerable to combined infantry-artillery attack
FM17|Skirmish Line|c. 18th century onward|loose formation of individual soldiers using cover; fires independently; screens main body|reduces casualties from artillery/volley; disrupts enemy formations; gathers intelligence|low concentration of fire; poor shock action; requires individual initiative and marksmanship
FM18|Trench System|c. 1914–1918|continuous fortified lines with fire, communication, and reserve trenches; barbed wire, machine gun positions|near-invulnerable to frontal infantry assault; excellent protection from fire|static; created stalemate; vulnerable to new technologies (tank, gas, infiltration tactics); enormous logistical burden
FM19|Panzer Kampfgruppe|c. 1939–1945|combined arms battle group centered on tanks with integrated mechanized infantry, engineers, artillery, and air support|speed, shock, firepower; flexible task organization; initiative at all levels|dependent on fuel and logistics; vulnerable in close terrain; requires excellent communications and trained leaders
FM20|Dispersed Formation (Modern)|c. 1917 onward|soldiers spread across terrain using cover; fire teams, squads as basic tactical unit; avoid massing under fire|survivable against modern firepower; uses terrain; enables fire and movement|requires high individual training; command and control more difficult; reduced local shock power

# tactics(id|name|era_introduced|description|effect|notable_employment)
TA1|Hammer and Anvil|c. 4th century BC (Macedonian)|infantry pins enemy frontally (anvil) while cavalry strikes flanks/rear (hammer)|enemy trapped between two forces; cannot retreat; panic and destruction|Alexander at Gaugamela (331 BC): phalanx held center, Companion cavalry struck left flank
TA2|Oblique Order|c. 371 BC|refuse one wing (echelon back), concentrate strength on the other; attack with strong wing first|local superiority on attack wing; enemy defeated in detail from one side before refused wing engages|Epaminondas at Leuctra (371 BC): 50-deep left wing crushed Spartan right; Frederick II at Leuthen (1757)
TA3|Double Envelopment|c. 216 BC|attack both flanks simultaneously while center holds or gives ground, then close behind enemy|complete encirclement; enemy annihilated or forced to surrender; gold standard of tactical victory|Hannibal at Cannae (216 BC): center retreated deliberately, cavalry closed rear; destroyed 8 Roman legions
TA4|Fabian Strategy|c. 217 BC|avoid pitched battle; harass supply lines; deny enemy decisive engagement; protract war|attacker's strength dissipates without battle; time favors defender with interior lines|Fabius Maximus vs Hannibal; George Washington 1776-1777; Kutuzov vs Napoleon 1812
TA5|Defeat in Detail|ancient onward|engage and destroy isolated portions of a divided enemy force before they can concentrate|locally outnumber an overall larger enemy; win multiple small battles instead of one large one|Napoleon at Austerlitz (1805): separated Allies from reinforcements; Frederick at Rossbach-Leuthen (1757)
TA6|Refused Flank|ancient onward|deliberately hold one flank back or anchor it on impassable terrain; concentrate strength elsewhere|protects weak flank; enables concentration on attack sector|Wellington at many battles: anchored flanks on terrain; Epaminondas (weak right refused)
TA7|Feigned Retreat|ancient/medieval|simulated flight to draw enemy into pursuit; disorder their formation; then counter-attack|breaks enemy cohesion; leads pursuit into ambush or prepared position|Mongols (standard tactic); William at Hastings (1066); Turks at Manzikert (1071)
TA8|Caracole|c. 16th century|cavalry rides to close range, fires pistols, wheels away; repeated waves|harassing fire without committing to melee; used by German Reiters|replaced by return to shock cavalry (Gustavus Adolphus re-introduced charge with sword)
TA9|Volley Fire|c. 16th–19th century|simultaneous discharge of firearms by rank or platoon on command|concentrated fire effect; maximizes lethality per discharge; psychological shock|Maurice of Nassau introduced platoon fire by rotation; enabled continuous fire
TA10|Creeping Barrage|1916|artillery fire advancing in timed lifts just ahead of attacking infantry; suppresses defenders until last moment|infantry arrives before defenders can recover from shellfire; closes gap between fire and movement|first effective use at Battle of the Somme (later stages); refined through 1917-1918
TA11|Infiltration Tactics (Stormtrooper)|1917–1918|small, heavily armed groups bypass strong points; penetrate deep into enemy rear; follow-up forces reduce bypassed positions|avoids frontal attrition on strong points; disrupts command and supply; creates confusion in depth|Hutier tactics; German Spring Offensive 1918 (Operation Michael); influenced all subsequent infantry doctrine
TA12|Blitzkrieg (Deep Battle Maneuver)|1939–1941|concentrated armor and mechanized forces with close air support penetrate at narrow point; exploit deep into enemy rear; encircle enemy forces|paralysis of enemy command; destruction of enemy coherence rather than forces; speed prevents organized response|Fall Gelb (France 1940): Guderian's panzer corps through Ardennes; Barbarossa opening (1941)
TA13|Combined Arms Maneuver (Modern)|20th century onward|synchronized employment of infantry, armor, artillery, engineers, aviation, electronic warfare as integrated system|each arm compensates for others' vulnerabilities; creates multiple simultaneous dilemmas for enemy|standard doctrine for all modern conventional armies; AirLand Battle (1982 US FM 100-5)
TA14|Vertical Envelopment|1941 onward (airborne)|insertion of forces by parachute or helicopter behind enemy lines; attack from unexpected direction|bypasses prepared defenses; threatens rear; creates encirclement opportunities|Crete (1941); Normandy (1944); Ia Drang (1965); helicopter-enabled in Vietnam
TA15|Air-Ground Integration (CAS)|WWI onward, mature WWII|aircraft directly support ground forces with fires, reconnaissance, interdiction|extends ground force reach; multiplies firepower; enables rapid response to developing situations|Stuka-panzer coordination (Blitzkrieg); Cab Rank system (Normandy); A-10 + ground forces (Gulf War)
TA16|Fire and Maneuver (Squad Level)|WWI onward|fire team suppresses enemy while maneuver team advances; alternating bounds; IMT (individual movement techniques)|enables advance against prepared positions under fire; fundamental building block of modern infantry tactics|universal modern infantry doctrine; evolved from WWI stormtrooper experience
TA17|Ambush (L-shaped)|ancient, formalized modern|assault element on one axis, support element perpendicular; kills zone in L intersection|interlocking fires; target cannot advance or retreat without entering kill zone; difficult to counter|standard small unit tactic in all modern armies; extensively used in guerrilla warfare
TA18|Defense in Depth (Modern)|WWI onward|outpost zone → main battle area → reserve; elastic defense; counterattack against overextended attacker|absorbs momentum; attacker weakens through successive positions; reserve strikes at culminating point|German elastic defense 1917-1918; Soviet defense at Kursk (1943); multi-belt defense
TA19|Encirclement and Reduction|ancient onward|surround enemy force; cut supply; reduce pocket by compression; force surrender or destruction|eliminates enemy force entirely; captures personnel and materiel; prevents escape|Stalingrad (1942-43): Soviet Operation Uranus encircled 6th Army; Falaise Pocket (1944)
TA20|Guerrilla Tactics|ancient onward, codified 20th century|irregular forces using hit-and-run, ambush, sabotage; avoid pitched battle; control population; protract conflict|denies enemy decisive battle; increases cost; exploits political dimension of conflict|Mao's three phases; Vietnamese strategy; Spanish guerrillas vs Napoleon; Afghan mujahideen
TA21|Scorched Earth|ancient onward|destroying resources useful to advancing enemy: crops, infrastructure, shelter|denies sustenance and shelter to attacker; forces reliance on long supply lines|Russian retreat 1812; Sherman's March 1864; Soviet retreat 1941
TA22|Naval Crossing the T|age of sail/early 20th century|maneuvering fleet perpendicular to enemy column; crossing ship fires full broadside while enemy can only use bow guns|concentrated fire against weak axis; devastating firepower advantage|Tsushima (1905): Togo crossed Rozhestvensky's T; Jutland attempted
TA23|Convoy System|WWI, WWII|merchant ships grouped with naval escorts; concentrated defense against submarines|reduced losses from submarine warfare by ~80%; forced submarines to attack defended targets|adopted 1917 (reduced shipping losses from 25% to 1%); WWII Battle of Atlantic
TA24|Carpet Bombing (Area Bombardment)|WWII|saturation bombing of area target; destroy military-industrial capacity and morale|devastates infrastructure and production; psychological effect; area denial|Combined Bomber Offensive (1943-45); B-52 Arc Light (Vietnam); controversial due to civilian casualties
TA25|Air Superiority Campaign|WWII onward|systematic destruction of enemy air force (fighters, airfields, industry) to gain control of airspace|enables all other air and ground operations; denies enemy air support and reconnaissance|Luftwaffe Battle of Britain (failed); USAAF Big Week 1944 (succeeded); Coalition 1991 Gulf War (72 hours)
TA26|Shock and Awe (Rapid Dominance)|1991–2003|overwhelming firepower, speed, and information dominance to paralyze enemy decision-making and will|enemy unable to respond coherently; rapid collapse of organized resistance|Gulf War 1991 (100-hour ground campaign); Iraq 2003 (21-day Baghdad fall)
TA27|Network-Centric Warfare|1990s onward|shared situational awareness via networked sensors, communications, and weapons; distribute information to all echelons|faster and better decisions; self-synchronization; distributed but coordinated operations|US military transformation post-1991; enabled by GPS, digital communications, drone ISR
TA28|Counterinsurgency (COIN)|formalized 20th century|population-centric operations: secure populace, build governance, isolate insurgents; clear-hold-build|addresses root causes; separates insurgent from population; long-duration; resource-intensive|Malaya (Briggs Plan); Galula doctrine; FM 3-24 (Petraeus/Nagl 2006); Afghanistan
TA29|Siege Warfare (Vauban)|17th century|systematic approach: parallel trenches, zigzag saps, artillery batteries reducing walls; scientific siege|predictable timeline to capture; reduced casualties for attacker compared to assault; methodical|Vauban designed 33 sieges, captured 53 fortresses for Louis XIV; standardized until explosive shells made obsolete

# weapons_systems(id|name|era|type|tactical_impact|notes)

WS2|Composite Bow|c. 2500 BC onward|ranged|increased range (250-400m) and power over self bow; enabled horse archery; Central Asian steppe weapon|horn-wood-sinew laminate; recurved design; Assyrian, Scythian, Mongol, Ottoman
WS3|Chariot|c. 2000–500 BC|mounted platform|mobile missile platform; shock weapon; command platform; status symbol; dominated Bronze Age warfare|Hyksos, Egyptian, Hittite, Chinese; gave way to cavalry as horse breeds improved
WS4|Sarissa (Macedonian Pike)|c. 359 BC|melee|5-6m pike; 5 ranks projecting points; created impenetrable front; required two hands (lighter shield)|Philip II and Alexander; Successor kingdoms; countered by Roman flexibility
WS5|Gladius (Roman Short Sword)|c. 3rd century BC|melee|short thrusting sword optimized for tight formation; used behind scutum (large shield); devastating in press|standard Roman infantry weapon; combined with pilum (javelin) for ranged-then-melee sequence
WS6|Pilum (Roman Javelin)|c. 4th century BC|ranged|heavy javelin; bent on impact to prevent return throw; weighed down enemy shields; disrupted formation before melee|thrown at ~15-30m before gladius engagement; soft iron shank design intentional

WS10|Trebuchet (Counterweight)|c. 12th century|siege|hurled 100-150kg projectiles 300m; could breach stone walls; psychological weapon|standard siege weapon of High Middle Ages; Acre (1191); Mongol sieges

WS14|Field Artillery (Smoothbore)|c. 16th century onward|ranged|canister (anti-personnel), roundshot (anti-formation), shell; mobile enough for battlefield|Gustavus Adolphus lightened guns; Napoleon massed artillery as "grand battery"; dominant arm by 1800
WS15|Rifled Musket (Minié Ball)|c. 1849–1870s|ranged|effective to 500m (vs 100m smoothbore); spinning projectile; loaded as fast as smoothbore; devastated Napoleonic formations|American Civil War: made frontal assault suicidal; forced field fortification; transition weapon

WS17|Machine Gun|c. 1884 onward (Maxim)|ranged|600+ rounds/min sustained; beaten zone; creates kill zones; one gun = firepower of 40+ riflemen|transformed defensive warfare; WWI trench stalemate primary cause; required new tactics to overcome
WS18|Quick-Firing Field Artillery|c. 1897 onward (French 75)|ranged|hydraulic recoil; no re-aiming between shots; 15+ rounds/min; shrapnel and HE shells|French 75mm (soixante-quinze); indirect fire doctrine; observation and fire correction
WS19|Barbed Wire|c. 1914 onward (military use)|obstacle|channelized movement; delayed attackers in kill zones of machine guns and artillery|simple, cheap, devastating combined with machine gun; defined WWI no-man's-land
WS20|Poison Gas|1915–1918|area weapon|chlorine, phosgene, mustard; area denial; forced mask wear reducing effectiveness; psychological terror|first large-scale use: Ypres 1915 (chlorine); mustard gas (1917) most effective; banned by Geneva Protocol 1925
WS21|Tank|1916 onward|mounted/armored|crosses trenches and wire; immune to small arms; combines firepower, protection, mobility|Cambrai (1917) first massed use; Blitzkrieg enabled by reliable tanks (Panzer III/IV); arms race with anti-tank weapons
WS22|Military Aircraft|1914 onward|air|reconnaissance → fighter → bomber → close air support → airlift; extends battle into third dimension|transformed warfare; air superiority prerequisite for ground operations by WWII
WS23|Submarine|1914 onward (effective)|naval|attacks shipping below surface; area denial; strategic blockade; nuclear missile platform|U-boat campaigns nearly defeated Britain (WWI, WWII); nuclear submarines = second-strike deterrent
WS24|Aircraft Carrier|1940s onward (dominant)|naval|mobile airfield projecting air power globally; replaced battleship as capital ship|Taranto (1940), Pearl Harbor (1941), Midway (1942) proved carrier dominance; power projection platform
WS25|Nuclear Weapon|1945 onward|strategic|city-destroying weapon; deterrence through mutually assured destruction (MAD); altered nature of great-power conflict|Hiroshima and Nagasaki; ended WWII Pacific; made great-power conventional war potentially suicidal
WS26|Guided Missile/PGM|1943 onward (Fritz-X), mature 1970s|ranged (precision)|precision strike on specific targets; reduced collateral damage; one weapon = one target (vs hundreds of bombs)|laser-guided (Vietnam), GPS-guided (JDAM); 1991 Gulf War demonstrated revolution in precision
WS27|Improvised Explosive Device (IED)|formalized 21st century|asymmetric|cheap; concealed; targets patrols and convoys; primary casualty producer in Iraq/Afghanistan; countered by MRAP vehicles and EW|leading cause of coalition casualties Iraq/Afghanistan; drove billions in countermeasure investment

WS29|Helicopter (Attack/Transport)|1950s onward|air (rotary)|vertical insertion; close air support; casualty evacuation; air assault; transformed infantry mobility|Vietnam (airmobile concept); AH-64 Apache (anti-armor); fundamental to modern combined arms

# doctrines(id|name|era|author_or_origin|core_tenets|influence)
DC1|Sun Tzu's Principles|c. 5th century BC|Sun Tzu (The Art of War)|know enemy and self; attack weakness, avoid strength; deception fundamental; win without fighting if possible; terrain and weather critical|foundational text for all subsequent Eastern and Western military thought; influenced Mao, Boyd, modern maneuver warfare
DC2|Vegetius' Principles|c. 390 AD|Publius Flavius Vegetius Renatus (De Re Militari)|who desires peace, prepares for war; training and discipline paramount; fortification; selection of soldiers; logistics|most read military treatise in medieval West; shaped European military organization for 1000 years
DC3|Clausewitz (On War)|1832|Carl von Clausewitz|war = continuation of politics; fog and friction; center of gravity; trinity (government, army, people); culminating point; absolute vs real war|dominated Western strategic thought; basis for operational art; influenced all modern doctrine
DC4|Jomini's Principles|1838|Antoine-Henri Jomini|principles of war are timeless and geometric; interior lines; concentration at decisive point; operations on a single line|influenced US Civil War commanders; basis for principles of war lists; competed with Clausewitz for doctrinal influence
DC5|Napoleon's Methods|c. 1796–1815|Napoleon Bonaparte|corps system (independent combined arms); central position; manoeuvre sur les derrières (rear maneuver); decisive battle; live off the land|revolutionized campaign planning; corps system adopted universally; exceeded logistics limits in Russia
DC6|Mahan (Sea Power)|1890|Alfred Thayer Mahan|command of the sea through decisive fleet battle; concentration of naval force; chokepoints; overseas bases; commerce protection|shaped US, British, Japanese, German naval policy; influenced dreadnought race; still relevant for maritime strategy
DC7|Corbett (Maritime Strategy)|1911|Julian Corbett|sea control vs sea denial; limited war; joint operations (army-navy); fleet-in-being; commerce warfare|complemented Mahan; influenced British maritime strategy; recognized limits of naval power alone
DC8|Douhet (Air Power)|1921|Giulio Douhet|strategic bombing of enemy cities destroys will to fight; air superiority first; bomber always gets through|influenced interwar air doctrine (RAF, USAAC); partially validated (strategic bombing campaigns WWII); strategic deterrence
DC9|Fuller/Liddell Hart (Mechanized Warfare)|1920s–1930s|J.F.C. Fuller, B.H. Liddell Hart|tank as dominant arm; indirect approach; strike at enemy nerve centers not body; expanding torrent; avoid frontal battle|influenced Guderian and German Blitzkrieg; Liddell Hart's indirect approach widely cited; armored doctrine foundations
DC10|Tukhachevsky (Deep Battle/Deep Operations)|1929–1936|Mikhail Tukhachevsky|simultaneous attack across full depth of enemy defense; multiple echelons; combined arms at operational depth; aviation and airborne in depth|Soviet operational art; Bagration (1944); influenced AirLand Battle; suppressed after Tukhachevsky's execution (1937), revived in WWII

# distinctions(id|side_a|side_b|key_asymmetry)
DI1|Offense|Defense|offense: seizes initiative, chooses time/place; requires ~3:1 local superiority (conventional wisdom); defense: inherent advantage of prepared positions, fortification, interior lines; historically defense dominant when technology favors firepower
DI2|Attrition|Maneuver|attrition: destroy enemy forces through cumulative losses; material-intensive; Grant, Verdun; maneuver: defeat enemy by disrupting cohesion, threatening flanks/rear; position-focused; Napoleon, Blitzkrieg; pure forms rare, most warfare combines both
DI3|Direct Approach|Indirect Approach|direct: frontal engagement of enemy main force; simpler; risks highest casualties; indirect: attack weakness, bypass strength; envelop; Liddell Hart: "the history of strategy is the history of the indirect approach"
DI4|Conventional Warfare|Irregular/Guerrilla Warfare|conventional: organized forces, open battle, territorial objectives; irregular: dispersed, concealed, population-based, avoiding decisive battle; asymmetric; political objectives
DI5|Firepower|Mobility|firepower: destroy enemy by volume or precision of fires; favors defense; mobility: move faster than enemy can react; bypass, envelop; favors offense; balance between the two defines tactical era
DI6|Concentration|Dispersion|concentration: mass force at decisive point; achieves local superiority; concentration = target for modern weapons; dispersion: spread forces to reduce vulnerability; survivable but reduces local power; modern warfare forces dispersion under precision fires
DI7|Initiative|Reaction|initiative: dictating tempo, choosing engagement terms; acting; reaction: responding to enemy action; defending; loss of initiative = fighting on enemy's terms; OODA loop speed determines who holds initiative
DI8|Shock|Fire|shock: physical and psychological impact of close combat or charge; cavalry, bayonet, tank; fire: killing and suppression at range; arrows, muskets, artillery, missiles; historical oscillation between shock and fire dominance
DI9|Symmetrical|Asymmetrical|symmetrical: similar forces, similar methods, similar objectives; asymmetrical: dissimilar forces/methods; weaker side avoids strength, attacks vulnerabilities; IED vs armored convoy
DI10|Tactical Success|Strategic Success|tactical victory may be strategically irrelevant (Pyrrhic victory); strategic success may not require tactical victory (Fabian strategy); alignment of tactical and strategic objectives = decisive
DI11|Land Power|Sea Power|land: controls territory and population; decisive in continental conflicts; sea: controls commerce and communication; enables global reach; Mahan vs Mackinder debate; modern: joint operations require both
DI12|Mass Army|Professional Army|mass: numerical superiority; cheaper per soldier; lower training; conscription; effective in industrial total war; professional: higher individual capability; expensive; better suited for complex modern operations; most effective when technology amplifies skill
