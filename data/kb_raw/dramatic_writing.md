# concepts(id|name|definition|category)
CO1|Story|unified narrative sequence with beginning, middle, and end; character(s) pursuing desire against resistance producing change|foundation
CO2|Plot|causal sequence of events; not merely what happens (story) but why and how events connect; "The king died and then the queen died" is story; "The king died and then the queen died of grief" is plot (Forster)|foundation
CO3|Character|fictional person (or entity) with desires, flaws, agency, and capacity for change; reader's primary point of emotional investment|foundation
CO4|Conflict|opposition between desire and obstacle; engine of narrative; without conflict, no story; external (character vs world) or internal (character vs self)|foundation
CO5|Theme|underlying meaning or question the story explores; not stated directly but enacted through character and plot; what the story is really about beneath surface events|foundation
CO6|Setting|when and where the story takes place; physical environment, historical period, social context, cultural milieu; constrains and enables character action|foundation
CO7|Tone|emotional attitude of the narrative voice toward the material; comic, tragic, ironic, earnest, detached, intimate; consistent tone creates coherent reading experience|foundation
CO8|Voice|distinctive quality of the narrator's or author's prose; rhythm, diction, syntax, sensibility; what makes one writer sound different from another|foundation
CO9|Scene|fundamental unit of dramatic narrative; continuous action in one time and place; contains goal, conflict, and outcome (yes-but or no-and); builds or releases tension|foundation
CO10|Summary (Narrative)|compressed narration covering time, events, or background without dramatizing moment by moment; tells rather than shows; useful for transitions, pacing, backstory|foundation
CO11|Tension|reader's sense that something is at stake; uncertainty about outcome; desire to keep reading; created by conflict, dramatic irony, withholding, ticking clock|foundation
CO12|Stakes|what character stands to gain or lose; consequence of failure; personal (emotional), public (social), cosmic (existential); higher stakes = more tension|foundation
CO13|Catharsis|emotional release experienced by audience through witnessing suffering and resolution; Aristotle: pity and fear purged through tragedy; applies to all powerful narrative|foundation
CO14|Suspension of Disbelief|reader's willingness to accept story's premises as true within its world; earned by internal consistency; broken by logical contradiction, tonal inconsistency, authorial convenience|foundation
CO15|Verisimilitude|quality of seeming true or real; not necessarily realistic (fantasy has verisimilitude); internal consistency and sensory specificity create it|foundation
CO16|Subtext|meaning beneath the literal surface; what characters think but don't say; what scenes imply but don't state; gap between surface and depth is where emotional power lives|foundation
CO17|Exposition|information the reader needs to understand the story; backstory, world rules, character history; art of exposition is hiding it inside drama, action, and dialogue|foundation
CO18|Dramatic Irony|audience knows something characters don't; creates anticipation, dread, or comedy; Oedipus doesn't know he killed his father but audience does|foundation
CO19|Motif|recurring image, phrase, situation, or object carrying thematic weight; accumulates meaning through repetition; water, mirrors, doors opening/closing|foundation
CO20|Symbol|concrete object or image representing abstract concept; green light in Gatsby (aspiration); scarlet letter (sin/identity); emerges from story, not imposed on it|foundation
CO21|Foreshadowing|planting elements early that pay off later; creates retrospective inevitability; Chekhov's gun: if shown in act one, must fire by act three; earns plot turns|foundation
CO22|Resonance|quality of story continuing to vibrate in reader's mind after finishing; produced by thematic depth, ambiguity, emotional truth, and structural elegance|foundation

# story_structure(id|name|description|stages|strengths|limitations)
SS1|Three-Act Structure|foundational dramatic structure; Setup → Confrontation → Resolution; each act roughly 25%/50%/25%|Act I: establish world, character, desire, inciting incident; Act II: escalating conflict, midpoint shift, complications; Act III: climax, resolution|universal; intuitive; flexible; underlies most narrative forms|can feel formulaic if mechanically applied; Act II sag common; not prescriptive about internal structure
SS2|Five-Act Structure (Freytag)|Gustav Freytag's pyramid; classical drama and Shakespeare|Exposition → Rising Action → Climax → Falling Action → Dénouement|models tragic structure well; falling action after climax distinguishes from three-act; honors aftermath|less suited to modern pacing; falling action often compressed in contemporary work; originally descriptive not prescriptive
SS3|Hero's Journey (Campbell/Vogler)|monomyth; archetypal narrative pattern across cultures; 12-17 stages|Ordinary World → Call to Adventure → Refusal → Mentor → Crossing Threshold → Tests/Allies/Enemies → Inmost Cave → Ordeal → Reward → Road Back → Resurrection → Return with Elixir|powerful mythic resonance; universal appeal; clear emotional arc; naturally creates transformation|can produce generic stories if followed rigidly; not all stories are heroic; cultural specificity of "hero"; over-applied
SS4|Kishōtenketsu|East Asian four-act structure without central conflict; Setup → Development → Twist → Reconciliation|Ki: introduction; Shō: development; Ten: surprising turn (not conflict-driven); Ketsu: reconciliation of elements|demonstrates narrative without antagonism; subtlety; contemplative pacing; different emotional logic|unfamiliar to Western audiences; can feel plotless if twist is too subtle; hard to sustain over long form
SS5|In Medias Res|beginning in the middle of action; backstory filled in later; Homer's Iliad opens in final year of war|Immediate action → Backward-filling context → Forward momentum|grabs attention; avoids slow setup; creates mystery about past; energetic opening|risks confusion if too disorienting; backstory delivery becomes challenge; flashback-heavy if poorly managed
SS6|Frame Narrative|story within a story; outer narrative frames inner narrative; provides context, unreliable perspective, or thematic commentary|Outer frame established → Inner story told → Return to frame (often transformed)|creates distance enabling taboo or extreme content; thematic commentary through juxtaposition; enables unreliable narration|can feel distancing; frame may be more interesting than inner story or vice versa; structural complexity
SS7|Nonlinear / Fragmented|events presented out of chronological order; reader assembles timeline; juxtaposition creates meaning|Fragments → Pattern recognition → Emergent whole|mimics memory and consciousness; thematic juxtaposition; reveals character through selection not sequence; intellectual engagement|risks confusion; demands active reader; can feel gimmicky if not serving meaning; hard to create momentum
SS8|Seven-Point Structure (Wells)|simplified beat structure; Hook → Plot Turn 1 → Pinch 1 → Midpoint → Pinch 2 → Plot Turn 2 → Resolution|Hook (opposite of resolution state) → PT1 (conflict engaged) → Pinch 1 (pressure) → Midpoint (character takes charge) → Pinch 2 (maximum pressure) → PT2 (acquire final piece) → Resolution|clear beats; easy to plan; works well for genre fiction; pinch points ensure tension maintained|mechanical if over-adhered; emphasis on plot over character; limited guidance on scene-level craft
SS9|Save the Cat (Snyder)|15-beat screenwriting structure adapted for novels; highly prescriptive timing|Opening Image → Theme Stated → Setup → Catalyst → Debate → Break into Two → B Story → Fun and Games → Midpoint → Bad Guys Close In → All Is Lost → Dark Night of Soul → Break into Three → Finale → Final Image|very specific guidance; demystifies structure; popular for commercial fiction and screenwriting|extremely prescriptive; can produce formulaic work; page-count timing advice creates rigidity; better as diagnostic than generative tool
SS10|Episodic Structure|series of loosely connected episodes united by character, theme, or setting rather than single causal chain|Episode 1 → Episode 2 → ... → (optional) culminating episode; picaresque; road trip; collection of linked stories|freedom; variety; exploration of world and character facets; each episode can stand alone; suitable for serial publication|lacks momentum of causal plot; can feel repetitive; hard to build to satisfying climax; reader may disengage without overarching question
SS11|Circular / Ouroboros|story ends where it began; final scene echoes or mirrors opening; changed meaning from changed understanding|Establish situation → Journey of change → Return to starting point (transformed meaning)|thematic closure; demonstrates change through contrast; satisfying symmetry; literary elegance|can feel predictable if telegraphed; requires genuine transformation to avoid stagnation; structurally constraining
SS12|Dual/Parallel Timeline|two (or more) timelines running simultaneously; past and present; two perspectives on same events; converging toward revelation|Timeline A and Timeline B alternate → Connections emerge → Convergence/revelation|dramatic irony between timelines; structural mystery; thematic parallelism; contrast illuminates|demands careful pacing; one timeline may be more engaging; convergence must be earned; can fragment attention

# character(id|name|definition|function|craft_notes)
CH1|Protagonist|central character whose desire drives the plot; reader's primary emotional investment; need not be likeable but must be compelling|carries theme through action; creates reader identification or fascination; change in protagonist = story's meaning|desire must be clear (what they want); need may differ from want (what they actually need); flaw creates growth potential; agency essential (things happen because of protagonist, not merely to them)
CH2|Antagonist|character or force opposing protagonist's desire; creates conflict; need not be villain; may have legitimate perspective|provides resistance that tests and reveals protagonist; embodies thematic counterargument; raises stakes|best antagonists believe they're the hero of their own story; as strong as protagonist (or stronger); specific, personal opposition more powerful than abstract; mirror of protagonist's flaw or fear
CH3|Foil|character who contrasts with another (usually protagonist) highlighting specific traits through difference|illuminates protagonist by contrast; shows alternative path or choice; demonstrates what protagonist is not|Sam Gamgee foils Frodo (simple courage vs burdened heroism); effective foils share one axis while differing on another; not merely opposite but meaningfully contrasting
CH4|Mentor|character who guides, teaches, or equips protagonist; often embodies wisdom protagonist must internalize|provides tools (literal or metaphorical); represents the knowledge protagonist needs; often removed to force protagonist's independence|must be removed or limited (otherwise solves protagonist's problems); may be flawed (makes advice complex); can be antagonist who teaches unwillingly
CH5|Trickster|character who disrupts, questions, and violates norms; chaos agent; reveals hypocrisy; catalyzes change through disruption|breaks stasis; forces other characters to adapt; provides comic relief with purpose; questions audience assumptions|operates outside social rules; truth-teller in disguise; can be protagonist (picaresque) or supporting; dangerous if used only for comic relief without thematic purpose
CH6|Confidant|character protagonist talks to; externalization device for inner thought; friend, ally, Watson figure|provides naturalistic way for protagonist to voice thoughts, plans, fears; audience learns through confidant's questions|must have own personality (not merely a sounding board); their reactions calibrate audience response; may have own subplot; danger: passive character without agency
CH7|Dynamic Character|character who undergoes significant internal change through story events; growth, corruption, revelation, transformation|embodies story's thematic argument through change; arc = beginning state → pressure → transformation → new state|change must be caused by story events (not arbitrary); must be demonstrated through action (not declared); protagonist is typically dynamic; supporting characters may or may not be
CH8|Static Character|character who does not fundamentally change; serves as constant against which dynamic characters are measured|provides stability; embodies principle; tests dynamic characters by remaining fixed|not a flaw — some stories require static characters (Holmes, Bond); flat ≠ static (flat = lacking dimension; static = not changing; a character can be round and static)
CH9|Round Character|character with multiple, sometimes contradictory traits; complex psychology; capable of surprising the reader in convincing ways (Forster)|creates psychological realism; engages reader's empathy; supports complex themes|internal contradiction is key (kind but capable of cruelty; brave but selfish); revealed through choices under pressure, not description; round characters surprise us yet feel inevitable
CH10|Flat Character|character built around single trait or idea; functional; recognizable immediately; does not require development|provides clarity; serves plot function efficiently; comedic types; background population|not inherently bad — most characters in any story should be flat (the bartender, the cab driver); only problematic when major characters are flat unintentionally
CH11|Archetype|universal recurring character pattern across cultures and stories; distinct from stereotype (which is reductive) by being a deep structural role|provides mythic resonance; instant recognition; enables reader to bring expectations that writer can fulfill or subvert|Hero, Shadow, Trickster, Mother, Sage, Innocent; archetypes are starting points, not destinations; subverting archetype requires establishing it first
CH12|Backstory|character history before story begins; shapes motivations, wounds, skills, relationships|explains why character acts as they do; provides wound driving current behavior; source of internal conflict|reveal only what's necessary; prefer action over flashback; the iceberg principle (writer knows 90%, reader sees 10%); backstory is not story — forward momentum matters
CH13|Character Want vs Need|want: conscious desire driving external plot (find treasure, win love); need: unconscious requirement for fulfillment (accept vulnerability, let go of control)|creates dual-layered narrative; resolution often involves abandoning want to embrace need; or achieving want only to discover need|want drives plot; need drives theme; gap between want and need = character's blind spot; stories where want ≠ need are typically most satisfying; stories where want = need tend to be simpler
CH14|Character Flaw / Wound|psychological limitation arising from formative experience; creates patterns of self-defeating behavior; both protects and imprisons character|provides obstacle for internal arc; source of reader empathy; flaw connected to strength (courage becomes recklessness; loyalty becomes blindness)|flaw must matter to plot (not cosmetic); wound must be specific (not generic); flaw creates the problem that plot forces character to confront; healing wound = character transformation
CH15|Agency|character's capacity to make meaningful choices that affect the story; things happen because of the character, not merely to them|essential for protagonist; passive protagonists kill reader engagement; even trapped characters can choose how to respond|every scene: what does character choose? what are consequences? characters who are purely acted upon are not protagonists; constraint enhances agency (interesting choices are constrained choices)

# plot(id|name|definition|mechanism|craft_notes)
PL1|Inciting Incident|event that disrupts status quo and launches protagonist into the central conflict; point of no return; story begins here|creates the question that drives the narrative; introduces the central conflict or opportunity; disturbs equilibrium|must be significant enough to warrant a whole story; protagonist must have personal stake; ideally arrives as early as possible; raises the dramatic question (will X achieve Y?)
PL2|Rising Action|sequence of escalating complications following inciting incident; stakes increase; obstacles multiply; protagonist tested|each complication raises stakes and reveals character; "yes, but..." or "no, and..." sequencing; protagonist's attempts create new problems|avoid repetitive obstacles at same level of difficulty; each complication should be different in kind not just degree; protagonist should be partly responsible for escalation (agency)
PL3|Climax|moment of highest tension where central conflict is decided; protagonist faces ultimate test; outcome determines meaning of story|everything built toward this moment; protagonist must be active agent in climax (not rescued by coincidence); irreversible|must emerge from established elements (no deus ex machina); protagonist's flaw/wound directly engaged; climax answers the dramatic question; ideally involves choice (not just action)
PL4|Resolution (Dénouement)|aftermath of climax; new equilibrium established; consequences shown; thematic implications crystallized; reader released|demonstrates how characters and world are changed; ties off (or deliberately leaves open) remaining threads; emotional landing|should be proportionate (not too long after climax); show don't tell the change; avoid explaining theme; new status quo should feel inevitable yet surprising; some threads may be left deliberately open
PL5|Subplot|secondary plot line running alongside main plot; intersects with and comments on main plot thematically|provides contrast, variety, breathing room; develops secondary characters; explores theme from different angle; can provide B-story (often relationship/emotional) that mirrors or inverts A-story|must connect to main plot thematically (not merely happen simultaneously); characters from subplot should affect main plot and vice versa; subplot climax often just before or after main climax; too many subplots fragment attention
PL6|Reversal (Peripeteia)|sudden shift in direction of story or character's fortune; situation changes to its opposite; Aristotle: most powerful when combined with recognition|creates surprise; demonstrates consequences; reveals hidden truths; keeps reader off-balance; drives story in new direction|must be causally connected to prior events (not arbitrary); best reversals are both surprising and inevitable in retrospect; reveal information that recontextualizes everything; avoid whiplash (too many reversals)
PL7|Recognition (Anagnorisis)|character's shift from ignorance to knowledge; seeing the truth; often combined with reversal; Oedipus recognizes himself as the murderer|drives character change; creates emotional power; connects external events to internal transformation; thematic crystallization|protagonist must earn recognition through suffering or effort; recognition changes how protagonist acts; reader may arrive at recognition before or after character (dramatic irony); the most powerful recognitions change the meaning of everything that came before
PL8|Complication|obstacle, setback, or new information that makes protagonist's task harder; each complication should escalate difficulty|prevents protagonist from achieving desire easily; forces adaptation, growth, or deeper commitment; reveals character through response|avoid static complications (same problem repeated); vary the source (external, interpersonal, internal); each complication should force protagonist to use different skills or confront different aspects of flaw
PL9|Turning Point|moment where story shifts direction permanently; protagonist's understanding or situation fundamentally changed; no going back|creates structural pivot; refreshes reader engagement; often coincides with act breaks; midpoint turn especially important (protagonist shifts from reactive to proactive)|must be genuinely irreversible (not fake-out); best turning points arise from character choice; midpoint turn: protagonist stops running from conflict and engages directly
PL10|Dramatic Question|the central question raised by inciting incident that drives reader through narrative; will X achieve Y? can X survive? who killed Z?|provides narrative engine; reader continues to find out the answer; answered definitively at climax|must be clear (reader should sense the question even if not articulate it); must matter (stakes must be high enough); may shift or deepen at midpoint; more complex stories have multiple layered questions
PL11|Plant and Payoff|establishing an element early (plant) that becomes significant later (payoff); Chekhov's gun principle; creates sense of inevitability|rewards attentive reading; creates structural elegance; earns plot developments; prevents deus ex machina|plant must be noticeable but not suspicious; payoff should illuminate the plant retroactively; the longer between plant and payoff, the more satisfying; payoff should be proportionate (small plant → small payoff)
PL12|Ticking Clock|time constraint pressuring protagonist; deadline creates urgency; external or internal|forces action; prevents deliberation paralysis; creates reader anxiety; natural escalation as deadline approaches|must be credible (why can't protagonist just wait?); consequence of missing deadline must be real and shown; clock can be accelerated (things get worse faster); most effective when protagonist becomes aware of clock
PL13|Red Herring|deliberately misleading element directing reader's attention away from truth; false clue; misdirection|creates surprise when truth revealed; tests reader's assumptions; extends mystery; provides satisfying "I should have seen it" moment|must be plausible (reader feels foolish, not cheated); true answer should be equally or more satisfying than red herring; don't overuse (reader stops trusting narrative); must be resolvable (reader can retrospectively see the truth was available)
PL14|Deus Ex Machina|resolution by sudden introduction of previously unestablished element; contrived rescue; authorial convenience|anti-pattern|never satisfying; breaks reader trust; robs protagonist of agency; signals author couldn't solve the problem dramatically; acceptable only as deliberate parody or commentary on narrative conventions
PL15|Cliffhanger|chapter or episode ending at moment of unresolved high tension; compels reader to continue|chapter-level: drives page-turning; serial-level: drives return for next installment; emotional or physical jeopardy|question posed must be answered relatively soon (or reader resentment); overuse diminishes impact; most effective when combined with character insight (not just physical danger); emotional cliffhangers more powerful than action cliffhangers

# dialogue(id|name|definition|function|craft_notes)
DL1|Dialogue|spoken exchange between characters; most immediate and dramatic mode of storytelling; characters revealed through what they say and how they say it|characterization; conflict; information delivery; relationship dynamics; pacing (dialogue scenes read faster)|every line should do at least two things (advance plot + reveal character; convey information + establish relationship); cut greetings, pleasantries, filler; characters should want something in every conversation
DL2|Subtext in Dialogue|what characters mean beneath what they say; gap between literal words and intended meaning; people rarely say exactly what they think|creates depth; engages reader actively (decoding); reflects real speech behavior; most powerful dialogue lives in subtext|characters talk around the thing, not about it; disagreements about surface topic mask real conflict; indirect dialogue more powerful than direct; if characters state theme directly, something is wrong
DL3|Dialogue Tag|attribution identifying speaker; "said" is invisible (best default); action beats replace tags while adding characterization|identifies speaker; sets pace; adds gesture or expression|"said" and "asked" are nearly invisible to reader; avoid creative tags (ejaculated, exclaimed, pontificated) — they draw attention to themselves; action beats (She set down her glass.) characterize while attributing; one tag or beat per exchange, not both
DL4|Dialect and Idiolect|distinctive speech patterns of region (dialect) or individual (idiolect); word choice, rhythm, grammar, vocabulary|characterization; setting; authenticity; distinguishes characters aurally|light touch (suggest rather than transcribe phonetically); consistency more important than accuracy; one or two distinctive patterns per character sufficient; readability always trumps authenticity; avoid stereotyping
DL5|Exposition in Dialogue|delivering necessary information through character speech; "As you know, Bob..." is the failure mode|naturalistic when characters have genuine reason to discuss information; fails when characters tell each other things they already know|character must have reason not to know; conflict over information is natural (interrogation, confession, argument); partial and reluctant delivery feels real; characters should resist giving information (creates tension)
DL6|Silence and Refusal|what characters choose not to say; pauses; topic avoidance; the dog that didn't bark; deliberate withholding|creates tension; characterizes through absence; engages reader's imagination; often more powerful than speech|narrate the silence explicitly (She said nothing. He waited.); what is not discussed reveals character as much as what is; refusal to answer is an answer; silence after significant statement amplifies impact
DL7|Monologue / Speech|extended single-character speech; soliloquy (alone), direct address, testimony, oration|reveals inner life (soliloquy); demonstrates passion, intellect, or madness; creates rhetorical power; risks breaking dramatic flow|must earn its length through emotional power or revelation; a monologue is a scene (it has conflict — even if internal); audience/reader must want to hear what's being said; best monologues reveal something the speaker doesn't intend to reveal
DL8|Interrogation Pattern|exchange structured around questions and resistance; one character pursuing information, other withholding or deflecting|natural conflict; natural subtext; every question is aggression, every evasion is defense; power dynamics visible|questioner's goal and respondent's resistance create scene structure; the most important question is the one not asked; answers that redirect or partially answer are more interesting than full answers

# prose(id|name|definition|function|craft_notes)
PS1|Show vs Tell|showing: rendering experience through concrete sensory detail, action, and dialogue; telling: stating facts, emotions, or judgments directly|showing creates experience; telling conveys information; both necessary; ratio depends on scene importance|show the important moments; tell the transitions; "She was angry" (tell) vs "She threw the plate against the wall" (show); showing respects reader's intelligence; telling respects reader's time
PS2|Sensory Detail|concrete information from the five senses: sight, sound, smell, touch, taste; specificity creates reality|grounds reader in physical world; creates verisimilitude; triggers reader's own sensory memory; makes abstract concrete|specific beats general ("the Lincoln Continental" beats "the car"); select details that do double duty (characterize + set scene); two or three sharp details outperform paragraph of description; unusual senses (smell, touch) are most evocative because least used
PS3|Sentence Rhythm|variation in sentence length, structure, and pattern; long flowing sentences vs short punchy ones; the music of prose|controls pacing (short = fast/tense; long = contemplative/flowing); creates emphasis; prevents monotony; mirrors content with form|vary length deliberately; short sentence after long creates emphasis; parallel structure creates momentum; sentence fragments for impact; match rhythm to emotional content; read aloud to test
PS4|Diction|word choice; level of formality; Anglo-Saxon vs Latinate; concrete vs abstract; simple vs complex|establishes voice, period, character education/class; creates mood; precision of meaning; controls distance|prefer concrete over abstract; prefer specific over general; prefer short words when short words work; Latinate vocabulary creates distance and formality; Anglo-Saxon creates immediacy and physicality; avoid words chosen to impress
PS5|Metaphor and Simile|comparison creating new understanding; metaphor: A is B; simile: A is like B; extended metaphor sustained across passage|illuminates unfamiliar through familiar; compresses meaning; creates emotional resonance; reveals character perception|original metaphors (not clichés); drawn from character's world (sailor sees waves in wheatfield); extended metaphors create structural unity; mixed metaphors confuse; dead metaphors (leg of table) are invisible — revive or avoid
PS6|Paragraph as Unit|paragraph break controls pacing and emphasis; new paragraph = new beat, new speaker, shift of attention|visual pacing on page; white space gives reader breath; single-sentence paragraph = emphasis; long paragraph = immersion|dialogue: new speaker = new paragraph; action: new beat = new paragraph; description: new focus = new paragraph; paragraph length variety creates visual rhythm on page
PS7|White Space|empty space on page; short paragraphs, line breaks, chapter breaks; what isn't written|pacing; emphasis; room for reader to feel; visual invitation (dense pages repel); creates tension (reader fills gaps)|more white space = faster read, more tension; less = immersion, density; thrillers use more white space than literary fiction; chapter breaks at tension points compel continuation
PS8|Economy|using minimum words necessary; cutting every word that doesn't earn its place; compression|respects reader's time; increases density of meaning per word; prevents boredom; creates authority|cut adverbs that duplicate verb meaning; cut adjectives that state the obvious; cut dialogue filler; cut scenes that don't advance plot or character; Hemingway: "write the truest sentence you know"
PS9|Defamiliarization (Ostranenie)|Shklovsky: making the familiar strange through unusual description, perspective, or language; restoring the freshness of perception|prevents automatized perception; forces reader to truly see/feel what has become invisible through familiarity; creates literary art|describe common things as if seen for first time; use unfamiliar perspective (alien, child, animal); precise observation defeats habit; Tolstoy describing opera from naive perspective
PS10|Free Indirect Discourse|narration blending narrator's voice with character's thoughts; third person but colored by character's vocabulary, rhythm, and perspective; no quotation marks or "she thought"|intimate access to character's mind without formal interiority signals; seamless movement between action and thought; flexibility of third person with intimacy of first|narrator's grammar + character's diction and thought patterns; "She walked into the kitchen. God, what a mess." (whose voice?); subtle shift between narrator and character perspective within same paragraph; Jane Austen, Virginia Woolf masterfully deploy this

# point_of_view(id|name|definition|advantages|limitations|craft_notes)
PV1|First Person (I)|narrator is character in story; sees only what they see; knows only what they know; filtered through their personality|maximum intimacy; natural voice; reliable or unreliable; reader fully inside one consciousness; confessional power|limited to one perspective (unless multiple first-person narrators); can't describe scenes narrator isn't present for; narrator's blind spots are story's blind spots; "I" narrator describing themselves physically is awkward|unreliable narrator: gap between what narrator says and what reader infers; present tense first person creates immediacy; past tense first person creates reflective distance; voice must be distinctive enough to sustain
PV2|Third Person Limited|narrator outside character but restricted to one character's perception, knowledge, and experience at a time; "he/she" but only their thoughts|flexibility of third person with focus of first; can shift POV between chapters; narrator can describe what character doesn't notice; close or distant psychic distance|multiple POV shifts can fragment; head-hopping (shifting mid-scene) is disorienting; each POV character needs distinct sensibility; less intimate than first person|psychic distance = how close narrator is to character's mind; can zoom from distant ("A woman walked") to close ("God, her feet hurt") within same passage; most common contemporary POV
PV3|Third Person Omniscient|narrator knows everything: all characters' thoughts, past, future, meaning; god-like perspective; can comment, judge, reveal|maximum scope; any scene accessible; authorial commentary; dramatic irony natural; pan across multiple characters|distance from any single character; harder to create intimacy; feels old-fashioned if voice isn't distinctive; reader may not know who to root for|requires strong, distinctive narrative voice (otherwise feels bland); most difficult POV to execute well; great omniscient narrators become characters themselves (Austen, Dickens, Pratchett); can modulate between panoramic and intimate
PV4|Second Person (You)|narrator addresses reader or character as "you"; reader cast as character; unusual; estranging or immersive|immediacy; reader complicity; defamiliarization; choose-your-own-adventure; imperative mood creates urgency|exhausting over length; can feel gimmicky; reader may resist being told what they feel/do; limited commercial precedent|Bright Lights, Big City; If on a winter's night a traveler; effective for present-tense, immersive, dissociative, or instructional modes; best when "you" has reason (addressing self, addressing specific person, recipe/instruction mode)
PV5|Multiple POV|story told from several characters' perspectives; each section restricted to one POV; creates prismatic effect|scope; dramatic irony (reader knows things individual characters don't); multiple emotional investments; complex portrait of events|each POV must be distinctive and necessary; unequal investment (one POV more interesting than others); structural challenge of balancing; reader may resent leaving preferred POV|each POV character should have a unique relationship to the central conflict; ideally each POV reveals something others can't; rotate at consistent intervals or by narrative logic; more than 4-5 POVs risks diffusion
PV6|Unreliable Narrator|narrator whose account is compromised by bias, ignorance, self-deception, mental illness, deliberate lying, or immaturity|creates rich interpretive space; reader actively engaged in detective work; gap between told and true is where meaning lives; dramatic irony|requires reader sophistication; must leave enough clues for reader to detect unreliability; can frustrate if reader feels manipulated rather than invited|signals of unreliability: contradictions, other characters' reactions, implausibility, defensive tone, excessive justification; best unreliable narrators are unreliable to themselves (self-deception rather than lying to reader)

# theme(id|name|definition|delivery|craft_notes)
TH1|Theme as Question|theme not as answer ("love conquers all") but as question ("can love survive betrayal?"); story explores, doesn't preach|characters embody different answers; plot tests each answer; resolution demonstrates (not declares) the story's perspective|framing theme as question prevents didacticism; multiple characters can represent different responses to the question; ambiguity is strength
TH2|Theme Through Character Arc|protagonist's transformation enacts thematic argument; what character learns = what story means|if character learns that trust is necessary despite risk of betrayal → theme is about trust and vulnerability; arc = theme in action|character's starting flaw connects to theme; obstacles force thematic confrontation; climax is thematic as well as dramatic; if theme is "courage," character must start lacking courage
TH3|Theme Through Contrast|opposing characters, subplots, or settings embody different positions on thematic question; contrast reveals through difference|antagonist represents thematic alternative; subplots explore theme from different angle; setting reflects or contradicts character's inner state|protagonist and antagonist often represent two responses to same problem; subplots that don't connect to main theme feel digressive; setting as thematic commentary (decay, renewal, confinement, openness)
TH4|Theme Through Motif|recurring images, objects, phrases accumulating thematic meaning through repetition and variation|motif appears in different contexts with shifting meaning; accumulation creates resonance; final appearance crystallizes theme|water motif: baptism → drowning → rain → tears → ocean; each appearance deepens and complicates meaning; variation more powerful than exact repetition; reader unconsciously absorbs pattern
TH5|Thematic Restraint|avoiding stating theme directly; trusting reader to derive meaning from dramatized experience; Chekhov: "the writer is not a judge but a witness"|respects reader intelligence; creates ambiguity that enables re-reading; prevents propaganda; keeps story primary|if character gives speech stating theme, story has failed at that moment; theme should be arguable (different readers may see different themes); complexity of theme correlates with quality of fiction
TH6|Universal vs Specific|paradox: the more specific and local the story, the more universally it resonates; universal themes (love, death, identity, justice) accessed through particular, unique, specific characters and situations|specificity creates verisimilitude and emotional truth; universality creates relevance and resonance|don't write about "loneliness" — write about this specific person in this specific moment experiencing this specific kind of aloneness; the universal is in the specific, never in the general

# genre(id|name|conventions|reader_expectations|genre_specific_craft)
GE1|Literary Fiction|character-driven; thematic depth; prose style foregrounded; ambiguity; open endings acceptable; interior life prioritized|psychological complexity; beautiful prose; intellectual engagement; insight into human condition; willingness to be challenged|voice and style are paramount; plot may be quieter; ending need not resolve everything; thematic ambiguity is strength; risk: privileging style over story
GE2|Mystery/Crime|central puzzle (usually: who did it?); investigation structure; clues and red herrings; resolution reveals truth|fair play (clues available to reader); satisfying solution; logical consistency; justice (or meaningful injustice); tension throughout|plant clues honestly; red herrings must be plausible; detective must earn solution; solution must be surprising yet inevitable; world must feel real (not just puzzle setting); procedural accuracy matters to readers
GE3|Thriller/Suspense|protagonist in danger; ticking clock; high stakes; fast pace; antagonist actively threatening; suspense over surprise|relentless pace; escalating danger; competent protagonist tested to limits; satisfying confrontation; twists and revelations|pace controls everything (short chapters, cliffhangers, ticking clocks); threat must be credible and personal; reader knows more than protagonist (dramatic irony creates suspense); protagonist must drive action (not merely survive)
GE4|Romance|central love story; emotionally satisfying ending (HEA: happily ever after, or HFN: happy for now); relationship is plot|emotional journey; chemistry between leads; satisfying emotional resolution; well-defined obstacles to relationship; wish fulfillment balanced with authenticity|both leads must be fully developed characters; obstacles must be genuine (not based on misunderstandings that a conversation would solve); genre has specific beats (meet-cute, first kiss, black moment, declaration); respect the reader's intelligence
GE5|Science Fiction|extrapolation from scientific or technological premise; "what if?"; world different from ours in specific, logical ways; novum (new thing) drives narrative|logical consistency of premise; world-building depth; exploration of how technology/change affects humanity; sense of wonder|one or two big changes, rigorously explored; avoid idea dumps; character must still drive story; technology creates metaphor for human condition; the novum should generate the plot (not be decoration)
GE6|Fantasy|secondary world or hidden supernatural layer; magic system; non-realistic elements treated as real within story|immersive world-building; magic with rules and costs; epic scope or intimate fairy tale; good vs evil or moral complexity; escapism + resonance|magic system needs consistent rules (Sanderson's laws: understood magic → satisfying solutions; mysterious magic → wonder); world-building revealed through character experience not exposition; familiar human emotions in unfamiliar settings
GE7|Horror|designed to evoke fear, dread, unease; confronts characters (and readers) with what they cannot understand or control|fear; dread (anticipation of fear); atmosphere; vulnerability; the uncanny (familiar made strange); sometimes catharsis|atmosphere over gore; what you don't show is scarier than what you show; protagonist must be vulnerable (invulnerable = no fear); horror reflects cultural anxieties; escalation of unease, not just jump scares; best horror is also about something (theme)
GE8|Historical Fiction|set in historical period; real or fictional characters in real historical context; research-grounded|period authenticity; immersion in different time; connection between past and present; compelling characters within historical constraints|research deeply, deploy lightly (iceberg principle); avoid anachronistic attitudes in characters; historical period should be necessary (not interchangeable decoration); balance accuracy with readability; period details through character experience, not info-dump
GE9|Comedy|primary purpose: provoke laughter; comic premise, comic characters, comic situations; may contain serious themes beneath humor|laughs; wit; surprise; recognition; relief from tension; often: restoration of social order or celebration of chaos|comic timing (sentence rhythm, paragraph break placement = punchline timing); comic characters defined by obsession or blindness; incongruity is engine of comedy; serious themes underneath comedy give it staying power

# tension(id|name|definition|mechanism|craft_notes)
TE1|Narrative Tension|reader's need to know what happens next; uncertainty about outcome; forward pull of story|created by unanswered questions; sustained by complications; released by answers that generate new questions|every scene should raise or answer a question (ideally both); chapters should end on unanswered question (not resolution); micro-tension (sentence-level) sustains even between major plot events
TE2|Conflict Escalation|each confrontation more intense than previous; stakes rise; options narrow; commitment deepens|forces in opposition grow stronger; neutral ground disappears; protagonist's resources depleted while opposition's increase|avoid flat conflict (same level of intensity throughout); each escalation should feel organic (caused by previous events); narrowing options create momentum toward climax
TE3|Dramatic Irony|reader knows something character doesn't; creates anticipation (when will they find out?) and dread (what will happen when they do?)|information asymmetry between reader and character; reader anticipates collision between character's ignorance and reality|most powerful sustained tension device; Romeo and Juliet (audience knows Juliet lives); horror film (audience sees killer, character doesn't); can sustain across entire novel
TE4|Suspense vs Surprise|Hitchcock: bomb under table. Surprise = bomb explodes (10 seconds of shock); Suspense = audience sees bomb, characters don't (10 minutes of tension)|suspense generated by information; surprise generated by withholding; suspense is stronger per unit of story time|suspense requires giving reader information characters lack; surprise requires withholding; both useful; suspense sustains longer; surprise punctuates; best narratives combine both
TE5|Micro-Tension|sentence-level and paragraph-level tension; quality of prose that creates forward momentum even in quiet scenes; no scene is "just" description or transition|word choice creates unease; syntax creates expectation; juxtaposition creates questions; every sentence should make reader need the next one|even literary fiction without high-stakes plot needs micro-tension; one character observing something off about another; an unexplained detail; a seemingly casual remark that hints at depth; tension in the prose itself
TE6|Withholding and Revelation|controlling information flow; delaying key information to create tension; revealing at maximum dramatic impact|reader senses something hidden; desire to know creates pull; revelation recontextualizes prior events; timing of revelation is everything|withhold too long → frustration; reveal too early → no tension; drip information (partial revelations create new questions); each revelation should change reader's understanding of story so far
TE7|Ticking Clock|external deadline forcing action; creates urgency independent of character psychology|bomb timer; approaching army; deadline; sunset; running out of air/fuel/money; biological clock|must be established credibly; consequence of expiry must be real and shown; can accelerate (deadline moves closer); most effective when protagonist chose the path leading to time pressure
TE8|Promise and Payoff|implicit contract with reader: if you invest attention, story will deliver; setup creates expectation that payoff fulfills or subverts|genre conventions are promises; character arcs promise resolution; planted details promise payoff; opening pages promise the kind of story this will be|betraying promises frustrates readers (not in a productive way); subverting expectations satisfies only if the subversion is more interesting than the expected payoff; payoff should exceed promise

# world(id|name|definition|function|craft_notes)
WO1|World-Building|creating the physical, social, cultural, political, economic, and ecological context of the story; applies to fantasy kingdoms and contemporary suburbs equally|constrains and enables plot; shapes character psychology and options; creates verisimilitude; secondary world must be complete enough that reader believes in it|reveal through character experience (not exposition blocks); iceberg principle (know ten times what you show); details selected for narrative relevance; world-building serves story (never reverse)
WO2|Setting as Character|environment that actively shapes, constrains, oppresses, or nurtures characters; setting with personality and agency|creates mood; provides obstacles and resources; reflects or contrasts internal states; the moors in Wuthering Heights; Yoknapatawpha County in Faulkner|setting should be necessary (story can't be set elsewhere); sensory details create physical reality; weather, architecture, landscape reflect emotional landscape; pathetic fallacy (nature reflecting mood) is powerful if not overused
WO3|Magic System / Speculative Element|rules governing non-realistic elements in fantasy, science fiction, or magical realism; must have internal consistency|creates unique constraints and opportunities for characters; generates plot problems and solutions; metaphor for real-world concerns|Sanderson's First Law: ability to use magic to solve problems is proportional to reader's understanding of that magic; cost and limitation create tension; unlimited power = no conflict; magic must surprise even the user sometimes
WO4|Social World|class structure, power dynamics, cultural norms, prejudices, institutions, relationships; the human ecology of the story|shapes character motivation and conflict; creates systematic obstacles; enables thematic exploration of power, justice, belonging|social world must be internally consistent; characters' relationship to social structure reveals character; changing social world = thematic territory; small social worlds (families, workplaces) need as much design as empires
WO5|Rules and Consistency|every story world has rules (even realistic fiction); these rules create the contract with the reader; breaking rules without justification breaks trust|enables reader prediction and investment; constraints generate plot; violations signal significance (if rules break, something important is happening)|establish rules through action (not proclamation); once established, rules constrain author as much as characters; apparent violations should have in-world explanation; inconsistency is the primary world-building failure

# revision(id|name|definition|process|craft_notes)
RV1|Structural Revision|evaluating and adjusting overall story architecture; act structure, pacing, plot logic, character arcs|read entire manuscript noting structural issues; outline what exists (vs what was planned); identify saggy middle, rushed ending, missing turns; may require major rewriting, scene reordering, subplot addition/removal|first revision pass should always be structural (don't polish sentences that might be cut); does every scene advance plot or character? is the climax the biggest scene? does the ending feel earned?
RV2|Character Revision|ensuring characters are consistent, motivated, distinctive, and dimensional; voice differentiation; arc completion|read through each character's scenes consecutively; check consistency of voice, motivation, knowledge; verify arc has beginning, middle, end; check that each character wants something in every scene|cover character names and test if you can identify speaker from dialogue alone; check for puppet characters (author's mouthpiece without own personality); verify antagonist has valid perspective; ensure protagonist has agency
RV3|Line Editing|sentence-level refinement; rhythm, precision, economy, clarity; eliminating dead words, clichés, vague language|read aloud (ear catches what eye misses); cut adverbs modifying strong verbs; eliminate filter words (she saw, he felt, they heard — just describe what's seen/felt/heard); activate passive voice; vary sentence length|cutting is the primary tool (most drafts are 10-20% too long); every word must earn its place; strongest word in sentence goes at end; read aloud tests rhythm; awkward = rewrite, never just rearrange
RV4|Dialogue Revision|making dialogue sound natural while being more purposeful than real speech; checking voice differentiation; subtext|read dialogue aloud (or have others read roles); cut anything that doesn't advance plot or reveal character; check that each character sounds different; verify subtext (is anything too on-the-nose?)|real speech is full of filler, repetition, digression — dialogue should feel real but be shaped; each character should have speech patterns (vocabulary, rhythm, habitual phrases, what they avoid saying)
RV5|Pacing Revision|adjusting speed of reading experience; scene length, chapter length, tension-to-release ratio; breathing room|map scene lengths and emotional intensity; identify sequences that drag or rush; insert breathing room after intense scenes; accelerate approach to climax|short chapters = fast pace; long chapters = immersive; scene-summary-scene rhythm; action scenes should be shorter than reader expects; aftermath scenes should be longer than writer wants to write
RV6|Continuity Check|verifying factual consistency; timeline, character knowledge, physical details, established rules|create timeline; track character locations and knowledge; verify established facts not contradicted; check world-building rules maintained|readers catch what writers miss; character eye color, day of week, distance between locations, what characters know and when they learn it; spreadsheets help for complex narratives; beta readers catch what author can't see
RV7|Kill Your Darlings|willingness to cut material you love that doesn't serve the story; beautiful sentences, clever scenes, beloved characters that don't pull their weight|if removing a passage doesn't damage the story, it shouldn't be there regardless of how good it is; save cut material in separate file (psychological comfort)|save file of cuts reduces pain; the test: does this scene exist for the reader's benefit or the writer's? favorite scenes are often the most self-indulgent; sometimes the best writing is what you don't include

# failure_modes(id|topic|mode|cause|consequence|prevention)
FM1|character|protagonist lacks agency|things happen to character rather than because of character; events driven by coincidence; character is passive recipient|reader disengages; feels like watching not participating; no emotional investment in outcomes|every scene: what does character choose? what do they cause? even constrained characters must choose how to respond; protagonist should drive at least 60% of plot events
FM2|character|flat protagonist|single trait; no internal contradiction; no surprising behavior; no depth|boring; predictable; reader can't form attachment; story feels thin|internal contradiction (kind but capable of cruelty); desire vs need gap; specific backstory wound creating specific behavioral pattern; test: can character surprise reader while remaining consistent?
FM3|plot|sagging middle|Act II lacks escalation; complications at same level; protagonist reactive; subplot doesn't connect to main plot|reader puts book down; loses momentum; "I couldn't finish it"|midpoint turn (protagonist goes from reactive to proactive); each complication escalates in stakes and difficulty; new information reframes conflict; subplot converges with main plot at midpoint
FM4|plot|unearned ending|climax resolved by coincidence, new information, or external rescue; deus ex machina; protagonist doesn't drive resolution|reader feels cheated; story's thematic argument undermined; no catharsis|every element of climax must be established earlier (plant and payoff); protagonist must be active agent; resolution must emerge from character growth and choice; test: could this ending happen without protagonist's transformation? if yes, ending is unearned
FM5|dialogue|on-the-nose dialogue|characters state exactly what they feel, want, or think; no subtext; exposition dumps through speech; "As you know, Bob..."|feels unnatural; boring; insults reader intelligence; eliminates interpretive engagement|characters have reasons to conceal, misdirect, or avoid; information emerges through conflict not cooperation; subtext: what characters mean ≠ what they say; every conversation has an agenda beneath the surface
FM6|prose|purple prose|overwritten; excessive adjectives and adverbs; ornate where simple would serve; drawing attention to prose rather than story|reader notices writing instead of experiencing story; pace drags; emotional moments feel manipulated|economy: prefer one precise word over three vague ones; sensory detail should be specific not abundant; let powerful moments be simple; test: is this sentence serving the reader or the writer's ego?
FM7|prose|white-room dialogue|characters speak without physical grounding; no setting, gesture, or sensory detail; floating heads in white space|reader loses spatial and physical reality; scenes feel abstract; characterization through body language absent|anchor dialogue in physical world; characters interact with environment; gesture and expression carry subtext; setting affects conversation (public vs private, comfort vs discomfort)
FM8|structure|false start|story begins too early; excessive setup before inciting incident; backstory before drama; "warm-up" pages the writer needed but reader doesn't|reader bored before story begins; may not reach the actual story; first pages are audition and story fails the audition|start as late as possible; begin with character in motion (physical or emotional); first scene should contain conflict, desire, or intriguing situation; cut opening pages until you reach the point where things start happening
FM9|theme|preachiness|author uses story to deliver message; characters become mouthpieces; plot engineered to prove thesis; reader feels lectured|reader resists; stops trusting narrative; story becomes propaganda regardless of message quality; artistry sacrificed to message|theme through dramatization not declaration; characters embody positions, not argue them; let reader draw conclusions; best thematic fiction makes opposing position sympathetic; Chekhov: "the writer is not a judge"
FM10|world|exposition dump|large blocks of world-building, history, or technical information halting narrative momentum; author's research on display|pace dies; reader skips paragraphs; information without context is unmemorable; "I did a lot of research and you're going to read all of it"|reveal world through character need (information given when character — and reader — needs it); three sentences of world-building woven into scene > one paragraph of explanation; trust reader to infer from specific details
FM11|pov|head-hopping|shifting between characters' internal thoughts within single scene without clear transition; reader unsure whose thoughts they're experiencing|disorienting; prevents deep immersion in any one character; breaks psychic distance; amateurish|one POV per scene (strict); establish whose scene it is within first paragraph; if POV must shift, use clear break (section break, chapter break); brief departures from POV for external observation acceptable if narrator is omniscient
FM12|tension|false tension|attempting to create tension through withholding information the POV character knows; "little did she know"; narrator teasing reader|reader feels manipulated not engaged; artificial rather than organic; breaks trust between narrator and reader|tension from genuine uncertainty (character and reader both don't know); dramatic irony from information asymmetry between characters (not between narrator and reader); if POV character knows something, reader should too

# distinctions(id|side_a|side_b|key_asymmetry)
DI1|Story|Plot|story: sequence of events in chronological order (what happened); plot: causal arrangement of events for dramatic effect (why things happened and in what order the reader experiences them); Forster: "the king died and then the queen died" (story) vs "the king died and then the queen died of grief" (plot)
DI2|Character-Driven|Plot-Driven|character-driven: events emerge from character decisions and psychology; change is internal; literary fiction tendency; plot-driven: events driven by external circumstances and action; change is situational; genre fiction tendency; best fiction integrates both
DI3|Showing|Telling|showing: rendering experience through concrete sensory detail, action, dialogue; reader experiences and concludes; slow but immersive; telling: stating information directly; narrator asserts; fast but distancing; both necessary; show important moments, tell transitions
DI4|Scene|Summary|scene: dramatized continuous action in real time; contains conflict and change; primary mode for important moments; summary: compressed narration covering time efficiently; transitions, backstory, routine events; secondary mode for connective tissue
DI5|Suspense|Surprise|suspense: reader knows danger exists, character doesn't; sustained tension; requires information given to reader; surprise: sudden reversal reader didn't anticipate; momentary shock; requires information withheld from reader; Hitchcock: suspense is superior per unit of story time
DI6|Sympathetic|Compelling|sympathetic: reader likes and identifies with character; wants good things for them; root-for quality; compelling: reader cannot look away; fascinated even if repelled; driven to understand; protagonist must be compelling; need not be sympathetic; many great protagonists are compelling but unsympathetic (Humbert Humbert, Walter White)
DI7|Literary Fiction|Genre Fiction|literary: style and character primary; thematic ambiguity; open endings; prose as art; internal focus; genre: plot and convention primary; satisfying resolution; reader expectations honored; external focus; best work transcends distinction; genre fiction with literary depth; literary fiction with compelling plot
DI8|Voice|Style|voice: the personality behind the prose; who is speaking; irreducible individuality; can't be fully separated from the person writing; style: the craft techniques deployed; sentence structure, diction, rhythm, rhetorical devices; can be analyzed and learned; voice uses style but is not reducible to it
DI9|Revision|Editing|revision: author reconceiving and reshaping own work; structural, creative, generative (adding material); seeing again; editing: refining existing material; cutting, polishing, correcting; removing excess; revision is creative; editing is critical; revision first, editing after
DI10|Premise|Theme|premise: what-if foundation of story; situation, scenario, concept; "what if a boy discovered he was a wizard?"; theme: what the story means; what it says about human experience; "love is the most powerful magic"; premise generates plot; theme generates meaning; connected but distinct
