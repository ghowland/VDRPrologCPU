# domains(id|name|definition)
DM1|ancient literature|works from earliest writing through fall of Western Roman Empire (~476 CE); Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Hebrew, Greek, Roman, Indian, Chinese; oral tradition preceding written; foundational myths, epics, philosophy, drama, history
DM2|medieval literature|~476–1450 CE; religious dominance (Christian, Islamic, Buddhist); allegory, romance, saga, courtly love; vernacular emergence alongside Latin; manuscript culture
DM3|Renaissance and early modern|~1450–1700; humanism; rediscovery of classical texts; printing press revolution; vernacular masterworks; drama (Elizabethan, Spanish Golden Age); essay form emerges; novel proto-forms
DM4|Enlightenment and 18th century|~1700–1800; reason, satire, social critique; rise of the novel; epistolary form; philosophical fiction; encyclopedic ambition; political literature
DM5|19th century|Romanticism → Realism → Naturalism; novel becomes dominant form; poetry: Romantic lyric → symbolism; drama: melodrama → Ibsen/Chekhov realism; American literature emerges; Russian golden age
DM6|20th century and modernism|~1900–1960; fragmentation, stream of consciousness, unreliable narrator, mythic method, existentialism; two world wars reshape all literature; colonial and postcolonial voices emerge
DM7|late 20th century and contemporary|~1960–present; postmodernism, magical realism, metafiction, identity politics, global literature, genre-crossing; canon expansion (women, non-Western, marginalized voices); Nobel recognitions broaden

# themes(id|name|definition|works_exemplifying|universal_significance)
TM1|fate vs free will|do humans control their destiny or are they predetermined?; classical: gods/fate vs human choice; modern: social determinism, psychological compulsion, existential freedom|WK3 (Oedipus: every free action fulfills fate), WK6 (Dante: divine plan allows free will within it), WK8 (Hamlet: delay as existential crisis of will), WK12 (Faust: striving as self-determination), WK23 (Meursault: indifferent to both)|every major literary tradition asks this; no resolution: the tension IS the insight; literature explores cases, not answers
TM2|identity and self-knowledge|who am I? can I know myself? is identity fixed or performed?|WK3 (Oedipus discovers he is what he hunted), WK8 (Hamlet: to thine own self be true — but which self?), WK10 (Quixote: is he mad or the only sane one?), WK19 (Gregor: does form determine identity?), WK22 (Sethe: claiming selfhood after slavery denied it)|literature's central question after survival; every culture's literature explores how identity is constructed, discovered, lost, and recovered
TM3|justice and morality|what is right? is the universe just? can human institutions achieve justice?|WK4 (Republic: what is justice?), WK9 (Lear: is there cosmic justice? — answer: no), WK14 (Raskolnikov: can reason justify murder?), WK18 (Ivan: if God doesn't exist, everything is permitted), WK23 (Meursault: judged for wrong crime)|every legal, political, and religious system rests on a theory of justice; literature tests these theories against lived experience
TM4|love and desire|romantic love, familial love, divine love, obsession, jealousy; love as redemption, destruction, illusion, truth|WK5 (Aeneid: Dido and Aeneas: love vs duty), WK6 (Dante: Beatrice: love as cosmic force), WK13 (Austen: love as mutual recognition), WK16 (Anna Karenina: passion that destroys), WK24 (Proust: love as projection and jealousy)|universal; every culture's literature; love as both individual experience and social institution; literature explores what love does to people, not what it should be
TM5|mortality and meaning|awareness of death shapes all human activity; literature confronts: does death give life meaning or render it meaningless?|WK1 (Achilles: glory vs long life), WK6 (Dante: afterlife as moral system), WK8 (Hamlet: to be or not to be), WK15 (Prince Andrei's death; Ivan Ilyich), WK23 (Camus: the absurd: consciousness of death + desire for meaning = human condition)|death is the one fact no culture, philosophy, or individual can avoid; literature's unique contribution: makes death imaginable, confrontable, and (sometimes) meaningful
TM6|power and corruption|how power changes those who hold it; institutional corruption; tyranny vs legitimate authority|WK3 (Oedipus: king becomes outcast), WK8 (Claudius: power through murder), WK9 (Lear: power surrendered → chaos), WK15 (Napoleon in War and Peace: illusion of individual power), WK18 (Grand Inquisitor: church as power structure that betrays its founder)|political literature from Plato to Morrison; power is literature's second subject after love; literature shows what political theory abstracts
TM7|alienation and isolation|modern theme: individual separated from society, from others, from self; loneliness as condition not choice|WK14 (Raskolnikov: isolation through theory), WK19 (Gregor: literal alienation: insect), WK20 (Bloom: wandering Jew in Dublin), WK21 (solitude: Buendía family destiny), WK23 (Meursault: emotional exile)|industrial, urban, modern life produces alienation; literature diagnoses what sociology describes; Kafka and Camus: defining voices
TM8|appearance vs reality|things are not what they seem; masks, deception, performance, illusion; truth hidden behind surfaces|WK3 (Oedipus: truth hidden in plain sight), WK8 (Hamlet: seeming: all Denmark performs), WK10 (Quixote: whose perception is real?), WK13 (Austen: first impressions mislead), WK24 (Proust: social world as elaborate performance concealing emptiness)|epistemological: can we know what is real? moral: should we prefer comfortable illusion or painful truth? aesthetic: art itself is appearance (fiction) that reveals truth

# literary_devices(id|name|definition|effect|master_practitioners)
LD1|dramatic irony|audience/reader knows something characters do not; gap between what character believes and what is true|creates tension, pity, dread; audience watches characters walk toward fate they cannot see; deepens engagement|Sophocles (WK3: every line in Oedipus Rex is dramatically ironic; audience knows what Oedipus discovers), Shakespeare (WK8: audience knows uncle is murderer before Hamlet proves it)
LD2|unreliable narrator|narrator whose credibility is compromised: by limitation (child, mentally ill), bias (self-serving), or deliberate deception; reader must read against the narration|creates interpretive complexity; reader becomes active participant (must decide what really happened); raises questions about all narration and representation|Dostoevsky (Notes from Underground: narrator contradicts himself), Nabokov (Lolita: Humbert Humbert's eloquence disguises monstrosity), Kazuo Ishiguro (Remains of the Day: Stevens's self-deception), Faulkner (WK27: Benjy section: cognitive limitation)
LD3|stream of consciousness|narrative technique representing continuous flow of thoughts, sensations, memories, and associations as they occur in a character's mind; minimal narrator intervention; often without conventional punctuation or syntax|intimacy with character's inner life; mimics actual mental experience; challenges linear narrative; reveals how consciousness actually works (non-linear, associative, sensory)|Joyce (WK20: Molly Bloom), Woolf (WK22: Mrs Dalloway: tunneling between characters' minds), Faulkner (WK27: Benjy, Quentin sections)
LD4|allegory|narrative where characters, events, and settings systematically represent abstract ideas or historical events; meaning operates on two levels simultaneously (literal story + symbolic meaning)|enables discussion of dangerous ideas under cover of fiction; universalizes specific experience; religious and political works: Bunyan, Orwell, Dante|Dante (WK6: entire Comedy is allegory operating on four levels), Kafka (WK19: Metamorphosis: allegorical but of what? — Kafka's genius: allegory without key), Orwell (Animal Farm: political allegory of Russian Revolution), Camus (The Plague: allegory of occupation)
LD5|free indirect discourse|narrator's voice merges with character's thought: third person but from character's perspective and vocabulary; no he thought or she felt tagging; reader is simultaneously inside and outside character|combines narrator's ironic distance with character's intimate perspective; enables Austen's characteristic irony; readers may not notice the technique (it feels natural: that is its power)|Austen (WK13: It is a truth universally acknowledged...: whose voice? narrator's irony? society's belief? both simultaneously), Flaubert (Madame Bovary: perfected technique), Woolf
LD6|in medias res|narrative begins in the middle of the action (not at chronological beginning); backstory filled in through flashback, narration, or dialogue|creates immediate engagement; avoids expository opening; mimics how we actually encounter situations (without full context); epic convention since Homer|Homer (WK1: Iliad begins in 10th year of war; WK2: Odyssey begins 10 years after war ended), Virgil (WK5: Aeneid opens mid-storm), Milton (WK11: Paradise Lost opens with Satan in Hell: most dramatic possible beginning)
LD7|magical realism|realistic narrative in which magical or supernatural elements are presented as ordinary, without surprise or explanation; not fantasy (which creates separate world); magic is embedded in the real|challenges Western rationalist assumptions about what is real; honors non-European epistemologies (Latin American, African, Indian: the magical is part of lived reality, not exception to it); political: magic as resistance to colonial rationalism|García Márquez (WK21: defining practitioner), Salman Rushdie (Midnight's Children), Isabel Allende (The House of the Spirits), Ben Okri (The Famished Road), Toni Morrison (WK22: Beloved as flesh: ghost made real)
LD8|epiphany (literary)|moment of sudden spiritual or psychological revelation triggered by ordinary experience; character (and reader) sees truth that was always present but invisible|transforms understanding without external action (nothing happens yet everything changes); elevates ordinary experience to spiritual significance; modern replacement for divine revelation|Joyce (coined literary use: Dubliners: every story builds to epiphanic moment), Woolf (moments of being: To the Lighthouse), Chekhov (stories end at moment of recognition that changes nothing externally but everything internally)

# concepts(id|name|definition|category)
LC1|canon|body of works considered essential, authoritative, or foundational; always contested: who decides? whose voices are included/excluded? canon = power (Harold Bloom: Western Canon; counter-arguments: canon excludes women, non-Western, marginalized voices; modern response: expand canon, don't abolish it: include Morrison alongside Shakespeare, not instead of)|literary_theory
LC2|mimesis|imitation or representation of reality in art; Plato: art imitates appearances (twice removed from truth: critique); Aristotle: art imitates action (praxis) and reveals universals (defense); all subsequent realism descends from Aristotelian mimesis; modernism: questioned whether mimesis is possible or desirable|literary_theory
LC3|catharsis|purging of emotions (pity and fear) through experiencing tragedy; Aristotle (Poetics): tragedy arouses pity (for suffering) and fear (that it could happen to us) then purges them → emotional and moral clarification; debated: is catharsis emotional release? intellectual clarification? both?|literary_theory
LC4|bildungsroman|novel of formation: protagonist grows from youth to maturity through experience, error, and self-discovery; structure: innocence → experience → integration; German origin (Goethe: Wilhelm Meister); became dominant novel form|genre_concept
LC5|intertextuality|every text exists in relation to other texts; meaning is produced not in isolation but through dialogue between texts; direct (allusion, quotation, parody, adaptation) and indirect (shared tropes, genres, conventions); Borges: every writer creates their precursors (our reading of Homer is changed by Joyce)|literary_theory
LC6|close reading|careful analysis of how a text generates meaning through specific word choice, syntax, imagery, sound, structure, and form; New Criticism (1940s–60s) formalized but practice is ancient; reading slowly, attending to every word, is the fundamental literary skill; the text itself is the primary evidence|method
LC7|unreliable narration|all narration is selection and arrangement (therefore interpretation); some narrators are more explicitly unreliable (see LD2); but the concept implies: every narrator, including omniscient, constructs rather than reports reality; reading is always interpretation|literary_theory
LC8|the sublime|experience of awe, terror, and transcendence before vastness (nature, art, God, death); Burke: sublime = terror at safe distance; Kant: sublime reveals our capacity to conceive what exceeds perception; in literature: language stretched to limits of expression|aesthetic_concept
LC9|tragedy|dramatic (or narrative) form in which a character of significance falls from prosperity to ruin through some combination of fate, character flaw (hamartia), and choice; Aristotle: protagonist must be neither entirely good nor entirely evil; fall must arouse pity and fear; tragedy reveals limits of human agency|genre_concept
LC10|irony|gap between appearance and reality, between what is said and what is meant, between expectation and outcome; verbal irony (saying opposite of meaning), situational irony (outcome contradicts expectation), dramatic irony (audience knows what characters don't); irony is the dominant mode of modern literature (since Austen)|literary_mode
