# domains(id|name|definition)
DM1|silviculture|the art and science of controlling establishment, growth, composition, health, and quality of forests to meet landowner objectives; manipulation of forest stands through planting, thinning, pruning, harvesting, and prescribed fire
DM2|forest ecology|study of forest ecosystems: species interactions, succession, nutrient cycling, disturbance regimes, biodiversity, and energy flow; understanding how forests function as systems
DM3|timber management|production of wood fiber and timber as a commodity; rotation planning, yield optimization, harvest scheduling, log grading, and market economics
DM4|watershed management|management of forested land to protect water quality, regulate flow, prevent erosion, and maintain aquatic habitat; forests as water infrastructure
DM5|wildlife management|management of forest habitat to support wildlife populations: structural diversity, mast production, denning sites, corridors, edge habitat, and species-specific management
DM6|fire management|prescribed fire, wildfire suppression, fuels management, fire ecology, and community protection; fire as both tool and threat
DM7|forest health|monitoring and management of biotic (insects, disease, invasive species) and abiotic (drought, wind, ice) threats to forest vigor
DM8|recreation and aesthetics|management of forests for human recreation, scenic beauty, cultural values, and spiritual connection; trail systems, viewsheds, wilderness
DM9|soil and site management|understanding and protecting the soil resource: productivity, erosion prevention, compaction avoidance, nutrient cycling, and site quality classification
DM10|carbon and climate|forests as carbon sinks and sources; carbon sequestration, storage, and flux; climate change adaptation and mitigation through forestry
DM11|agroforestry|intentional integration of trees with crops and/or livestock on the same land management unit; alley cropping, silvopasture, riparian buffers, windbreaks, forest farming
DM12|urban forestry|management of trees and forests in and around human settlements; street trees, parks, greenways, urban canopy, stormwater management, heat island mitigation

# silviculture(id|name|type|objective|method|stand_structure_before|stand_structure_after|regeneration_method|species_favored|rotation_interval|notes)
SV1|clearcut|even-aged|remove entire stand; regenerate new even-aged stand; maximize volume harvest; reset successional clock|harvest all merchantable trees in one operation; area: 2–40+ ha (size regulated by jurisdiction and objectives); may retain scattered seed trees, snags, or legacy trees for wildlife|closed-canopy mature or overmature stand|open (full sun); slash and stumps; prepared for regeneration|planting (most common in commercial); natural seeding from adjacent stands or retained seed trees; sprouting (hardwoods: oak, maple)|shade-intolerant: loblolly pine, Douglas-fir, aspen, birch, jack pine, Eucalyptus|20–50 (plantation); 60–120 (natural)|most efficient harvest method; lowest per-unit cost; most controversial aesthetically; best for shade-intolerant species; creates early successional habitat (valuable for many wildlife species); requires BMPs for soil and water protection; retention of some trees (variable retention) improves biodiversity outcomes
SV2|shelterwood|even-aged|establish regeneration under partial canopy shade; protect seedlings from frost, sun, and wind; produce even-aged stand with mid-tolerant species|preparatory cut (optional: thin to develop seed trees); seed cut (reduce to 30–50% canopy to allow light and seed fall); removal cut (remove overstory after regeneration established: 5–15 years after seed cut)|closed-canopy mature stand|semi-open after seed cut (residual overstory provides shelter); open after removal cut|natural seeding from retained overstory; supplemented by planting if natural regeneration insufficient|mid-tolerant: oak, cherry, walnut, white pine, some pines; shade-tolerant under certain conditions|80–120 (hardwoods); 40–80 (some conifers)|two to three cuts over 10–20 years; more aesthetically acceptable than clearcut; better micro-environment for seedlings; higher cost than clearcut (multiple entries); timing of removal cut critical: too early → seedlings not established; too late → overstory suppresses regeneration
SV3|seed tree|even-aged|remove most of stand but retain scattered seed trees (5–15 per hectare) to provide seed source for natural regeneration|harvest all trees except selected seed trees: well-formed, vigorous, good crown, wind-firm; remove seed trees after regeneration established (3–10 years)|closed-canopy mature stand|open with scattered individuals (2–5% canopy cover)|natural seeding from retained seed trees; wind-dispersed species most suitable (pine, birch, aspen)|shade-intolerant with wind-dispersed seed: loblolly pine, longleaf pine, birch; moderate: some oaks|similar to clearcut rotation|cheaper than shelterwood (fewer entries); more natural appearance than clearcut; seed trees must be wind-firm (isolated trees vulnerable to blowdown); seed trees of highest quality (genetic improvement: retain best phenotypes); risk: seed tree blowdown before regeneration established
SV4|single-tree selection|uneven-aged|maintain continuous canopy cover; harvest individual trees at maturity; create small canopy gaps for regeneration; produce uneven-aged stand with multiple age classes simultaneously|mark and harvest individual trees based on target diameter distribution (reverse-J curve: many small, few large); cutting cycle: 10–20 years; remove 25–35% of volume per entry|uneven-aged: multiple canopy layers; all age classes present|uneven-aged: canopy gaps where trees removed; surrounding trees expand into gaps; smaller and mid-story trees released|natural regeneration in canopy gaps; shade-tolerant species fill gaps; advance regeneration (seedlings already established in understory) is released|shade-tolerant: sugar maple, beech, hemlock, spruce, fir; mid-tolerant can be maintained with larger gaps|cutting cycle: 10–20 years (continuous; no rotation per se; individual trees reach target diameter over 80–150 years)|continuous canopy cover (aesthetically preferred); maintains late-successional habitat; complex structure; higher per-unit harvest cost (selective marking, careful logging); requires skilled markers; does NOT work for shade-intolerant species (they will not regenerate in small gaps); residual stand damage from repeated entries is concern
SV5|group selection|uneven-aged|create openings (0.1–0.5 ha) within stand; openings large enough for shade-intolerant or mid-tolerant species; maintain uneven-aged structure at landscape level|harvest all trees within group (opening diameter = 1–2× tree heights for full sun in opening center); leave surrounding stand intact; new groups harvested each cutting cycle|uneven-aged (at stand level: mosaic of different-aged groups)|mosaic: openings (regenerating) + mature forest (surrounding)|natural regeneration in openings (may be supplemented by planting); species determined by opening size: larger openings → intolerant species; smaller → tolerant|any species (adjust opening size to shade tolerance): oak (0.2–0.5 ha openings), pine (0.3–0.5 ha), maple (0.05–0.1 ha)|cutting cycle: 10–20 years (new groups each cycle)|compromise between even-aged (clearcut) and uneven-aged (selection); accommodates range of species; maintains more structural diversity than clearcut; lower per-unit cost than single-tree selection; creates diverse habitat mosaic
SV6|thinning (pre-commercial)|intermediate treatment|reduce stocking in young stands to improve growth rate, health, and quality of remaining trees; no merchantable product removed|cut or girdle undesirable trees at age 10–25 (depending on species); reduce to target density (e.g., 1,000 to 500 stems/ha); favor crop trees with best form, vigor, and species|overstocked young stand (>1,000–3,000 stems/ha from natural regeneration)|reduced density; remaining trees have more growing space; crown expansion begins|n/a (intermediate treatment, not regeneration)|favors desired species and best individuals within species|once (sometimes twice) before commercial thinning|cost without revenue (investment); essential for quality timber production; reduces competition stress; prevents stagnation; should be done before crown ratio drops below 40%
SV7|thinning (commercial)|intermediate treatment|remove merchantable trees to improve growth of residuals and generate intermediate revenue; reduce stocking to optimize growth rate|mark and harvest trees to target residual basal area or relative density; types: low thinning (from below: remove suppressed, intermediate); crown thinning (from above: remove some codominants to release best codominants and dominants); free thinning (remove trees that compete with selected crop trees)|fully stocked to overstocked stand (basal area above optimal for growth)|reduced basal area; remaining trees respond with increased diameter growth; improved crown vigor|n/a (intermediate treatment)|retains best trees; removes poor form, low vigor, diseased, or less desirable species|every 10–15 years; typically 1–3 commercial thins before final harvest|generates revenue during rotation; accelerates growth on crop trees; reduces density-related mortality; improves stand health; thinning too heavy: epicormic branching (especially in hardwoods), windthrow risk; thinning too light: insufficient response; residual stand damage from logging: minimize (directional felling, designated skid trails)
SV8|prescribed fire|intermediate or site preparation|use controlled fire to: reduce fuel loads, prepare seedbed, control competing vegetation, maintain fire-adapted ecosystems, recycle nutrients, control disease|set fire under prescription (weather, fuel moisture, wind, relative humidity conditions specified); types: surface fire (burns understory, litter); broadcast burn (entire area); pile burn (slash piles); backing fire (against wind: low intensity); head fire (with wind: higher intensity)|varies: any stand condition where fuel reduction, seedbed prep, or ecosystem maintenance is objective|reduced litter and understory; exposed mineral soil (seedbed); reduced ladder fuels (vertical continuity from ground to canopy); nutrient release; stimulated seed germination (some species)|fire-adapted species: longleaf pine (requires fire for grass-stage seedling survival), ponderosa pine, oak (fire promotes oak over maple), jack pine (serotinous cones: require fire to open)|varies by ecosystem: longleaf: every 1–3 years; ponderosa: every 3–7 years; oak: every 5–10 years|most important management tool in fire-adapted ecosystems; absence of fire → fuel accumulation → catastrophic wildfire; also → species conversion (shade-tolerant species replace fire-adapted species: maple replacing oak in eastern US); smoke management plan required (air quality regulations); burn plan: legal document specifying conditions and contingencies; liability varies by state (simple negligence vs gross negligence standard)
SV9|pruning|intermediate treatment|remove lower branches from crop trees to produce clear (knot-free) wood; increases log value (clear wood premium: 2–5× knotty)|prune to 5–6 m height (one 5 m log); prune when branches <5 cm diameter (heal faster, smaller knot core); prune 100–300 crop trees per hectare (not all trees); leave at least 50% live crown after pruning|any young stand with crop trees identified|same stand with pruned lower stems; clear wood produced over pruned core as tree grows diameter|n/a|high-value species: walnut, cherry, Douglas-fir, white pine, sugar maple, radiata pine|once or twice (pruning to 3 m at age 8–10; to 6 m at age 15–20)|ROI depends on species value and growth rate; highest return on fast-growing high-value species; pruning wounds must heal quickly (prune living branches flush with collar; don't leave stubs; don't use wound paint); dead branch pruning less critical (branches already occluded)

# hydrology(id|name|type|function|forest_management_impact|BMP|notes)

FE4|wildfire|unplanned fire|ranges from surface to crown fire; behavior driven by weather, topography, and fuels (fire behavior triangle); extreme fire weather: temperature >32°C, RH <15%, wind >30 km/h, unstable atmosphere → dangerous fire behavior|natural ecological role: creates mosaic of successional stages; creates snags and logs (wildlife habitat); opens canopy; recycles nutrients; however: fire exclusion → fuel accumulation → fires now more severe than historical; WUI (wildland-urban interface) at increasing risk|suppression: direct attack (fire line construction, retardant, water); indirect attack (backfiring from control line); extended attack (large fire organization: ICS); prevention: fuels management, defensible space (30–60 m around structures), building codes (ignition-resistant construction)|fire seasons lengthening with climate change; area burned increasing in western US, Canada, Australia, Mediterranean; WUI expansion increases risk to lives and structures; suppression costs: US >$3B/yr federal; cost of suppression + damage increasingly exceeds cost of prevention

# concepts(id|name|definition|category)
LC1|succession|predictable sequential change in species composition and structure over time following disturbance; early: shade-intolerant pioneers (birch, aspen, pine); mid: mixed; late: shade-tolerant species dominate (maple, beech, hemlock); each stage has characteristic wildlife, structure, and productivity|ecology
LC2|stand|contiguous group of trees sufficiently uniform in species composition, age, arrangement, and condition to be a distinguishable unit; the basic management unit in forestry|management
LC3|stocking|relative measure of stand density compared to optimal for a given management objective; fully stocked: optimal growth (no wasted growing space, minimal competition-related mortality); overstocked: growth suppression, mortality; understocked: wasted site potential|management
LC4|rotation|period between stand establishment (regeneration) and final harvest; biological rotation: age of maximum mean annual increment (MAI = total volume / age); economic rotation: age that maximizes NPV (Faustmann); always shorter than biological because of discounting|management
LC5|site quality|inherent productive capacity of a site for growing trees; determined by soil (depth, texture, drainage, nutrients), climate (temperature, precipitation, growing season length), and topography (aspect, slope position, elevation); measured by site index|ecology
LC6|best management practices (BMPs)|set of practices designed to prevent or minimize environmental degradation (especially water quality and soil) during forest operations; typically regulatory requirement; includes: road construction/maintenance standards, SMZs, equipment operation restrictions, reforestation requirements, slash management|management
LC7|sustained yield|management principle: annual or periodic harvest should not exceed net growth of the forest over the same period; ensures perpetual production; may be modified to allow capitalization of overstocked or overmature stands, but long-term harvest ≤ growth|management
LC8|multiple use|management philosophy: forests managed for multiple simultaneous outputs: timber, water, wildlife, recreation, grazing, aesthetics, carbon, and non-timber forest products; no single use dominates to the exclusion of others; tradeoffs acknowledged and balanced|management
LC9|adaptive management|management approach: implement management action as a deliberate experiment; monitor results; adjust actions based on observed outcomes; cycle of: plan → implement → monitor → evaluate → adjust → plan; essential when outcomes are uncertain (climate change, novel pests)|management
LC10|ecosystem services|benefits humans derive from ecosystems; provisioning (timber, water, food); regulating (carbon sequestration, flood control, water purification, pollination); supporting (nutrient cycling, soil formation, habitat); cultural (recreation, aesthetics, spiritual); forests provide all four categories simultaneously|ecology
LC11|carbon sequestration|net removal of CO₂ from atmosphere and storage in biomass (wood) and soil; forests sequester ~2.6 Gt C/year globally (net: after fire, decay, and disturbance); young growing forests: highest sequestration rate per year; old forests: highest standing stock (total carbon stored); managed forests: harvested wood products extend carbon storage in buildings and furniture|climate
LC12|forest certification|voluntary third-party verification that forest management meets specified environmental, social, and economic standards; major systems: FSC (Forest Stewardship Council) and PEFC/SFI (Programme for the Endorsement / Sustainable Forestry Initiative); certified area: ~450 million ha globally (~11% of world forest)|management
LC13|deforestation|permanent conversion of forest to non-forest land use (agriculture, urban, mining); ~10 million ha/year net loss globally (2015–2020: gross loss higher but partially offset by afforestation/reforestation); concentrated in tropics (Brazil, Indonesia, DRC); drivers: agricultural expansion (70–80%), infrastructure, logging (usually degradation not deforestation per se)|threat
LC14|fire return interval (FRI)|average time between fires at a given point on the landscape; fire regime classification: short (<35 yr: longleaf pine, ponderosa pine); moderate (35–200 yr: mixed conifer, oak); long (>200 yr: PNW Douglas-fir); FRI is species-dependent: fire-adapted ecosystems require fire at natural FRI to maintain composition and structure|ecology
LC15|landscape ecology|study of spatial patterns and processes across large areas; patches (forest stands), corridors (riparian, roads), matrix (dominant land cover); fragmentation, connectivity, edge effects; management at landscape scale (not just stand) integrates wildlife, water, fire, and timber objectives|ecology
