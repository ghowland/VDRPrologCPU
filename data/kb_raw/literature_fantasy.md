# foundations(id|concept|definition|significance)
FD1|fantasy|fiction in which impossible elements (magic, supernatural beings, violation of natural law) are essential to the narrative — not explained by science|the defining genre boundary: if removing the impossible element collapses the story, it is fantasy
FD2|secondary world|invented world with its own geography, history, cultures, and rules — not Earth|Tolkien's "sub-creation" — the author builds a complete reality; reader enters it fully
FD3|primary world fantasy|impossible elements intrude into the real world (or a version of it)|urban fantasy, contemporary fantasy, magical realism — reader's reality is the baseline
FD4|portal fantasy|character travels from primary world into secondary world|transition is the narrative engine — fish-out-of-water structure; reader discovers world alongside protagonist
FD5|immersive fantasy|story begins in secondary world with no connection to ours — reader must learn the world from context|no portal, no frame narrative — reader dropped in; worldbuilding through implication, not exposition
FD6|worldbuilding|construction of the secondary world's physical, cultural, historical, magical, and ecological systems|the fantasy author's unique burden — insufficient worldbuilding destroys believability; excessive worldbuilding buries narrative
FD7|magic system|the rules governing how impossible things work in the fiction — who can do what, at what cost, with what limits|the structural spine — determines plot constraints, character capabilities, and conflict resolution
FD8|the numinous|sense of awe, wonder, or the holy evoked by encounter with the supernatural or transcendent|what distinguishes fantasy from adventure fiction with magic — the emotional register of the impossible
FD9|eucatastrophe|sudden turn from catastrophe to unexpected good — the "good catastrophe" (Tolkien)|the characteristic fantasy ending — not deus ex machina but earned through prior sacrifice and structure
FD10|mythopoeia|deliberate creation of mythology — invented cosmogony, pantheon, legendarium underlying the narrative|Tolkien's project — the Silmarillion as mythological substrate for Lord of the Rings
FD11|secondary belief|reader's willing engagement with the internal logic of the fantasy world — not "suspension of disbelief" but active belief within the frame|Tolkien's distinction — the author earns secondary belief by internal consistency, not by asking the reader to ignore problems
FD12|the fantastic|Todorov's term — moment of hesitation where reader/character cannot decide if event is natural or supernatural|structural engine of gothic and dark fantasy — ambiguity itself is the mode
FD13|estrangement|cognitive displacement from the familiar — making the reader see the world differently through the lens of the impossible|shared with science fiction but achieved through different means — magic vs technology
FD14|enchantment|state of being under the power of the story's impossible elements — not merely witnessing but being affected by them|the reader's experience mirrors the character's — both are changed by contact with the impossible

# subgenres(id|subgenre|defining_features|setting|tone|exemplars)
SG1|high/epic fantasy|world-threatening conflict, chosen hero or fellowship, secondary world, large scope, good vs evil|secondary world (usually medieval-coded)|elevated, mythic, heroic|Tolkien (LotR), Jordan (WoT), Sanderson (Stormlight)
SG2|low fantasy|personal stakes, morally ambiguous characters, grounded concerns (survival, power, revenge), less magic|secondary or primary world|gritty, cynical, pragmatic|Abercrombie (First Law), Martin (ASoIaF), Cook (Black Company)
SG3|sword and sorcery|individual heroes, physical combat + magic, episodic adventures, amoral or roguish protagonists|secondary world (urban + wilderness)|pulpy, visceral, adventurous|Howard (Conan), Leiber (Fafhrd & Gray Mouser), Moorcock (Elric)
SG4|urban fantasy|magic in modern cities, supernatural subculture hidden within or alongside mundane society|primary world — contemporary cities|noir-inflected, street-level, identity-driven|Butcher (Dresden Files), Gaiman (Neverwhere), de Lint (Newford)
SG5|dark fantasy|horror elements, dread, transgression, moral corruption — fantasy that frightens|secondary or primary|oppressive, horrific, psychologically intense|Barker (Weaveworld), Miéville (Bas-Lag), Bakker (Second Apocalypse)
SG6|fairy tale / mythic fantasy|draws directly on fairy tale structure, archetypes, and logic — often retelling or subversion|liminal — "once upon a time" space|dreamlike, symbolic, compressed|McKinley (Beauty), Valente (Deathless), Novik (Spinning Silver)
SG7|comic/satirical fantasy|humor as primary mode — parody of genre conventions, absurdist premises, social satire through fantasy lens|secondary world (often parodic)|irreverent, witty, absurd|Pratchett (Discworld), Asprin (Myth), Adams (Hitchhiker's — borderline SF)
SG8|heroic fantasy|individual hero's journey, martial prowess, personal honor, quests — overlaps sword & sorcery but less pulpy|secondary world|earnest, adventure-driven, character-focused|Gemmell (Drenai), Eddings (Belgariad), Feist (Riftwar)
SG9|portal/isekai fantasy|protagonist transported to another world — must adapt, often discovers hidden powers or role|primary → secondary world|wonder, displacement, growth|Lewis (Narnia), Donaldson (Thomas Covenant), most LitRPG
SG10|magical realism|impossible elements presented as unremarkable within otherwise realistic fiction — no fantasy world, no system|primary world — often Latin American, post-colonial|matter-of-fact, literary, layered|García Márquez (100 Years), Allende (House of Spirits), Okri (Famished Road)
SG11|new weird|genre-blending — fantasy + horror + SF + surrealism, rejection of Tolkienian templates, emphasis on the strange|secondary world (often urban, industrial)|unsettling, intellectual, baroque|Miéville (Perdido), VanderMeer (Ambergris), Cisco (Traitor Baru)
SG12|grimdark|subset of low fantasy — extremely dark tone, graphic violence, nihilistic worldview, anti-heroic protagonists|secondary world|brutal, bleak, morally exhausting|Morgan (Steel Remains), Lawrence (Broken Empire), Bakker
SG13|romantasy|fantasy + romance as co-primary plot — romantic relationship is as structurally important as the fantasy quest|secondary world (often fae-adjacent)|passionate, emotionally heightened, dual-arc|Maas (ACOTAR), Bardugo (Shadow & Bone), Kuang (Poppy War — borderline)
SG14|LitRPG/progression|characters exist within or are aware of game-like systems — levels, stats, skills, quantified advancement|secondary world (game world or game-like)|systematic, escalatory, power-fantasy|many web serials, some published (Shirtaloon, Grossman)
SG15|dying earth|far-future setting where science has become indistinguishable from magic — world in entropic decline|far-future Earth or equivalent|melancholic, decadent, arch|Vance (Dying Earth), Wolfe (Book of the New Sun — borderline SF), Harrison (Viriconium)
SG16|mythic fiction|literary fiction drawing on mythological archetypes and structures without building systematic secondary worlds|primary or mythic space|literary, allusive, archetypal|Le Guin (Earthsea — borderline), Kay (Tigana, Fionavar), Gaiman (American Gods)
SG17|flintlock/gunpowder fantasy|secondary world at early modern tech level — firearms, cannons, industrialization alongside magic|secondary world (post-medieval)|action-driven, politically complex|Sanderson (Mistborn Era 2), McClellan (Powder Mage), Django Wexler (Shadow Campaigns)

# world_elements(id|element|description|design_function|failure_if_absent)
WE1|geography|physical terrain, climate, distances, natural resources|constrains travel time, military strategy, trade, culture zones — maps are not decoration|implausible travel times, armies appearing anywhere, cultures with no geographic basis
WE2|history|past events shaping current politics, enmities, ruins, legends, lost knowledge|provides depth without exposition — "the past presses on the present"|world feels new-minted, static, without weight or consequence
WE3|cultures|distinct peoples with languages, religions, customs, taboos, art, governance|diversity of perspective, source of conflict and alliance, reader engagement through unfamiliarity|monoculture world — one language, one religion, one cuisine across a continent
WE4|economy|trade goods, currency, labor, wealth distribution, guilds, taxation|grounds world in material reality — characters need money; wars need funding|armies march without supply, cities exist without trade, peasants are backdrop
WE5|religion/cosmology|gods, creation myths, afterlife beliefs, clergy, religious conflict, prophecy|motivates characters, justifies social structures, provides mythic resonance|gods are props, religion is costume, characters have no genuine belief
WE6|politics/governance|kingdoms, empires, councils, succession, law, diplomacy, factions, rebellion|drives macro-conflict, shapes character allegiance, provides stakes beyond personal|politics is "evil king vs good rebels" with no institutional depth
WE7|ecology|flora, fauna, food sources, seasons, natural hazards — how the world sustains life|grounds the world biologically — what do people eat? what threatens them?|characters never eat, weather never matters, wilderness is empty space between cities
WE8|technology level|materials, tools, weapons, construction, medicine, communication, transport|sets baseline for what is possible without magic — anchors reader expectations|anachronistic mixing without explanation — plate armor and cannon in same scene with no transition
WE9|language|naming conventions, loan words, untranslated terms, registers of speech, scripts|signals cultural difference, marks class/region/era, provides texture|all names from same phonetic palette, no linguistic differentiation between cultures
WE10|social structure|class, caste, gender roles, age hierarchies, slavery, citizenship, mobility|defines characters' constraints and ambitions — what is possible for whom|modern egalitarian values in medieval setting without acknowledgment or justification
WE11|calendar/timekeeping|days, months, years, seasons, festivals, ages, reckonings|marks passage of time, provides rhythm, connects to cosmology|time passes without markers — reader cannot track chronology

# magic_systems(id|type|description|narrative_function|exemplars)
MS1|hard magic|explicit rules, costs, limitations — reader understands the system|conflict resolution is problem-solving — reader can anticipate and judge solutions|Sanderson (Allomancy, Stormlight), Rothfuss (sympathy), Weeks (chromaturgy)
MS2|soft magic|undefined or mysterious rules — reader does not fully understand how it works|creates wonder, dread, awe — magic is numinous, not engineering|Tolkien (Gandalf), Le Guin (Earthsea — semi-soft), Martin (Others, dragons)
MS3|hybrid magic|some elements hard (combat magic, daily use), others soft (ancient powers, divine intervention)|allows both problem-solving and wonder in same world — different scales of magic|Jordan (channeling = hard; Pattern/Dark One = soft), Hobb (Skill = semi-hard; Wit = soft)
MS4|cost-based|magic requires sacrifice — physical, emotional, material, temporal, moral|every use carries weight — prevents "why didn't the wizard just..." problems|blood magic (many), Sanderson (metals consumed), Le Guin (equilibrium), Rothfuss (caloric energy)
MS5|talent-based|magic is innate ability — born with it or not — training develops but doesn't grant|creates social stratification (mages vs mundanes), personal identity tied to power|Jordan (channelers), Rowling (wizards), Hobb (Skill/Wit talent)
MS6|learned/scholarly|magic is knowledge — anyone with training/study can use it — libraries, academies, grimoires|magic as technology analog — access to education determines power|Rothfuss (University), Novik (Scholomance), D&D Vancian (spellbooks)
MS7|divine/granted|power comes from gods, spirits, or external entities — may be withdrawn|creates dependency, moral obligation, crisis when faith falters or patron betrays|clerical magic (D&D), Erikson (warrens — partially), Bakker (Psûke, Gnosis — partially)
MS8|ritual/ceremonial|magic requires elaborate preparation — ingredients, timing, incantation, geometry|pacing tool — magic cannot be used in combat improvisation; preparation IS the tension|Le Guin (true names — partially), much folk-magic-based fantasy, ceremonial magic traditions
MS9|wild/chaotic|magic is unpredictable, dangerous, entropic — using it risks catastrophe|source of horror and tension — magic is not a tool but a force barely contained|Miéville (thaumaturgy), Moorcock (Chaos), Bakker (Tekne), Wolfe (New Sun — partially)
MS10|systematic progression|power increases through defined advancement — levels, tiers, stages, breakthroughs|the engine of progression fantasy — reader anticipates next milestone|LitRPG systems, xianxia (cultivation), Sanderson (Surgebinding oaths)

# creatures(id|creature|origin|role_in_narrative|notable_treatments)
CR1|dragon|Indo-European and Chinese mythology — serpentine, winged, fire-breathing (Western) or benevolent/elemental (Eastern)|ultimate monster or ultimate ally — the most powerful non-divine being; hoard-guardian, world-ender, mount, wise elder|Tolkien (Smaug — malevolent, speaking), Le Guin (Earthsea — wise), McCaffrey (Pern — bonded), Martin (Dany's dragons — weapon)
CR2|elf|Norse mythology (álfar) via Tolkien — immortal, beautiful, ancient, magical, declining|embody the numinous and the melancholic — immortality as both gift and curse; the world's memory|Tolkien (canonical treatment), Pratchett (subverted), Miéville (absent — deliberate rejection)
CR3|dwarf|Norse mythology (dvergar) via Tolkien — underground, craftsmanship, mining, martial, stubborn|embody endurance, material culture, resistance to magic — counterpoint to elves|Tolkien (canonical), Pratchett (satirized), Sanderson (absent — creates own species instead)
CR4|orc/goblin|Tolkien's invention (drawing on Old English/folklore) — corrupted, mass-produced soldiers of evil|the disposable enemy — raises moral questions when authors grant them interiority|Tolkien (corrupted elves — debated), Abercrombie (deconstructed), many games (standard enemy)
CR5|undead|universal folklore — corpses animated by magic, curse, or will — vampires, zombies, liches, ghosts|death is not final — horror element; necromancy as moral transgression; ghost as unfinished business|Martin (Others/wights), Tolkien (Barrow-wights, Dead Men of Dunharrow), Abercrombie (Eaters)
CR6|fae/faerie|Celtic and European folklore — capricious, beautiful, dangerous, operating by alien logic|the truly alien in fantasy — not evil, not good, but Other; bargains, glamour, stolen children|Gaiman (Stardust), Clarke (Jonathan Strange), Beagle (Last Unicorn), Bardugo (Grisha — loosely)
CR7|shapeshifter|universal folklore — lycanthropes, skinwalkers, selkies, pooka|identity instability — what are you when your form is not fixed? social metaphor for hidden selves|werewolf tradition, Hobb (Wit-bonded), Le Guin (Earthsea — hawk transformation as temptation)
CR8|demon/devil|Abrahamic and cross-cultural — malevolent supernatural entities, tempters, corruptors, invaders|externalized evil, moral testing, summoning as Faustian bargain|Erikson (Jaghut/Forkrul — subverted), Friedman (Coldfire), Barker (Cenobites — horror-fantasy)
CR9|familiar/companion animal|folklore + fantasy convention — magical animal bonded to protagonist|emotional anchor, deus ex machina risk, proxy for reader's affection|Pullman (daemons — soul manifestation), McCaffrey (fire-lizards), Hobb (Wit-bond)
CR10|golem/construct|Jewish folklore (golem), broader automaton tradition — artificial being animated by magic or inscription|labor, servitude, free will questions — "what is alive?"|Pratchett (Feet of Clay — civil rights), Miéville (constructs), Sanderson (lifeless)
CR11|giant|Norse, Greek, widespread mythology — beings of immense size, often primordial|embodied landscape, primal force, the world before civilization|Tolkien (Ents — tree-giants), Martin (giants beyond the Wall), Norse mythology (jötnar)
CR12|phoenix/rebirth creature|Egyptian, Greek, Chinese — dies in fire, reborn from ashes|cyclical renewal, sacrifice and resurrection, hope|Rowling (Fawkes), widespread symbolic use
CR13|kraken/leviathan|Norse, Biblical — sea monster of impossible size|the deep as unknown and terrifying — ocean as unconquered frontier|Miéville (The Scar), Erikson (ocean-based sequences), Lovecraftian influence
CR14|trickster|universal folklore — Loki, Anansi, Coyote, Puck — clever, amoral, rule-breaking, transformative|narrative catalyst — breaks stasis, reveals hypocrisy, creates chaos from which new order emerges|Gaiman (Anansi Boys, American Gods — Loki/Low Key), Pratchett (various)

# character_archetypes(id|archetype|description|narrative_function|subversion_examples)
CA1|chosen one|prophecy or destiny marks protagonist for world-saving role|provides motivation, raises stakes, creates tension between agency and fate|Donaldson (Thomas Covenant — refuses the role), Abercrombie (Logen — "chosen" is meaningless), Bakker (Kellhus — manipulates prophecy)
CA2|mentor|older, wiser figure who guides protagonist — often dies or departs|transfers knowledge, raises stakes through sacrifice, forces protagonist to mature|Tolkien (Gandalf), Le Guin (Ogion), Jordan (Moiraine), Pratchett (Death — inverted)
CA3|dark lord|supreme evil embodied in a single antagonist — wants dominion, destruction, or corruption|provides clear opposition, unifying threat, moral clarity|Tolkien (Sauron — definitive), Jordan (Dark One), Donaldson (Lord Foul), Martin (absent — deliberate)
CA4|rogue/trickster hero|morally ambiguous protagonist — thief, con artist, assassin, mercenary with hidden code|provides pragmatic counterpoint to idealism, enables moral complexity, reader enjoys competence|Leiber (Gray Mouser), Lynch (Locke Lamora), Abercrombie (Glokta), Hobb (Fitz — partially)
CA5|reluctant hero|protagonist who does not want the quest — dragged by circumstance, duty, or guilt|reader identification through ordinary-person response; heroism earned through unwilling sacrifice|Tolkien (Frodo, Bilbo), Donaldson (Covenant), Hobb (Fitz), Pratchett (Rincewind)
CA6|wise woman/witch|female figure with knowledge, power, and outsider status — healer, seer, hedge-witch|challenges patriarchal power structures, provides alternative knowledge system, often marginalized|Le Guin (Tenar, Tehanu), Pratchett (Granny Weatherwax), Hobb (Kettle)
CA7|knight/paladin|warrior bound by code — honor, duty, protection of the weak, service to a cause|embodied virtue under pressure — what happens when the code conflicts with reality|Martin (Jaime Lannister — code broken and rebuilt), Sanderson (Kaladin — oath-bound), Tolkien (Aragorn)
CA8|scholar/mage|character whose power comes from knowledge — academy-trained, book-learned, physically weak|magic as intellectual discipline, knowledge as double-edged power, hubris of the learned|Rothfuss (Kvothe), Le Guin (Ged), Novik (El/Scholomance), Wolfe (Severian — unreliable)
CA9|companion/fellowship|group of diverse characters with complementary skills undertaking shared quest|provides multiple viewpoints, internal conflict alongside external, emotional bonds that raise stakes|Tolkien (Fellowship — definitive), Jordan (main cast), Sanderson (Bridge Four)
CA10|fallen/corrupted figure|once-good character turned by power, despair, or manipulation|embodies theme that good can become evil — tragedy, warning, redemption possibility|Tolkien (Saruman, Boromir — partial), Martin (many), Jordan (Forsaken — former heroes)
CA11|monster-hunter|specialist who tracks and kills supernatural threats — often liminal figure between human and monster|embodies pragmatic relationship with the fantastic — knowledge of the enemy requires proximity to it|Sapkowski (Geralt/Witcher), Butcher (Dresden — partially), Abercrombie (Logen — partially)
CA12|heir in hiding|rightful ruler raised in obscurity, unaware of true identity until revelation|provides personal identity crisis alongside political restoration arc|Tolkien (Aragorn), Jordan (Rand), Eddings (Garion), Martin (Jon Snow — partially)

# narrative_structures(id|structure|description|characteristic_of|examples)
NS1|quest|protagonist must travel to a destination or retrieve an object — journey IS the story|high/epic fantasy, portal fantasy|Tolkien (destroy Ring), Jordan (find/use sa'angreal), Eddings (retrieve Orb)
NS2|coming of age|protagonist matures from youth to adulthood through trials — gains power, wisdom, responsibility|most fantasy involves maturation; explicit in many|Le Guin (Ged in Wizard of Earthsea), Hobb (Fitz trilogy), Rothfuss (Kvothe)
NS3|war/siege|large-scale military conflict with battles, strategy, logistics, and consequences|epic fantasy, military fantasy|Tolkien (Helm's Deep, Pelennor), Erikson (Siege of Capustan), Martin (Battle of Blackwater)
NS4|political intrigue|power struggles, succession crises, espionage, betrayal — the throne is the prize|court fantasy, low fantasy|Martin (definitive modern example), Kay (Tigana, Lions of Al-Rassan), Jemisin (Broken Earth — partially)
NS5|mystery/investigation|protagonist must uncover hidden truth — who, why, how — using both mundane and magical means|urban fantasy, some epic (secrets of the world)|Butcher (Dresden — detective), Miéville (City & the City), Sanderson (Mistborn — "what happened 1000 years ago")
NS6|heist|team assembles to steal something valuable using elaborate plan — complications ensue|rogue fantasy, secondary world crime|Lynch (Lies of Locke Lamora), Sanderson (Mistborn — partially), Weeks (Night Angel — partially)
NS7|tragedy|protagonist's virtues or choices lead inevitably to downfall — awareness comes too late|dark fantasy, literary fantasy|Bakker (Second Apocalypse), Martin (Ned Stark, Red Wedding), Donaldson (Thomas Covenant — ambiguous)
NS8|eucatastrophic turn|darkest moment gives way to unexpected salvation — not arbitrary but structurally earned|high fantasy — Tolkien's signature|Tolkien (eagles, Gollum's fall), Lewis (Aslan's return), Sanderson (Sanderlanche — structural climax)
NS9|chronicle/saga|multi-generational story spanning decades or centuries — world changes across the narrative|epic fantasy, family saga|Erikson (Malazan — millennia), Jordan (WoT — shorter), Martin (ASoIaF)
NS10|frame narrative|story within a story — narrator recounts past events, reliability may be questioned|fairy tale fantasy, literary fantasy|Rothfuss (Kvothe narrating), Wolfe (Severian — unreliable), Morgenstern (Night Circus)
NS11|chosen one's refusal|protagonist rejects destiny, subverting the expected arc — finds alternative path or fails entirely|subversive fantasy, deconstruction|Donaldson (Covenant refuses to believe), Abercrombie (First Law — prophecy is a lie), Pullman (Lyra — partially)
NS12|Sanderlanche|escalating convergence of multiple plot threads into a single climactic sequence — term coined by fandom for Sanderson's signature technique|epic fantasy, complex multi-POV|Sanderson (every Stormlight book), Jordan/Sanderson (WoT climax), Erikson (convergences)

# themes(id|theme|description|how_fantasy_explores_it|exemplars)
TM1|power and corruption|does power inevitably corrupt? can the powerful serve justly?|the Ring, the Throne, the magic system as power metaphor — fantasy literalizes the abstraction|Tolkien (Ring), Martin (Iron Throne), Le Guin (Earthsea — power as responsibility)
TM2|good vs evil|is morality absolute or contingent? do good and evil exist as cosmic forces?|high fantasy tends toward absolute; low fantasy toward contingent; both illuminate by contrast|Tolkien (absolute), Martin (contingent), Abercrombie (deconstructed), Donaldson (internalized)
TM3|death and immortality|what does it mean to die? is immortality blessing or curse?|elves and vampires explore immortality; necromancy explores death's boundary; afterlife worldbuilding|Tolkien (mortality as Gift of Men), Erikson (Hood, god of death), Pratchett (Death as character)
TM4|identity and transformation|who are you when you can change shape, lose memory, gain power, or discover hidden heritage?|shapeshifting, amnesia, revealed parentage, power acquisition all literalize identity questions|Hobb (Fitz — identity through bond), Le Guin (Earthsea — true names), Wolfe (Severian — unreliable self)
TM5|duty vs freedom|must you serve the prophecy, the crown, the code? or can you choose your own path?|chosen ones who rebel, knights who break oaths, mages who refuse their role|Jordan (Rand resisting destiny), Martin (Jaime breaking oath), Sanderson (Kaladin's oaths — chosen duty)
TM6|loss and nostalgia|the world is diminishing — the golden age is past, magic is fading, the old ways are dying|Tolkien's dominant mode — the world of LotR is a world of loss; elvish departure is entropy|Tolkien (definitive), Kay (Tigana — erased memory), Beagle (Last Unicorn)
TM7|colonialism and empire|conquest, cultural erasure, resistance, hybrid identity|post-colonial fantasy examines empire from below — who conquers, who is erased, who resists|Jemisin (Broken Earth), Kuang (Poppy War), Kay (Lions of Al-Rassan), Erikson (Lether)
TM8|nature and civilization|wildness vs order, forest vs city, the green vs the machine|environmental fantasy — the ancient forest threatened by industry; druids vs developers|Tolkien (Shire vs Mordor/Saruman), Le Guin (Earthsea ecology), Hobb (Wit as nature connection)
TM9|knowledge and hubris|the danger of knowing too much, seeking forbidden lore, overreaching through intellect|mage-scholars who break the world, forbidden books, the price of omniscience|Rothfuss (Kvothe), Le Guin (Ged releasing shadow), Bakker (Mandate — knowing the past is torment)
TM10|redemption|can the fallen be redeemed? what is the cost? is redemption earned or granted?|fallen knights, dark lords' lieutenants, criminals seeking absolution|Martin (Jaime, Theon), Sanderson (Dalinar), Tolkien (Gollum — partial, tragic)
TM11|sacrifice|what must be given up to save the world, the people, the beloved?|the structural engine of eucatastrophe — salvation requires someone to lose something precious|Tolkien (Frodo's Shire — can't go home), Lewis (Aslan), Erikson (Itkovian), Pullman (Lyra/Will)
TM12|language and naming|words have power — true names control, lies reshape reality, stories create truth|magic systems built on language, naming as power, unreliable narration|Le Guin (true names), Rothfuss (Naming), Barker (the word as reality), Pratchett (narrative causality)

# tropes(id|trope|description|function|subversion)
TR1|the dark lord's tower|evil has a visible, geographic center — a fortress, a mountain, a throne|gives evil a location and therefore a target — quest has a destination|Abercrombie (the tower is real but what's inside is not what you expect), Erikson (evil is distributed, not centralized)
TR2|the ancient prophecy|foretold events constrain or guide the plot — protagonist is "the one"|provides structure and raises stakes — fate vs free will tension|Jordan (prophecy is real and terrible), Abercrombie (prophecy is propaganda), Bakker (prophecy is a trap)
TR3|the magical artifact|object of great power — ring, sword, stone, crown — must be found, used, or destroyed|externalizes power and temptation — who controls the artifact controls the story|Tolkien (One Ring — definitive), Moorcock (Stormbringer — the artifact is evil), Sanderson (Shardblades — technology)
TR4|the hidden heir|true king raised in ignorance, discovered through birthmark, sword, prophecy|restoration narrative — rightful order overturned, now restored|Martin (R+L=J — subverted, heir doesn't want throne), Tolkien (Aragorn — played straight)
TR5|the fellowship breaks|united group fractures under pressure — members die, betray, or separate|raises stakes, creates multiple plotlines, tests individual character without group support|Tolkien (Breaking of the Fellowship — definitive), every epic fantasy that splits its cast
TR6|the mentor's sacrifice|wise guide dies or departs, forcing protagonist to act independently|removes the safety net — protagonist must become the hero|Tolkien (Gandalf's fall), Jordan (Moiraine's disappearance), Rowling (Dumbledore)
TR7|magic has a price|every use of power costs something — health, sanity, life force, moral standing|prevents deus ex machina, makes magic dramatic rather than convenient|Sanderson (formalized), Le Guin (equilibrium), Hobb (Skill/Wit — physical/mental cost)
TR8|the unmaking/apocalypse|world-ending threat that must be averted — darkness rising, seal breaking, dead god waking|ultimate stakes — provides urgency and scope; failure = everything ends|Tolkien (Sauron's return), Jordan (Dark One breaking free), Erikson (Crippled God)
TR9|the wise fool|character who appears foolish but speaks truth — court jester, village simpleton, the drunk seer|delivers uncomfortable truths through comedy or naivety — truth from unexpected source|Pratchett (many — Rincewind, the Fool), Martin (Dontos, Moon Boy), Shakespeare influence
TR10|maps in the front|secondary world fantasy traditionally includes maps — reader orients spatially|worldbuilding shorthand — implies geographic coherence, invites exploration|expected in epic fantasy; absence is a statement (Miéville — no maps for Bas-Lag initially)
TR11|constructed languages|author creates one or more languages for the secondary world|deepest level of worldbuilding — demonstrates cultural depth, provides naming consistency|Tolkien (definitive — Quenya, Sindarin), most authors use naming conventions rather than full conlangs
TR12|the training montage|protagonist learns magic/combat through extended study sequence|provides competence believably, shows system mechanics, paces power acquisition|Rothfuss (University), Novik (Scholomance), Sanderson (Bridge Four training)
TR13|the true name|knowing something's true name grants power over it — naming as magic|makes language literally powerful — words are not arbitrary but essential|Le Guin (Earthsea — foundational), Rothfuss (Naming discipline), Barker, folk traditions

# conventions(id|convention|description|when_effective|when_harmful)
CV1|medieval European setting|majority of secondary-world fantasy uses Western European Middle Ages as cultural template|familiar, provides ready-made social structures, rich existing imagery|exclusionary, monotonous, historically inaccurate ("Ye Olde Europe" never existed as depicted)
CV2|feudal governance|kings, lords, peasants, knights — feudal hierarchy as default political system|provides clear power structures and social conflict|oversimplifies actual medieval governance, ignores non-European systems
CV3|Tolkienian species|elves, dwarves, orcs, hobbits as default species palette|instant reader recognition, built-in cultural associations|reduces creative possibility, creates expectation of rehash
CV4|quest structure|go there, get/destroy thing, come back|clear narrative drive, spatial storytelling, journey as transformation|formulaic if not complicated; MacGuffin risk if the object doesn't matter
CV5|trilogy format|three-volume structure as default publication format|allows rising action, development, and resolution across adequate space|middle-book syndrome (Book 2 is all setup), artificial cliffhangers, padding
CV6|multiple POV|rotating viewpoint characters, often chapter-length|shows world from many angles, enables dramatic irony, builds ensemble|reader attachment diluted, pacing disrupted by unwanted POV, exponential complexity
CV7|invented words without context|character orders a "flagon of kaeth" without explanation|immersive — reader infers from context, world feels lived-in|alienating if too dense or if context is insufficient for inference
CV8|the returning king|rightful ruler restored to throne as resolution|satisfying closure, order restored, justice served|conservative politics implicit — hereditary power validated, revolution delegitimized
CV9|magic as secret|magical world hidden from mundane awareness — masquerade, hidden society|enables primary-world fantasy, creates dramatic irony, explains why magic isn't known|increasingly implausible with modern technology — surveillance, internet, forensics
CV10|good races / evil races|entire species are inherently good (elves) or evil (orcs)|simplifies moral landscape, enables guilt-free violence against "evil" species|morally troubling — biological determinism, coded racism, denies individual agency

# conflicts(id|conflict_type|description|examples|resolution_patterns)
CF1|good vs evil (cosmic)|metaphysical opposition between absolute moral poles — often embodied in dark lord vs chosen one|Tolkien (Sauron vs Free Peoples), Jordan (Dark One vs Dragon Reborn), Lewis (Tash vs Aslan)|eucatastrophe, sacrifice, destruction of evil's anchor
CF2|succession/throne|who should rule? — competing claimants, usurpers, regents, civil war|Martin (War of the Five Kings), Kay (Tigana), Hobb (Six Duchies succession)|political resolution — marriage, battle, revelation of true heir, democratic reform (rare)
CF3|civilization vs wilderness|expansion of civilization threatens wild places and ancient powers — or vice versa|Tolkien (Shire/Ents vs Saruman's industry), Hobb (Wit vs civilization), Le Guin (balance)|negotiated coexistence, one side destroyed, transcendence of the opposition
CF4|freedom vs destiny|protagonist struggles against prophecy, bloodline duty, or divine plan|Jordan (Rand vs Pattern), Donaldson (Covenant vs role of hero), Bakker (Kellhus vs No-God)|acceptance of destiny transformed by personal choice, or successful rebellion against fate
CF5|knowledge vs ignorance|forbidden knowledge must be sought or suppressed — truth is dangerous|Rothfuss (the Chandrian), Bakker (the Consult), Le Guin (Ged's shadow)|knowledge gained at terrible cost, willful ignorance shown as worse
CF6|empire vs resistance|colonial power oppresses subjugated people — resistance is the story|Jemisin (Broken Earth), Kuang (Poppy War), Erikson (Letherii vs Tiste Edur)|revolution (successful or failed), negotiated autonomy, empire collapses from within
CF7|internal/psychological|protagonist battles their own nature — addiction, madness, corruption, despair|Hobb (Fitz — depression, isolation), Donaldson (Covenant — self-loathing, leprosy), Martin (Cersei — narcissism)|self-mastery, redemption, tragic failure, acceptance
CF8|human vs monster|survival against supernatural threat — dragon, demon, undead horde, elder god|Howard (Conan), Sapkowski (Witcher), Erikson (K'Chain Che'Malle)|martial victory, magical binding, sacrifice, understanding (monster is not what it seems)
CF9|magical vs mundane|tension between magic-users and non-magic population — fear, persecution, privilege|Sanderson (Mistborn — nobles have allomancy), Weeks (Lightbringer), Novik (Scholomance)|integration, segregation, revolution, magic system democratized or destroyed
CF10|divine vs mortal|gods meddle in mortal affairs, or mortals challenge divine authority|Erikson (definitive — gods as characters), Pratchett (Small Gods), Sanderson (Shards)|gods constrained, mortals ascend, divine order reformed, gods killed

# prose_craft(id|technique|description|function_in_fantasy|pitfall)
PC1|show don't tell (worldbuilding)|reveal world through character interaction, not exposition dumps|reader discovers world naturally — immersion maintained|insufficient showing leaves reader confused; too much leaves reader lost
PC2|iceberg principle|world is larger than what's shown — history, geography, magic implied but not fully explained|creates depth and believability — reader senses more exists|too little iceberg = shallow world; too much hidden = frustrating withholding
PC3|defamiliarization|describe familiar things as if unfamiliar — bread becomes "grain-cake," a horse becomes unfamiliar|places reader inside the secondary world's perspective — nothing is taken for granted|overdone = pretentious; inconsistent = breaks immersion
PC4|naming as characterization|names carry meaning — phonetic, etymological, cultural — character names signal origin, class, culture|names ARE worldbuilding; they signal before any description|lazy naming (Bob the Elf), unpronounceable naming (Xkr'thn'gol), inconsistent naming conventions
PC5|pacing through structure|alternating POVs, chapter length, cliffhangers, quiet moments between action|manages reader energy — rest between intense sequences, tension through delayed resolution|all climax = exhausting; all setup = boring; Sanderlanche works because preceding books are slow
PC6|dialogue as world-window|characters speak in registers, dialects, idioms that reveal social position and cultural background|every conversation builds world without narration|uniform speech across all characters, modern slang in medieval setting
PC7|unreliable narration|narrator may deceive, misunderstand, or be limited — reader must read between lines|adds depth, rewards re-reading, creates dramatic irony|frustrating if reader cannot access truth by any means; manipulative if unreliability is arbitrary
PC8|foreshadowing / prophecy|hints planted early that pay off later — prophecy is fantasy's unique foreshadowing tool|creates structural satisfaction — the reader sees the pattern retroactively|too obvious = spoiler; too obscure = reader misses it; unfulfilled foreshadowing = broken promise

# rules(id|rule|domain|rationale|violation_consequence)
RL1|internal consistency trumps all other worldbuilding virtues|worldbuilding|the reader will believe the impossible if it follows its own rules — inconsistency destroys secondary belief instantly|reader disengagement — "but earlier you said..." breaks the spell; the author cannot recover trust
RL2|magic must have costs or limitations to generate dramatic tension|magic|unlimited magic eliminates conflict — if the wizard can solve everything, there is no story|deus ex machina, tension collapse, "why didn't the wizard just..." syndrome
RL3|the world must exist beyond the protagonist's awareness|worldbuilding|characters in a living world encounter evidence of events they weren't involved in — other people have lives|protagonist-centric world feels like a stage set — NPCs waiting for the hero to arrive
RL4|consequences must follow from established rules|narrative|if the magic system says fire magic requires heat source, the mage cannot conjure fire in a vacuum without explanation|reader loses trust in the system — stakes become meaningless if rules bend for plot convenience
RL5|exposition must be dramatized, not delivered|prose|information conveyed through conflict, dialogue, and action is absorbed; information delivered as lecture is endured|"as you know, Bob" syndrome — characters explaining things they both know for the reader's benefit
RL6|names must be internally consistent within cultures|worldbuilding|a culture that names children Aragorn, Legolas, and Bob has broken its own linguistic logic|immersion rupture — reader is reminded they are reading fiction rather than experiencing a world
RL7|the map must match the narrative|worldbuilding|distances, travel times, and geographic features in the text must be consistent with any provided map|characters teleporting across continents, armies appearing without marching, rivers flowing uphill
RL8|a subgenre sets expectations — violating them without signaling is a betrayal|genre|a book marketed as epic fantasy that becomes a romance (or vice versa) without earning the shift loses its audience|reader betrayal — the implicit contract between genre label and content is broken
RL9|soft magic cannot solve hard problems — hard magic cannot evoke wonder|magic|Sanderson's law — the more the reader understands the magic, the more it can resolve plot; the less understood, the more it creates atmosphere|using soft magic to solve specific plot problems = deus ex machina; using hard magic for awe = engineering report
RL10|every sentient species must have culture, not just combat stats|worldbuilding|if orcs have language and social structure, they have art, religion, cuisine, disagreement — or they are not sentient, merely biological weapons|morally bankrupt worldbuilding — genocide of sentient beings treated as heroic because the author denied them interiority

# failure_modes(id|mode|cause|symptom|prevention)
FM1|worldbuilding dump|author built a world and wants you to know all of it — exposition replaces narrative|chapters of history, geography, magic theory with no character or conflict|iceberg principle (PC2), dramatize exposition (RL5), trust the reader
FM2|Chosen One passivity|protagonist is acted upon by prophecy — things happen TO them, they make no meaningful choices|reader bored by protagonist, supporting cast more interesting, deus ex machina endings|give chosen one agency within the prophecy — choices matter even if destiny exists
FM3|magic without cost|magic solves problems without sacrifice, cost, or limitation|no tension in magical scenes, reader stops worrying, "why didn't they just..." complaints|Sanderson's first law (RL9), establish costs early, enforce them consistently
FM4|evil race problem|entire species defined as irredeemably evil — heroes kill them without moral weight|uncomfortable implications, shallow worldbuilding, missed dramatic opportunity|give "evil" species interiority (RL10), or acknowledge them as constructs/weapons, not people
FM5|middle book syndrome|second volume of trilogy is pure setup — no self-contained arc, ends on cliffhanger|reader frustrated, feels manipulated, may not continue to book 3|each volume must have its own dramatic arc even within a larger story
FM6|power creep without stakes|protagonist becomes increasingly powerful but threats scale to match — no real danger|numbing repetition, each fight is the same at higher power level, reader stops caring about combat|tie power to cost (RL2), make power create new problems, not just bigger fights
FM7|Ye Olde Speeche|characters speak in pseudo-archaic English — "thee," "thou," "forsooth" — without linguistic consistency|sounds ridiculous rather than atmospheric, inconsistent with characters' actual communication needs|study actual historical registers, or use modern English with controlled vocabulary and diction
FM8|travelogue fantasy|long sequences of traveling between plot events — walking, camping, describing landscape|pacing death — reader skims, tension evaporates, nothing happens between set pieces|make travel scenes do character work, introduce complications, or skip with time jump
FM9|prophecy as spoiler|prophecy is so specific that the reader knows exactly what will happen — removes all surprise|no tension — reader is just waiting for the foretold events to occur|make prophecy ambiguous, metaphorical, or misleading; let fulfillment surprise
FM10|theme as sermon|author's message overwhelms the story — characters become mouthpieces for ideology|reader feels lectured, characters flatten into symbols, narrative collapses under didactic weight|theme must emerge from character and situation, not from authorial commentary; show, don't preach
FM11|Sanderlanche without setup|author attempts convergent climax without adequate foreshadowing and parallel development|climax feels chaotic, unearned, confusing — reader cannot track all threads|careful structural preparation — each thread must have independent development before convergence
FM12|grimdark exhaustion|unrelenting darkness without contrast, hope, humor, or humanity|reader becomes numb — atrocity loses impact, cynicism becomes boring|darkness requires light for contrast — the rare moment of kindness is devastating only against a dark background
