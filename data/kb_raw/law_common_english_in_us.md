# domains(id|name|definition)
DM1|negotiation theory|systematic study of how parties reach agreement; distributive (fixed-pie, win-lose) vs integrative (expanding pie, win-win); BATNA-based analysis; game theory applications; behavioral economics influences
DM2|contract law|body of law governing formation, performance, interpretation, and enforcement of voluntary agreements; common law (US, UK, Australia) and civil law (continental Europe, most of world) traditions; UCC (goods) and common law (services, real estate) in US
DM3|commercial contracts|agreements governing business transactions: sale of goods, services, licensing, distribution, supply chain, joint ventures; governed by UCC Article 2 (goods) or common law (services) in US; CISG internationally
DM4|employment law|agreements governing employment relationship: compensation, benefits, duties, termination, restrictive covenants; governed by common law + statutory overlay (FLSA, Title VII, ADA, FMLA, WARN, NLRA in US)
DM5|real estate contracts|agreements for sale, lease, or development of real property; governed by state law; Statute of Frauds requires writing; title, encumbrances, zoning, environmental
DM6|intellectual property|agreements governing creation, ownership, licensing, and transfer of patents, copyrights, trademarks, trade secrets; governed by federal law (US: Patent Act, Copyright Act, Lanham Act) + contract law
DM7|construction contracts|agreements for design and construction of buildings and infrastructure; unique: progress payments, change orders, liens, bonding, retainage, substantial completion; AIA, ConsensusDocs, EJCDC standard forms
DM8|international transactions|cross-border agreements: choice of law, choice of forum, currency, CISG, Incoterms, foreign corrupt practices, export controls, sanctions, arbitration (ICC, LCIA, SIAC)
DM9|dispute resolution|mechanisms for resolving contract disputes: negotiation, mediation, arbitration, litigation; each has different cost, speed, confidentiality, enforceability, and precedential value

# negotiation_phases(id|name|sequence|activities|objectives|common_mistakes|outputs)
NP1|preparation|1 (before negotiation begins)|research counterparty (interests, constraints, BATNA, authority, reputation, financial position); define own interests (not just positions); calculate BATNA; determine reservation price (walk-away point); set aspiration point (best realistic outcome); identify ZOPA (zone of possible agreement); prepare value-creation options; assemble team; set agenda|understand own and counterparty's interests deeply; know your BATNA precisely (most important single preparation element); develop multiple proposals (not one take-it-or-leave-it); plan concession strategy|insufficient BATNA analysis (→ accept bad deals or reject good ones); confusing positions with interests; failing to research counterparty; preparing only one proposal; not knowing own authority limits|BATNA assessment; interest inventory; reservation price; target price; concession plan; opening offer strategy; agenda; team roles
NP2|opening|2|establish rapport; set tone (collaborative or competitive based on strategy); frame the negotiation (define issues, scope, timeline); exchange initial positions or interests; make or receive opening offers; establish procedural ground rules|frame favorably (anchoring); signal collaborative intent if integrative approach; gather information about counterparty's priorities; avoid early concessions without reciprocity|opening too aggressively (damages relationship); opening too softly (sets low anchor); revealing BATNA or reservation price; accepting first offer without exploration; skipping rapport|opening offers; framing; agenda agreement; ground rules; initial information exchange
NP3|exploration and value creation|3|probe interests behind positions (why do they want X?); share information selectively and reciprocally; brainstorm options; identify tradeoffs (items valued differently by each party); logroll (trade concessions on different issues); expand the pie before dividing it|discover interests; identify asymmetric valuations (you value X more than they do; they value Y more than you do → trade X for Y: both gain); build trust through reciprocal information sharing|premature commitment to positions; failing to explore underlying interests; competitive behavior that shuts down information sharing; treating every issue as zero-sum; failure to listen; making unilateral concessions|interest map; option list; potential trades; value-creation opportunities; information gained about counterparty priorities
NP4|bargaining and concession exchange|4|make and receive proposals; exchange concessions; package issues (multi-issue proposals better than single-issue: enables tradeoffs); use objective criteria (market data, precedent, cost analysis, industry standards) to justify positions; manage pace|converge toward agreement within ZOPA; each concession should be reciprocated; concessions should decrease in size over time (signals approaching limit); package issues to enable integrative trades|unreciprocated concessions (pattern of giving without getting); splitting the difference prematurely (anchoring: whoever splits from a more extreme position wins); conceding on principle without getting value in return; time pressure causing capitulation|narrowed range on each issue; tentative packages; conditional agreements (if-then)
NP5|closing|5|finalize terms; capture agreement in writing (term sheet, MOU, heads of terms, or full contract); confirm mutual understanding of all terms; address implementation details (timeline, responsibilities, milestones); plan for contingencies; sign|lock in gains; ensure no misunderstanding; establish enforcement mechanism; create relationship foundation for performance phase|leaving ambiguous terms (→ disputes); verbal-only agreement (Statute of Frauds; memory diverges); missing implementation details; failing to address what-ifs (contingency clauses); winner's curse (if they accepted immediately, you offered too much)|signed agreement; term sheet or full contract; implementation plan; relationship framework; contingency provisions
NP6|implementation and relationship management|6 (post-agreement)|perform obligations; monitor compliance; manage change orders and amendments; maintain relationship; address disputes early (escalation ladder); renegotiate if circumstances change materially; document everything|successful performance; relationship preservation; value realization; early identification and resolution of problems|ignoring the contract after signing (contract is living document); failure to document changes; allowing small violations to accumulate (precedent erosion); adversarial stance when problems arise (collaborative problem-solving is better)|performance; documented changes; relationship health; dispute avoidance; lessons learned for future negotiations

# negotiation_strategies(id|name|type|approach|when_to_use|advantages|disadvantages|examples)
NS1|distributive (positional)|competitive|fixed-pie assumption; anchoring high/low; concede slowly; use pressure tactics; claim value; winner takes more of existing value|one-time transactions; no ongoing relationship; single-issue negotiations (price only); counterparty is purely competitive; time pressure favors you|simple; maximizes individual gain in zero-sum situations; effective against unprepared opponents|damages relationships; leaves value on the table (misses integrative gains); escalation risk; impasse if both sides extreme|used car negotiation; one-time supplier deal; commodity purchasing; lawsuit settlement
NS2|integrative (interest-based)|collaborative|identify interests behind positions; share information; brainstorm options; expand the pie; create value before claiming it; use objective criteria; separate people from problem (Fisher & Ury)|ongoing relationships; multiple issues; complex negotiations; parties have different priorities (asymmetric valuation enables trade); creative solutions possible|creates more total value; preserves/builds relationships; more stable agreements (both sides satisfied); reduces impasse|requires trust and information sharing (vulnerable to exploitation by competitive counterparty); slower; more complex; requires skill|business partnerships; labor agreements; multi-issue commercial deals; international diplomacy
NS3|principled negotiation (Getting to Yes)|hybrid|four principles: separate people from problem; focus on interests not positions; generate options for mutual gain; insist on objective criteria; use BATNA as power source|default strategy for most negotiations; particularly when relationship matters and issues are complex; when you want to be both firm and fair|systematic framework; avoids positional bargaining; resilient to dirty tricks; preserves relationship; produces wise agreements|counterparty may interpret openness as weakness if they are purely distributive; requires discipline to not revert to positional|most professional and institutional negotiations; standard training framework (Harvard PON)
NS4|competitive (hardball)|competitive|extreme anchoring; limited concessions; take-it-or-leave-it; deadline pressure; information withholding; power plays; brinkmanship|strong power position; weak counterparty BATNA; transactional context; counterparty expects it (some cultures); when you can walk away easily|maximizes short-term value capture; effective when power differential is large|high impasse risk; destroys relationships; leaves integrative gains on table; reputational damage; may trigger retaliation; legal risks if crosses into duress/bad faith|monopoly supplier; corporate acquisition with leverage; hostage negotiation (modified)
NS5|collaborative problem-solving|collaborative|treat negotiation as joint problem-solving; full transparency on constraints; brainstorm solutions together; build relationship first; focus on shared goals|long-term partnerships; joint ventures; internal negotiations (within organization); alliance formation; situations where parties' fates are interdependent|deepest value creation; strongest relationships; most creative solutions; highest compliance and satisfaction|vulnerable to exploitation if counterparty is not reciprocally transparent; may share too much (information asymmetry advantage lost); requires high trust|strategic partnerships; M&A integration planning; labor-management cooperation; cross-functional team resource allocation
NS6|BATNA-centric|analytical|rigorously assess own BATNA and estimate counterparty's BATNA; negotiate to do better than BATNA; walk away if proposed deal is worse than BATNA; improve own BATNA before and during negotiation|always (BATNA awareness should underlie every strategy); particularly when considering whether to continue or walk away; when evaluating fairness of proposals|prevents bad deals; provides objective basis for decision-making; reduces emotional decision-making; power source independent of positional tactics|BATNA estimation of counterparty is uncertain; overconfidence in BATNA leads to impasse; may overlook creative options that exceed both BATNAs|every negotiation benefits from BATNA analysis; especially: job offers (compare to other offers); vendor selection; litigation settlement (compare to expected trial outcome)

# tactics(id|name|type|mechanism|counter|ethical_status|notes)
TC1|anchoring|framing|first number on the table becomes reference point; subsequent offers gravitate toward anchor; research: anchors influence outcomes even when arbitrary|counter-anchor with own well-researched number; explicitly reject anchor as irrelevant; reframe discussion around objective criteria|ethical (normal)|most powerful single tactic; make first offer when you have good information about fair range; don't make first offer when you lack information (let them anchor, then adjust)
TC2|bracketing|positional|set opening offer so that your target falls at midpoint between your offer and their expected counteroffer; assumes splitting the difference is likely endpoint|recognize the tactic; don't split the difference reflexively; anchor on your own range; use objective criteria|ethical (normal)|requires estimating counterparty's opening position; more effective when combined with diminishing concessions
TC3|logrolling|integrative|trade concessions across issues: give on issues you value less in exchange for gains on issues you value more; requires multiple issues and asymmetric valuations|identify your own priorities clearly before logrolling; don't concede high-priority items for low-priority gains; ensure trades are actually trades (not unilateral concessions)|ethical (desirable)|core integrative technique; prerequisite: multiple issues on the table; single-issue negotiations cannot logroll; package proposals (multi-issue offers) enable logrolling
TC4|nibbling|positional|after main deal is agreed, ask for small additional concessions (free shipping, extended warranty, additional features); exploits commitment and desire to finalize|recognize nibble; treat each request as re-opening negotiation; require reciprocal concession; set clear cutoff (no changes after handshake)|ethical but annoying|effective because counterparty has invested time and wants to close; counter: everything is final until everything is signed; or: build buffer into your offers anticipating nibbles
TC5|good cop / bad cop|positional|one negotiator is aggressive and unreasonable; the other is friendly and reasonable; target makes concessions to the reasonable one (who seems moderate by contrast but is still asking for more than fair)|recognize the tactic explicitly (names it and defuses it); negotiate only with decision-maker; ask to deal with good cop alone; don't make concessions to relieve bad cop pressure|ethically questionable (manipulative)|common in car sales, procurement; counter by naming the tactic: once identified, it loses power; or: insist on single counterparty negotiator
TC6|FOMO (fear of missing out) / artificial scarcity|pressure|create urgency: limited time offer; other buyers interested; deadline approaching; this deal won't be available later|verify scarcity claims independently; slow down (urgency reduces rational analysis); be willing to walk away; test: if it's real, it will survive scrutiny|ethically gray (can be genuine or manufactured)|effective because loss aversion is ~2× more powerful than gain motivation (Kahneman); counter: if the deal is right, take it; if pressure is the only reason, walk away
TC7|silence|information gathering|after making an offer or asking a question, remain silent; discomfort often causes counterparty to fill silence with concessions or information|be comfortable with silence yourself; don't rush to fill gaps; prepare brief responses if needed|ethical (normal)|one of the most powerful and underused tactics; discomfort with silence is cultural (varies); in Western business: silence after an offer creates pressure to respond; often the next person to speak makes a concession
TC8|conditional concession (if-then)|integrative|every concession is conditional: I'll give you X if you give me Y; prevents unilateral concessions; maintains reciprocity|use same framework: respond with your own if-then; refuse unconditional demands|ethical (desirable)|fundamental discipline: never concede without getting something; protects against nibbling; builds toward packages; format: If you can do X, then I can do Y
TC9|objective criteria|principled|anchor proposals on external standards: market rate, industry benchmark, cost analysis, precedent, expert opinion, legal standard; removes subjective debate|challenge the relevance or accuracy of their criteria; present alternative criteria; agree on criteria before applying them to specific issues|ethical (desirable)|Fisher & Ury third pillar; most powerful in principled negotiation; criteria should be independent of either party's will; examples: comparable sales, published salary surveys, cost-plus margin, inflation index
TC10|BATNA revelation (strategic)|power|selectively reveal your strong BATNA to establish power; or credibly threaten to exercise BATNA (walk away)|improve your own BATNA; question credibility of their claimed BATNA; don't reveal your BATNA if it's weak|ethical if truthful; unethical if fabricated|never bluff a BATNA you won't exercise (credibility loss is permanent); revealing a strong BATNA shifts negotiation toward your reservation price; but: revealing a weak BATNA is catastrophic; default: keep BATNA private unless it's genuinely strong and the negotiation is stalling

# cognitive_biases(id|name|type|effect_on_negotiation|exploitation|defense)
CB1|anchoring bias|judgment|first number strongly influences subsequent estimates; even arbitrary anchors affect outcomes; initial offer anchors the entire negotiation range|set aggressive but defensible first offer; anchor with multiple data points (list price, comparable, assessed value)|prepare own independent valuation before receiving any anchor; explicitly reject irrelevant anchors; counter-anchor immediately
CB2|loss aversion|prospect theory|losses feel ~2× more painful than equivalent gains feel good; negotiators are more risk-seeking to avoid losses than to achieve gains; framing matters: same deal framed as loss vs gain changes acceptance|frame proposals as what counterparty will lose by not accepting (loss frame); frame concessions as preventing their loss, not giving them a gain|reframe: consider each proposal against BATNA (objective reference point), not against aspiration or current position; be aware of sunk-cost-driven loss aversion
CB3|endowment effect|ownership bias|people value what they already own more than equivalent items they don't own; sellers demand more than buyers will pay for identical item; current terms become reference point (hard to concede from status quo)|present changes as restoring fairness rather than taking away; introduce new issues (no endowment yet); create trial/provisional agreements (reduces endowment on full deal)|value items based on market, not on ownership feeling; be aware of status quo bias in renegotiations
CB4|reactive devaluation|attribution|proposals from counterparty are valued less simply because they come from the counterparty; same offer from neutral third party would be evaluated more favorably; any concession they make must be worth little since they offered it|have proposals suggested by mediator or neutral party; present options as discovered jointly rather than as your offer|evaluate each proposal on its merits against BATNA and interests, not by source; ask: would I accept this if a neutral offered it?
CB5|winner's curse|auction/acceptance|if counterparty accepts your offer immediately, you probably offered too much (or asked too little); creates regret even when outcome is objectively good|use to your advantage by accepting with apparent reluctance even when deal is good for you (validates their outcome)|don't make opening offers at your limit; start with room to concede; expect some back-and-forth; if they accept immediately, learn for next time (don't renegotiate)
CB6|confirmation bias|information processing|seeking and interpreting information to confirm pre-existing beliefs about the deal or counterparty; ignoring disconfirming evidence; overconfidence in own position|present information that confirms their existing beliefs while leading to your desired outcome|actively seek disconfirming evidence; assign team member as devil's advocate; stress-test your assumptions; listen to understand, not to rebut
CB7|fixed-pie bias|perception|assumption that negotiation is zero-sum: my gain is your loss; prevents exploration of integrative solutions; leads to distributive behavior even when integrative opportunities exist|demonstrate expanding-pie possibilities with multi-issue proposals; ask diagnostic questions about priorities|assume every negotiation has integrative potential until proven otherwise; explore interests and priorities before claiming value; ask: are there issues we value differently?
CB8|overconfidence|self-assessment|overestimate probability of favorable outcome (litigation, arbitration, counterparty conceding); leads to rejection of fair offers; excessive optimism about BATNA|provide objective data on typical outcomes (base rates); challenge their assumptions with specific questions|use base rates (how often do plaintiffs win?); get outside perspective; challenge own assumptions; calculate expected value of BATNA honestly
CB9|sunk cost fallacy|decision error|continuing negotiation or deal because of prior investment (time, money, emotion) rather than future value; refusing to walk away from bad deal because of effort already spent|use time pressure and sunk costs to extract concessions from a committed counterparty (they won't want to walk away after investing)|evaluate deal on current and future merits ONLY; ask: if I were starting fresh today, would I enter this deal? ignore past investment in decision

# contract_types(id|name|type|parties|consideration|key_features|governing_law|examples)
KT1|bilateral contract|mutual exchange|two parties; both make promises|each party's promise is consideration for the other's|both parties bound immediately upon agreement; most commercial contracts; mutual obligations|common law or UCC (Article 2 for goods)|sale of goods, service agreements, leases, employment contracts
KT2|unilateral contract|one-sided promise|offeror promises; offeree performs (no promise to perform)|offeree's performance is the acceptance and the consideration; offeror cannot revoke once offeree begins performance (modern rule)|only one party makes promise; binding only when performance complete (or begun); rare in commercial context|common law|reward offers (find lost dog, report crime); option contracts; insurance policies (insurer promises; insured pays premium = performance)
KT3|express contract|stated terms|any number|stated in words (oral or written)|terms explicitly stated by parties; may be oral (enforceable unless Statute of Frauds applies: real estate, contracts >1 year, goods >$500 UCC, suretyship, marriage)|common law or UCC|written commercial contract; signed lease; purchase order with terms
KT4|implied contract|inferred from conduct|any number|reasonable value of benefit conferred|terms inferred from parties' conduct, course of dealing, trade usage, or circumstances; not explicitly stated but recognized by law|common law; UCC gap-filling (Article 2)|repeat customer at restaurant (implied contract to pay); contractor performs work at owner's request without written contract (implied-in-fact: reasonable compensation owed)
KT5|adhesion contract|take-it-or-leave-it|stronger party drafts; weaker party accepts or rejects (no negotiation)|standard terms; consideration varies|one party has no bargaining power over terms; terms drafted entirely by stronger party; courts scrutinize for unconscionability (procedural + substantive); enforceable unless unconscionable|varies|insurance policies; software EULA; cell phone contracts; rental car agreements; airline tickets; employment arbitration clauses (increasingly scrutinized)
KT6|requirements contract|output/requirements|buyer and seller|buyer's actual requirements (must be in good faith; cannot be unreasonably disproportionate to estimates)|buyer agrees to purchase all requirements of specified goods from seller; quantity determined by buyer's actual needs; exclusive dealing (seller is sole source); obligation of good faith in determining requirements|UCC 2-306|exclusive supplier agreements; fuel supply contracts; component supply for manufacturing
KT7|option contract|right without obligation|grantor (bound) + grantee (free to exercise or not)|consideration paid for option (option premium)|grantee has right to exercise (purchase, sell, extend, etc.) within specified period; grantor irrevocably bound during option period; consideration keeps option open|common law; UCC 2-205 (firm offer: merchant, written, ≤3 months, no consideration needed)|real estate option; stock option (employee compensation); lease renewal option; right of first refusal (ROFR)

# contract_elements(id|name|type|definition|requirements|absence_effect|notes)
CE1|offer|formation|manifestation of willingness to enter contract on specified terms with intent that acceptance creates binding agreement|definite terms (identity of parties, subject matter, quantity, price or mechanism for determining price); communicated to offeree; distinguished from invitation to treat (advertisement, price list, catalog)|no offer → no contract; indefiniteness → no contract (unless UCC gap-filling applies: reasonable price, reasonable time)|offer can be revoked before acceptance (except option, firm offer, or offeree has relied); mirror image rule (common law): acceptance must match offer exactly; UCC 2-207: modified mirror image (additional terms may become part of contract between merchants)
CE2|acceptance|formation|unequivocal assent to terms of offer; creates binding contract upon effective acceptance|must be communicated to offeror (or performance of act in unilateral contract); must be by authorized offeree; must be timely (within reasonable time or stated deadline); must mirror offer (common law) or substantially agree (UCC)|no acceptance → no contract; conditional acceptance = counteroffer (rejects original offer)|mailbox rule (common law): acceptance effective when dispatched (not received); electronic: UETA/E-SIGN: electronic signatures and records equivalent to written; silence is NOT acceptance (exception: prior course of dealing; membership organizations)
CE3|consideration|formation|bargained-for exchange of legal value; each party must give something of legal value (promise, act, forbearance) in exchange for the other's promise|must be bargained for (not gift or past consideration); must have legal value (not illusory promise: I'll pay if I feel like it); adequacy not required (courts don't evaluate fairness of exchange: peppercorn doctrine); must be legal|no consideration → no enforceable contract (exception: promissory estoppel / detrimental reliance: party who relied on promise to their detriment can enforce even without consideration)|pre-existing duty rule: doing what you're already obligated to do is not new consideration (exception: UCC 2-209: modification of goods contract doesn't require consideration); past consideration: promise made after benefit conferred is generally not enforceable (exception: moral obligation + material benefit in some jurisdictions)
CE4|capacity|formation|legal ability to enter contract; parties must have mental capacity to understand terms and consequences|age: 18+ (minors can void contracts except for necessaries); mental competency: not adjudicated incompetent; not intoxicated to point of incapacity; corporate: must have authority (actual or apparent)|contract by minor: voidable at minor's option; contract by incompetent: void or voidable depending on adjudication|corporate authority: ensure signatory has actual authority (board resolution, operating agreement, power of attorney); agent must be authorized (actual authority: express or implied; apparent authority: reasonable belief by third party based on principal's conduct)
CE5|legality|formation|contract must have legal purpose and not violate law or public policy|subject matter must be legal; performance must be legal; cannot contract to commit crime, tort, or violation of statute; cannot contract against public policy (restraint of trade beyond reasonable scope, unconscionable terms)|illegal contract: void and unenforceable (neither party can enforce; courts leave parties where they are); exception: party protected by statute (e.g., tenant under rent control) can enforce protective provisions|some contracts require licensing: unlicensed contractor may not recover for work performed in jurisdictions requiring licensure; gambling contracts: unenforceable in most states unless specifically authorized; covenant not to compete: must be reasonable in scope, duration, geography (otherwise: void or reformed by court)
CE6|mutual assent (meeting of the minds)|formation|parties must agree to same terms; objective test: would a reasonable person conclude agreement was reached based on parties' words and conduct|determined by objective manifestation (outward behavior), not subjective intent (secret mental reservation); must agree on material terms (if ambiguity exists: mutual mistake → void; unilateral mistake → generally enforceable unless other party knew or should have known)|no mutual assent → no contract; mistake: mutual mistake of material fact → voidable by either party; unilateral mistake → generally enforceable (exception: if non-mistaken party knew or should have known of mistake)|parol evidence rule: for fully integrated written contract, prior or contemporaneous oral agreements that contradict the writing are inadmissible to vary terms; exceptions: fraud, duress, condition precedent, subsequent modification, ambiguity (parol evidence admissible to explain ambiguity)
CE7|writing (when required)|formation (Statute of Frauds)|certain contracts must be in writing and signed to be enforceable|Statute of Frauds categories: real estate (sale, lease >1 year); contracts not performable within 1 year; goods ≥$500 (UCC 2-201); suretyship (guarantee of another's debt); marriage (prenuptial); executor promises|oral contract within Statute of Frauds: unenforceable (but not void: can be ratified by subsequent writing or part performance; in UCC: written confirmation between merchants; part performance exceptions)|writing need not be formal contract: email, text message, signed memo, purchase order can satisfy; electronic records satisfy under UETA and E-SIGN; part performance (real estate: buyer takes possession + makes improvements + pays consideration → court may enforce oral contract to avoid injustice)

# contract_clauses(id|name|type|function|typical_language_summary|negotiation_significance|risk_if_absent)
CL1|definitions|boilerplate|defines key terms used throughout contract; establishes shared vocabulary; prevents ambiguity|Defined terms capitalized; section lists each term with precise meaning; cross-references to exhibits|establishes the playing field; ambiguity in definitions → disputes; invest time here|terms interpreted differently by each party → litigation over meaning
CL2|scope of work / specifications|substantive|describes what is to be delivered, performed, or provided; defines the core obligation|detailed description of deliverables, services, products, milestones, acceptance criteria, exclusions|most important substantive clause; vagueness → disputes over what was promised; be specific|scope creep; disputes over completeness; party claims more was promised than stated
CL3|price / payment terms|substantive|total price or pricing mechanism; payment schedule; currency; invoicing; late payment consequences|fixed price, cost-plus, time-and-materials, unit pricing; payment net 30/60/90; interest on late payment (1.5%/month); retention|heavily negotiated; cash flow implications; discounts for early payment; retention motivates completion|non-payment; cash flow mismatch; no leverage for performance if fully paid upfront
CL4|term and termination|duration|contract duration; renewal mechanism; termination rights (for cause and for convenience); survival of certain clauses beyond termination|initial term (1–5 years); auto-renewal unless notice (60–90 days); termination for cause (material breach with cure period: 30 days); termination for convenience (with notice: 30–90 days + payment for work performed)|who can exit and at what cost; termination for convenience: critical flexibility; cure period: opportunity to fix before termination|locked into bad contract with no exit; or: counterparty can walk without consequence; no survival clause → confidentiality and IP provisions die at termination
CL5|representations and warranties|risk allocation|statements of fact (representations) and promises of condition (warranties) about the subject matter, parties, or circumstances; if false, creates breach|Seller represents that: goods conform to specifications, free of defects in materials and workmanship for [12 months]; Seller has authority and capacity to enter this agreement; no pending litigation|allocates risk of unknown facts; heavily negotiated in M&A (rep and warranty insurance common); survival period (typically 1–3 years after closing); baskets and caps limit exposure|no recourse if facts are wrong; buyer bears all risk of undisclosed problems; no basis for indemnification claim
CL6|indemnification|risk allocation|one party agrees to compensate the other for specified losses, damages, claims, or expenses arising from specified events or breaches|Seller shall indemnify, defend, and hold harmless Buyer from and against any claims, damages, losses arising from Seller's breach of representations, negligence, or willful misconduct; subject to cap and basket|critical risk allocation: who pays when things go wrong; scope (what triggers it), cap (maximum amount), basket (minimum threshold before indemnification kicks in), survival period|no contractual remedy for third-party claims; must rely on tort law (slower, uncertain); each party bears own risk
CL7|limitation of liability|risk allocation|caps total liability and/or excludes certain types of damages (consequential, incidental, punitive)|In no event shall either party's total liability exceed the amount paid under this agreement in the preceding 12 months; neither party shall be liable for consequential, incidental, or punitive damages|heavily negotiated; often mutual; consequential damage exclusion is standard but critical (consequential damages can dwarf contract value); cap amount is key: 1× vs 12-month vs total contract value; some liabilities carved out from cap (IP indemnity, confidentiality breach, willful misconduct)|unlimited liability (party exposed to damages far exceeding contract value); no consequential damage exclusion → liable for lost profits, lost business, downstream damages
CL8|confidentiality / NDA|protection|restricts disclosure and use of proprietary information shared between parties|Definition of confidential information (broad); obligations (not to disclose, use only for purpose); exclusions (publicly known, independently developed, rightfully received from third party, compelled by law); duration (2–5 years or perpetual for trade secrets); return/destroy upon termination|precedes most business negotiations (mutual NDA before sharing information); duration and scope most negotiated; residuals clause (information retained in memory can be used: debated)|no legal protection for shared information beyond trade secret law (which requires reasonable efforts to maintain secrecy); competitive harm; loss of trade secret status
CL9|intellectual property ownership|IP allocation|specifies who owns IP created during or related to the contract; work-for-hire; assignment; license-back|Work product created by Contractor shall be deemed work-for-hire and owned by Client; to the extent not work-for-hire, Contractor hereby assigns all right, title, and interest; Contractor retains license to pre-existing IP and grants Client irrevocable license to use|critical in service/development contracts; default without clause: contractor owns what contractor creates (copyright: author owns unless work-for-hire or assignment); pre-existing IP vs new IP distinction essential|contractor owns deliverables (client paid for work but doesn't own the IP); can't use, modify, or license without contractor's permission; must license from contractor or re-procure
CL10|force majeure|risk allocation|excuses or suspends performance when prevented by events beyond reasonable control|Neither party shall be liable for failure to perform obligations due to events beyond its reasonable control including but not limited to: acts of God, war, terrorism, pandemic, government action, natural disaster, strike; affected party must notify; obligation to mitigate; if force majeure continues beyond [90 days], either party may terminate|scope of qualifying events is key negotiation point; COVID elevated this clause from boilerplate to critical; does economic hardship qualify? (generally no: commercial impracticability is separate doctrine)|no excuse for non-performance due to unforeseeable events; breach of contract even when performance is impossible; must rely on common law doctrines (impossibility, impracticability, frustration of purpose: higher burden to prove)
CL11|dispute resolution|procedural|specifies mechanism for resolving disputes: negotiation → mediation → arbitration or litigation; choice of law; choice of forum / venue|Any dispute arising under this agreement shall first be submitted to negotiation between senior executives (30 days); then to mediation (60 days); then to binding arbitration under [AAA/JAMS/ICC] rules in [city]; governed by the laws of [state/country]|determines cost, speed, confidentiality, expertise, and enforceability of dispute resolution; arbitration: faster, confidential, final (limited appeal), but can be expensive (arbitrator fees); litigation: public, precedent-setting, broader discovery, appeal rights|default: litigation in any court with jurisdiction (unpredictable forum; potentially hostile jurisdiction); no escalation ladder (jumps straight to adversarial proceedings); no confidentiality
CL12|governing law / choice of law|procedural|specifies which jurisdiction's law governs interpretation and enforcement|This agreement shall be governed by and construed in accordance with the laws of the State of [X], without regard to its conflicts of laws principles|determines applicable rules of interpretation, implied terms, statute of limitations, available remedies; "without regard to conflicts of laws" prevents court from applying different jurisdiction's law under conflict rules|applicable law uncertain (court applies conflict-of-laws rules to determine: could be unfavorable jurisdiction); different jurisdictions have different rules on consequential damages, implied warranties, non-compete enforcement
CL13|assignment|transfer|governs whether and how a party can transfer its rights and obligations under the contract to a third party|Neither party may assign this agreement without prior written consent of the other party, which consent shall not be unreasonably withheld; assignment in connection with merger, acquisition, or sale of all or substantially all assets is permitted|anti-assignment prevents you from being stuck with unwelcome counterparty; but: carve-out for M&A is standard (otherwise contract impedes legitimate business transactions); change of control trigger (different from assignment)|counterparty can assign to financially weaker or hostile party; you're stuck performing for someone you didn't choose; your counterparty disappears via assignment to shell entity
CL14|entire agreement (integration / merger)|interpretation|contract is the complete and final expression of parties' agreement; supersedes all prior negotiations, proposals, and agreements (oral or written)|This agreement constitutes the entire agreement between the parties and supersedes all prior and contemporaneous agreements, representations, and understandings; no modification except in writing signed by both parties|protects against claims that oral side deals or prior drafts are binding; "no oral modification" clause prevents informal changes from binding (but: some jurisdictions still enforce oral modifications if relied upon)|prior representations (maybe fraudulent) enforceable; side letters and emails may modify contract; ambiguity about which version is controlling
CL15|notice|procedural|specifies how official communications under the contract must be delivered; defines effective date of notice|All notices shall be in writing and delivered by hand, overnight courier, or registered mail to the addresses set forth herein; email notice is effective if acknowledged; notice is effective upon receipt (or [3 business days] after mailing)|ensures critical communications (termination, breach, default, force majeure) are properly delivered and documented; email-only notice is risky (delivery uncertain, spam filters)|notice not properly given → may not be effective (termination, cure period, default notice all require proper notice); party claims it never received critical communication
CL16|waiver|protection|failure to enforce a provision does not waive the right to enforce it in the future; waivers must be in writing|No waiver of any provision shall constitute a waiver of any other provision; no waiver shall be effective unless in writing; failure to enforce shall not constitute a waiver|prevents precedent erosion: if you allow one late payment, you don't lose the right to enforce on-time payment going forward|pattern of non-enforcement = implied waiver; counterparty argues: you let it slide before, so it's the new normal; court may agree (course of dealing, estoppel)

# remedies(id|name|type|availability|measure|limitations|notes)
RM1|expectation damages (benefit of the bargain)|legal (money)|default remedy for breach of contract|put the non-breaching party in the position they would have been in had the contract been performed; lost profits + cost of completion − payments saved|must be foreseeable (Hadley v. Baxendale: arising naturally from breach or in contemplation of parties at formation); must be certain (not speculative); duty to mitigate (take reasonable steps to reduce damages)|most common contract remedy; includes direct damages (cost to procure substitute performance) and consequential damages (downstream losses: lost profits, lost customers, etc.); consequential damages often contractually excluded (CL7)
RM2|reliance damages|legal (money)|alternative to expectation when lost profits are uncertain|put non-breaching party back in position they were in before the contract: out-of-pocket expenditures made in reliance on the contract|cannot exceed expectation damages (if provable); must be reasonable reliance expenditures|used when expectation damages (profits) are too speculative to prove; promissory estoppel cases; pre-contract reliance expenditures
RM3|restitution|legal (money) or equitable|available when one party is unjustly enriched at other's expense; available even without valid contract (quasi-contract)|restore value of benefit conferred on breaching party; measured by: value of benefit received (not cost to conferring party)|cannot exceed actual benefit conferred; not available if contract fully performed by non-breaching party (use expectation instead)|prevents unjust enrichment; available in quasi-contract (implied-in-law): no actual agreement, but fairness requires compensation; quantum meruit: reasonable value of services rendered
RM4|specific performance|equitable|available when money damages are inadequate: unique goods (real estate, art, rare items); long-term contracts where substitute performance unavailable|court orders breaching party to perform as promised|not available for personal services (involuntary servitude concerns); subject to court discretion; requires clean hands (requesting party must not have breached); impracticability defense|common in real estate (every parcel is unique); rare in goods contracts (substitute usually available); court supervises performance; contempt of court if order violated
RM5|injunction|equitable|court order prohibiting or requiring specific conduct|preliminary: during litigation to preserve status quo; permanent: final remedy|must show: likelihood of success on merits + irreparable harm + balance of hardships favors movant + public interest|commonly used to enforce: non-compete agreements, confidentiality obligations, IP rights; preliminary injunction: powerful tool (changes behavior before trial); bond required (covers wrongful injunction damages)
RM6|liquidated damages|contractual|parties agree in advance to fixed amount of damages for specific breach|must be: (1) reasonable estimate of anticipated damages at time of contracting AND (2) actual damages must be difficult to calculate at time of breach|if amount is unreasonable (punitive rather than compensatory): court may treat as unenforceable penalty; enforceability varies by jurisdiction|common in: construction (daily delay damages per day late); software (SLA credits); non-compete (fixed payment per violation); advantage: avoids costly damage calculation; certainty for both parties; must not be so high as to be punitive
RM7|rescission|equitable|cancellation of contract; restores parties to pre-contract positions; available for: fraud, mutual mistake, duress, undue influence, incapacity, impossibility|contract treated as if it never existed; both parties return consideration received; restitution of any benefits conferred|must be timely (cannot rescind after ratifying or accepting benefits with knowledge of defect); must be able to restore status quo (if irreversible performance, rescission may not be available)|often combined with restitution; unilateral rescission: rare (only for serious defects in formation); mutual rescission: by agreement (both parties release each other)

# dispute_resolution(id|name|type|cost|speed|confidentiality|finality|enforceability|best_for|notes)
DR1|direct negotiation|informal|very low (time only)|fast (hours to weeks)|private|non-binding (unless settlement agreement signed)|depends on settlement agreement|most disputes; first step in any dispute; preserves relationship|85–95% of disputes settle without formal proceedings; senior executive escalation (NP6): bring decision-makers who can resolve; without positional commitment of lawyers
DR2|mediation|assisted negotiation|low to moderate ($1,000–20,000 per party per day; mediator fee shared)|moderate (days to weeks for scheduling; 1–3 day sessions typical)|confidential (by agreement and often by statute; mediator cannot testify in subsequent proceedings)|non-binding (unless settlement agreement signed); mediator has no authority to impose outcome|settlement agreement is enforceable contract|complex disputes where parties want to preserve relationship; commercial; employment; construction; family|mediator: neutral facilitator; does not decide; facilitates communication, reality-tests positions, proposes solutions; evaluative vs facilitative styles; high success rate (60–80% settle at mediation); low risk (worst case: no agreement)
DR3|arbitration (binding)|adjudicative|moderate to high ($10,000–500,000+ depending on complexity and arbitrator fees; discovery more limited than litigation)|moderate (6–18 months typical; faster than litigation)|confidential (by agreement; no public record)|final and binding (very limited grounds for appeal: fraud, arbitrator misconduct, exceeding authority; Federal Arbitration Act favors enforcement)|highly enforceable (FAA; New York Convention for international: 170+ countries)|commercial contracts (especially international); securities; employment; construction; consumer (controversial)|arbitrator(s): 1 or 3 (panel for complex); selection: party-appointed + chair; or from roster (AAA, JAMS, ICC); discovery: limited (efficient but may disadvantage party needing extensive discovery); no jury; no appeal (finality is feature or bug depending on perspective); class action waiver in arbitration clauses: enforced by SCOTUS (controversial)
DR4|litigation|adjudicative (court)|high ($50,000–10,000,000+ depending on complexity)|slow (1–5+ years: discovery, motions, trial, appeal)|public record (proceedings, filings, decisions are public unless sealed)|binding but appealable (de novo for law; clearly erroneous for facts)|fully enforceable (court judgment; enforcement mechanisms: garnishment, lien, contempt)|cases requiring public precedent; constitutional issues; injunctive relief; class actions; complex multi-party disputes; where discovery is essential|full discovery rights (depositions, interrogatories, document requests, subpoenas); jury trial right (US: 7th Amendment for claims >$20); res judicata (final judgment bars relitigation); stare decisis (precedent); fee-shifting: American rule (each party pays own fees unless contract or statute provides otherwise); English rule (loser pays)
DR5|expert determination|adjudicative (limited)|low to moderate|fast (weeks to months)|confidential|typically final and binding (by contract)|contract governs (treated as binding contractual agreement on determined issue)|technical disputes: valuation, accounting, quality, measurement; earn-out disputes; pricing adjustments; technical specifications|narrower than arbitration: expert determines specific factual or technical question (not legal issues); parties pre-agree to be bound by expert's determination; quick; cheap; expert has domain knowledge; but: limited procedural protections (no discovery, limited hearing); challenge only for manifest error or fraud
DR6|dispute review board (DRB)|advisory or adjudicative|moderate (board retained throughout project)|fast for each dispute (hearing within weeks of referral)|confidential|advisory (non-binding recommendation) or adjudicative (binding); varies by contract|recommendation: not enforceable (but parties can agree to accept); binding determination: enforceable as arbitral award in some jurisdictions|construction (most common); infrastructure; large projects; standing board prevents disputes from festering|three-member panel appointed at project start; site visits; real-time dispute resolution during construction; dramatically reduces litigation (projects with DRB litigate <2% of disputes vs 30%+ without); cost included in project budget; FIDIC contracts incorporate DRB

# risk_allocation(id|name|type|mechanism|which_party_bears|negotiation_points|notes)
RA1|warranty|quality risk|warranting party guarantees condition, quality, or performance of goods or services; breach → remedy (repair, replace, refund, damages)|warranting party (seller, contractor)|duration (1–5 years typical); scope (defects vs fitness for purpose vs design); exclusions (misuse, modification, consumables); remedy (repair vs replace vs refund); warranty period commencement (delivery vs acceptance vs commissioning)|express warranty: stated; implied warranty of merchantability (UCC: goods fit for ordinary purpose); implied warranty of fitness for particular purpose (UCC: seller knows buyer's purpose and selects goods); implied warranties can be disclaimed (conspicuously, in writing)
RA2|insurance requirements|risk transfer|party must maintain specified insurance to cover losses arising from performance; named additional insured provides direct claim rights|insured party pays premiums; risk transferred to insurer; additional insured has coverage under insured's policy|types required: CGL (commercial general liability: $1–5M); professional liability (E&O: $1–5M); workers' comp (statutory); auto; umbrella; builder's risk (construction); cyber (emerging); limits; deductibles; waiver of subrogation|certificate of insurance (COI) is evidence, not the policy; additional insured endorsement: critical (names other party on policy); waiver of subrogation: insurer cannot seek recovery from additional insured for covered claims
RA3|indemnification|risk shifting|one party agrees to compensate the other for losses from specified events (see CL6)|indemnifying party bears risk of specified losses|scope (what triggers indemnity); cap (maximum: often 1× contract value); basket (threshold: $0 vs de minimis vs percentage); deductible vs tipping basket; survival period; duty to defend vs duty to indemnify; procedure (notice, cooperation, control of defense)|types: first-party (losses you suffer); third-party (claims by others against you); mutual vs one-way; knock-for-knock (each party indemnifies the other for injury to own employees regardless of fault: common in oil and gas)
RA4|limitation of liability|risk capping|caps maximum exposure for breach or other claims (see CL7)|both parties typically (mutual cap)|cap amount (most negotiated); consequential damage exclusion (standard); carve-outs from cap (IP indemnity, breach of confidentiality, willful misconduct, data breach); super-cap for carved-out items|types: direct damage cap (1× fees paid); consequential exclusion (standard); aggregate cap (total contract value or annual fees); per-incident cap; time limit (claims must be asserted within period); effectively: contract-level insurance — caps downside risk for both parties
RA5|performance bond / surety|credit risk|third party (surety company) guarantees performance of contractor's obligations; if contractor defaults, surety steps in to complete work or pay damages (up to bond amount)|contractor pays premium; surety bears risk (but has right of recourse against contractor); owner protected|bond amount (typically 100% of contract price for construction); bonding capacity (contractor must qualify: surety evaluates contractor's finances, experience, backlog); payment bond (protects subcontractors and suppliers separately); maintenance bond (warranty period)|common in public construction (often mandatory: Miller Act for federal >$150K); less common in private (negotiable); surety ≠ insurance: surety expects zero losses (right of indemnity from contractor); obtainable only by financially sound contractors; bonding capacity is competitive advantage

# financial_terms(id|name|type|definition|calculation|negotiation_significance|notes)
FI1|net 30 / net 60 / net 90|payment terms|invoice due in full within 30, 60, or 90 days of invoice date|simple: pay face amount by due date; 2/10 net 30: 2% discount if paid within 10 days, else full amount in 30 days|cash flow: longer terms benefit buyer (free financing); shorter terms benefit seller (working capital); discount terms incentivize early payment; late payment interest: 1–1.5% per month is standard|2/10 net 30 discount = ~36% annualized return for paying 20 days early; always take the discount if cash available
FI2|retention (retainage)|payment holdback|owner withholds percentage (typically 5–10%) of each progress payment until project completion or acceptance|5–10% withheld from each payment; released at substantial completion or after punch list; some jurisdictions: retainage limited by statute (5% max)|secures completion; motivates contractor to finish punch list; counterparty position: reduces working capital; negotiate: reduced retention after 50% complete; release at substantial completion vs final completion; prompt release after conditions met|common in construction; some states regulate retainage amount and release timing; withholding retainage beyond statutory period → penalty
FI3|earn-out|contingent payment|portion of purchase price in M&A is contingent on post-closing performance of acquired business (revenue, EBITDA, customer retention)|defined metric measured over defined period (1–3 years); payment calculated by formula; accounting method specified; dispute resolution for calculation disagreements|bridges valuation gap between buyer and seller; seller's perspective: want control over operations to achieve targets; buyer's perspective: want freedom to integrate; most litigated M&A provision; metrics must be precisely defined (GAAP vs non-GAAP; adjustments; excluded items)|misalignment of incentives: buyer may manage business to minimize earn-out (suppress revenue, allocate costs to acquired unit); seller wants earn-out period to reflect standalone performance; best practice: clear definitions, audit rights, dispute mechanism (expert determination)
FI4|letter of credit (LOC)|payment security|bank issues irrevocable commitment to pay beneficiary upon presentation of conforming documents|issuing bank pays; applicant (buyer) reimburses bank; standby LOC: pays only if applicant defaults (backup); commercial LOC: primary payment mechanism in trade|shifts credit risk from counterparty to bank (much safer); cost: 1–3% of face value annually; irrevocable: cannot be cancelled without beneficiary consent; expiry date; conforming documents required (strict compliance)|common in international trade; construction (performance security); real estate (lease guarantee); commercial LOC: documentary compliance is exact (bank examines documents, not goods); UCP 600 (ICC): uniform rules governing LOC
FI5|escrow|neutral third-party holdback|funds deposited with neutral escrow agent; released upon satisfaction of specified conditions|buyer deposits → escrow agent holds → release conditions met → agent releases to seller (or returns to buyer if conditions not met)|protects both parties: buyer knows funds are available; seller knows funds are committed; escrow agent follows escrow agreement strictly (will not release without documented satisfaction of conditions or joint instruction)|common: real estate closing; M&A indemnification holdback (rep and warranty escrow); software source code escrow (if vendor goes bankrupt, buyer gets source code); IP license escrow; cost: escrow agent fee ($500–10,000+ depending on amount and complexity)
FI6|cost-plus pricing|pricing mechanism|buyer pays actual costs incurred by seller plus agreed markup (fee or percentage)|price = direct costs + indirect costs (allocated) + markup (fixed fee or percentage of costs; percentage: 10–25% typical for services); open-book: buyer has right to audit costs|used when scope is uncertain (R&D, construction changes, consulting); seller risk: low (costs reimbursed); buyer risk: high (no ceiling unless GMP agreed); guaranteed maximum price (GMP): cost-plus with cap → seller absorbs cost overruns above GMP; savings sharing: split savings below GMP|incentive problem: cost-plus without GMP incentivizes seller to increase costs (more cost = more fee if percentage); mitigate with: GMP, fixed fee (not percentage), audit rights, target cost with sharing formula

# concepts(id|name|definition|category)
NC1|BATNA (best alternative to negotiated agreement)|the best outcome you can achieve without reaching agreement with this counterparty; your walk-away option; the stronger your BATNA, the more power you have; never accept a deal worse than your BATNA; always try to improve your BATNA before and during negotiation|negotiation
NC2|ZOPA (zone of possible agreement)|the range between each party's reservation price (walk-away point) where agreement is possible; if buyer's maximum exceeds seller's minimum, ZOPA exists; if not, no agreement is possible without changing one party's reservation price|negotiation
NC3|reservation price (walk-away point)|the worst deal you would accept; determined by your BATNA; any deal worse than this → walk away; should be calculated precisely before negotiation begins; keep private|negotiation
NC4|aspiration point (target)|the best realistic deal you hope to achieve; sets your opening framework; research shows negotiators with higher aspirations achieve better outcomes (Galinsky & Mussweiler); aspirations anchored by market data and precedent|negotiation
NC5|value creation vs value claiming|creating: expanding total value available (integrative: make the pie bigger through trades, options, and creative problem-solving); claiming: dividing the value (distributive: take your share of the pie); tension: must do both; share information to create but protect interests to claim|negotiation
NC6|mutual assent|agreement formed when offer is accepted; objective test: would a reasonable person conclude that agreement was reached based on parties' outward manifestations; subjective intent is irrelevant if objective manifestation indicates agreement|contract
NC7|consideration|bargained-for exchange of legal value; each party must give something (promise, act, or forbearance) to make the other's promise enforceable; distinguishes enforceable contract from unenforceable gift promise|contract
NC8|material breach vs minor breach|material breach: failure to perform a significant obligation that goes to the heart of the contract; non-breaching party may: terminate, seek damages, withhold performance; minor breach: failure of non-essential obligation; non-breaching party: may seek damages but must continue performing; Restatement (Second) factors: deprivation of expected benefit, adequacy of compensation, likelihood of cure, good faith|contract
NC9|good faith and fair dealing|implied covenant in every contract: parties must act honestly, fairly, and in accordance with reasonable expectations; cannot exercise discretion to deprive the other party of the benefit of the bargain; UCC 1-304: obligation of good faith in performance and enforcement|contract
NC10|unconscionability|contract or clause so one-sided that it is unenforceable; requires both: procedural unconscionability (unfair bargaining process: fine print, no opportunity to read, unequal bargaining power) AND substantive unconscionability (unreasonably favorable terms); court may: refuse to enforce contract, sever unconscionable clause, or limit application|contract
NC11|parol evidence rule|for a fully integrated written contract, prior or contemporaneous oral agreements that contradict the writing are inadmissible; partially integrated: consistent additional terms may be admitted; exceptions: fraud, duress, condition precedent, ambiguity, subsequent modification, separate consideration|contract
NC12|statute of limitations|time limit for bringing legal action after breach; varies by type: written contract (4–6 years most states); oral contract (2–4 years); UCC (goods): 4 years (parties can reduce to 1 year but not extend); discovery rule: clock starts when breach is or should have been discovered|contract
NC13|privity of contract|only parties to the contract can enforce it; third parties generally cannot sue on contract they are not party to; exceptions: third-party beneficiary (intended beneficiary can enforce); assignment (assignee steps into assignor's shoes); agency (principal bound by agent's authorized contracts)|contract
NC14|mitigation of damages|non-breaching party has duty to take reasonable steps to minimize damages after breach; cannot recover damages that could have been avoided by reasonable effort; failure to mitigate: damages reduced by amount that could have been avoided; does not require extraordinary effort or unreasonable expense|contract
NC15|course of dealing / course of performance / trade usage|prior conduct between parties (course of dealing), conduct under current contract (course of performance), and industry customs (trade usage) are admissible to interpret and supplement contract terms; hierarchy: express terms > course of performance > course of dealing > trade usage|contract
