# concepts(id|name|definition|category)
CO1|Blacksmithing|shaping iron and steel by heating in forge and hammering on anvil; oldest metalworking trade; "black" from black oxide scale (firescale) on iron surface|foundation
CO2|Forging|plastically deforming metal at elevated temperature using compressive force (hammer, press); reshapes without removing material (unlike machining); grain flow follows shape → strongest possible form|foundation
CO3|Heat Color|visible glow indicating temperature; iron/steel: black heat (<400°C, not workable), dark red (600°C), cherry red (750°C), bright cherry (800°C), orange (900-1000°C), yellow (1050°C), light yellow (1100°C), white (1200°C+)|foundation
CO4|Working Temperature|range where metal is plastic enough to forge without cracking; low-carbon steel: ~800-1100°C (bright cherry to yellow); high-carbon: narrower range (~750-1000°C); below working temp = cold working → cracks|foundation
CO5|Grain Structure|crystalline microstructure of metal; forging at correct temperature refines grain (smaller = stronger); overheating grows grain (weaker, "burned"); cold working distorts grain (work hardening)|foundation
CO6|Scale (Firescale)|black iron oxide (Fe₃O₄) forming on surface during heating; flakes off during forging; wastes material (~3% per heat); indicates oxidation; flux protects during forge welding|foundation
CO7|Decarburization|loss of carbon from steel surface during heating; carbon reacts with oxygen in atmosphere; surface becomes softer than interior; longer heating = deeper decarburization; critical for tool steel|foundation
CO8|Critical Temperature|temperature at which steel's crystalline structure transforms from ferrite+pearlite (body-centered cubic) to austenite (face-centered cubic); ~727°C for plain carbon steel (A1 line); must exceed for hardening|foundation
CO9|Normalizing|heating steel above critical temperature (~50°C above) then cooling in still air; refines grain structure; relieves internal stress from forging; produces uniform fine-grained pearlite; performed between forging sessions and before final heat treatment|foundation
CO10|Stock Removal|amount of material lost to scale, grinding, filing, and cutting during the making process; plan for ~5-10% loss; affects starting stock size calculation|foundation
CO11|Forging Sequence|planned order of operations from raw stock to finished piece; work from larger cross-sections to smaller (draw out before detail); work from center outward (or end inward depending on piece); economy of heats|foundation
CO12|Heat (noun)|single cycle of heating stock in forge and working it on anvil until too cool to work; return to forge for next heat; minimize heats to minimize scale loss and fuel consumption; plan work per heat|foundation
CO13|Moving Metal|fundamental concept: metal displaced by hammer goes somewhere; understanding where metal moves under each blow determines technique selection and hammer placement|foundation
CO14|Centerline|imaginary axis through center of stock; all operations reference centerline; bends occur at centerline; tapers are symmetric about centerline; maintaining centerline awareness prevents crooked work|foundation
CO15|Blacksmith's Triangle|three variables in balance: temperature, hammer force, and time; higher temperature = less force needed = faster work; lower temperature = more force = risk of cracking; optimal: work at correct temperature with appropriate force|foundation

# materials(id|name|carbon_content|properties|forging_behavior|uses)
MT1|Wrought Iron|<0.08% C|fibrous grain (slag inclusions create wood-like grain); very soft; extremely malleable; corrosion resistant (slag inclusions create galvanic protection); forge welds easily; historic material (no longer commercially produced)|forges at widest temperature range; most forgiving; can be worked at lower temperatures than steel; forge welds at lower temperature (~1100°C); bends without cracking even at moderate heat|traditional ironwork; gates; railings; hardware; decorative work; rarely available new (reclaimed from old buildings, bridges, ships)
MT2|Mild Steel (Low Carbon)|0.05-0.25% C (A36: ~0.26% max)|strong; ductile; does not harden appreciably by quenching; weldable; most common modern blacksmithing material; inexpensive; A36 and 1018 most common|excellent forging material; wide working range (800-1100°C); forgiving of temperature errors; forge welds at ~1200°C (higher than wrought iron; needs flux); dominant modern stock|general fabrication; gates; railings; furniture; hooks; hardware; decorative work; anything not requiring a cutting edge or spring
MT3|Medium Carbon Steel|0.25-0.60% C (1045 typical)|harder than mild; can be hardened and tempered; stronger; less ductile; weldable with care (higher carbon = harder to weld)|narrower forging range than mild; more sensitive to overheating; can be quench-hardened (unlike mild steel); normalizing important between forging sessions|tools; hammers; tongs; hardy tools; punches; axes; chisels; springs (1050-1060); parts requiring moderate hardness
MT4|High Carbon Steel|0.60-1.5% C (1075, 1084, 1095, W1, W2)|hard; can achieve full hardness by quenching; holds cutting edge; more brittle; difficult to weld; spark test: many fine branching sparks|narrow forging range (~750-1000°C); very sensitive to overheating (grain growth rapid); must not forge below cherry red (cracks); normalize between forging sessions; quench from critical temperature|knives; chisels; punches; springs (lower end); files; blades; cutting tools; anything requiring sharp edge or spring temper
MT5|Tool Steel (W1, W2, O1, S7)|varies by grade; W1: ~0.95% C water-hardening; O1: ~0.90% C oil-hardening; S7: 0.50% C shock-resistant|W1/W2: simple carbon steel, water quench (aggressive, risk of cracking); O1: oil quench (gentler), contains chromium, manganese, tungsten; S7: tough, impact-resistant|each grade has specific forging range and sensitivity; follow manufacturer's data sheet; O1 more forgiving than W1 during heat treatment; S7 extremely tough|W1: hand tools, cold chisels, punches; O1: knives, dies, precision tools; S7: hot work tools, hammer faces, chisels for demolition; alloy tool steels for specific applications
MT6|Spring Steel (5160, 1075)|5160: 0.60% C + chromium; 1075: 0.75% C|excellent fatigue resistance; high resilience (returns to shape after deflection); 5160 has added toughness from chromium|5160 forges well; good forging range; air-cool normalizing; oil quench for hardening; temper higher for spring (blue/purple ~300°C)|springs; tongs; vehicle springs (reclaimed for knife-making); anything requiring elastic return; leaf springs are common stock source for bladesmiths
MT7|Stainless Steel (300/400 series)|varies; 304: 18Cr-8Ni (austenitic, non-hardenable); 440C: 17Cr-1.0% C (martensitic, hardenable)|304: corrosion-resistant, non-magnetic, work hardens rapidly, cannot be heat-treated; 440C: hardenable, magnetic, holds edge, corrosion-resistant|300 series: difficult to forge (work hardens, gummy, narrow range); 400 series: forgeable but narrow range; both require more force than carbon steel; scale is chromium oxide (harder, tenacious)|304: decorative, food-contact, architectural; 440C: kitchen knives, surgical instruments; generally avoided by beginners (difficult and expensive); not traditional blacksmithing material
MT8|Cast Iron|2.0-4.5% C|hard; brittle; excellent casting material; poor forging material (shatters under hammer); high fluidity when molten; good compressive strength|CANNOT be forged — shatters; included because beginners sometimes attempt to forge cast iron (always fails); identify by: heavy, gray fracture surface, very hard to file, spark test shows short dull orange sparks with no branching|casting only (engine blocks, cookware, weights, ornamental); do not attempt to forge; not blacksmithing material; distinction from wrought iron and steel is critical
MT9|Damascus / Pattern-Welded Steel|alternating layers of different steels (e.g., 1084 + 15N20) forge-welded together; folded and drawn repeatedly|combines properties of constituent steels; reveals layered pattern when etched (acid attacks different steels at different rates); aesthetic and functional|forge weld multiple billets together at welding temperature; fold and draw; repeat (6-10 fold cycles = 64-1024 layers); requires excellent forge welding skill; each fold doubles layers|blades (knives, swords); decorative objects; combines beauty with function; historical significance (wootz, Viking, Japanese laminated); modern: stock removal and forging both used

# fire_management(id|name|definition|method|indicators)
FM1|Building the Fire|starting forge fire from cold; coal forge requires specific technique to establish clean coke bed|light kindling (paper + wood) or gas torch in firepot; add coal around edges (not on flame); as fire grows, coal converts to coke (volatile gases burn off → hot, clean coke); push coke to center, fresh coal to edges|fire established when center is clean coke (gray-white, no smoke); fresh coal on edges smokes (normal, volatiles burning off); green coal (raw) → brown → black → coke (gray, porous); coke is the fuel, not raw coal
FM2|Fire Shape (Coal)|controlling size and shape of fire zone to match workpiece; deep concentrated fire for thick stock; long narrow fire for long stock; small fire for small work|mound shape: coal packed around and over coke center; air from below enters through tuyere; wet coal packed on edges contains fire; "cave" or tunnel of coke over tuyere for concentrated heat|deep fire: thick stock needs all-around heat; shallow fire: thin stock heats faster, less scale; long fire: heating middle of long bar; localized: heating only specific area (rest stays cool)|match fire to work; oversized fire wastes fuel and heats stock you don't want heated; wet coal on edges prevents fire from spreading; clinker (fused ash) accumulates at tuyere (remove periodically; blocks air flow)
FM3|Air Control|managing bellows or blower speed to control fire temperature; more air = hotter; less air = cooler; no air = fire dies|gentle blast for soaking heat (slow, even, deep penetration); strong blast for quick surface heat (forge welding temperature); intermittent for maintaining without working; constant medium for general forging|listen to fire: roaring = hot; quiet = cooling; look at coke: bright white-yellow = welding heat; orange = forging; dull red = losing heat; sound changes with air flow|more air ≠ always better; excessive air oxidizes more (more scale), decarburizes faster, can overheat/burn steel; gentle sustained air for even heating; strong blast only when needed (welding, quick heat)
FM4|Fuel Selection|choosing appropriate fuel for work; coal, coke, charcoal, or gas|bituminous coal (blacksmithing grade): cokes well, moderate sulfur, moderate ash; coke (pre-processed): clean, hot, no smoke, expensive; charcoal: traditional, low sulfur (good for high-carbon), lower heat, high consumption; gas (propane): clean, consistent, no clinker|coal quality varies enormously; good blacksmithing coal: low ash, low sulfur, cokes readily; poor coal: high ash (excessive clinker), high sulfur (makes steel "hot short" — crumbles when forged); test coal before buying in quantity; charcoal for blade work (no sulfur contamination)
FM5|Soaking Heat|holding metal at working temperature allowing heat to penetrate uniformly to center; not just surface hot|reduce air to maintain temperature without increasing; leave stock in fire longer with less blast; center of thick stock lags behind surface by significant time|even color throughout cross-section when removed; surface and center at same temperature; test: nick with chisel — color of interior matches exterior; uneven heat = exterior forges while interior is cold (cracks, folds)|thick stock requires soaking; heating too fast → hot surface, cold core → cracking during forging; rule of thumb: 1 minute per 6mm of thickness at forging temperature; thicker = proportionally longer; patience critical for quality work
FM6|Clinker Management|removing fused ash (clinker) that accumulates at tuyere; blocks airflow; degrades fire quality|clinker forms from coal ash fusing in hottest zone (at tuyere); heavy, glassy, irregular mass; remove periodically: lift out with clinker breaker or poker when fire is burning; clear tuyere|signs of clinker buildup: fire becomes lazy despite air; fire shifts to one side; stock takes longer to heat; air seems reduced; poker into tuyere dislodges clinker; lift out with tongs|clinker starves fire of air; ignore too long → fire goes cold; some coals produce more clinker (high ash content); regular clinker removal is routine maintenance; clean firepot at end of session

# techniques(id|name|definition|method|hammer_control|common_errors)
TK1|Drawing Out|lengthening stock by reducing cross-section; making metal longer and thinner; most fundamental technique|heat to working temperature; hammer with cross peen perpendicular to desired direction of elongation; rotate 90° and flatten with flat face; repeat; work from thick to thin end|cross peen: stretches metal perpendicular to peen direction; start at transition point and work toward end; overlapping blows; consistent force; don't hammer same spot repeatedly without rotating|cold spots (working below temperature → surface cracks); uneven cross-section (not rotating 90° between passes); fish-mouth (spreading without drawing because blows too close to end); wavy surface (inconsistent hammer angle); losing centerline
TK2|Upsetting|shortening stock by increasing cross-section; making metal thicker and shorter; opposite of drawing out; most difficult basic technique|heat ONLY the section to be upset (rest stays cool and rigid); hold vertically; hammer straight down on end (or against anvil face upward); gravity assists if hot end is on top|even, centered blows directly along axis of stock; stock must be straight; only heated section should deform; cool section acts as handle and resists bending; frequent reheating (cools quickly because contact with anvil absorbs heat)|buckling (stock bends instead of upsetting because heated too long a section; heat only 1.5-2× diameter in length); mushrooming only at end (heat not deep enough); off-center bulge (stock not straight or blows not aligned); slow and frustrating (most heats required of any operation)
TK3|Bending|changing direction of stock at specific point; all bends have inside radius (compression) and outside radius (tension); metal thickness changes at bend|mark bend point; heat bend area; position on anvil edge, horn, or in vise at bend point; hammer to desired angle; inside of bend compresses (thickens); outside stretches (thins)|blows directed to move metal in desired direction; bend over anvil edge for sharp bends; bend over horn for curves; support one side, hammer other; for accurate angle: bend slightly past desired (spring-back) and correct|bend at wrong location (not marking accurately before heating); thinning at outside of bend (especially in sharp bends; upset first to add material at bend point); twisting during bending (stock rotates; must be straight and properly oriented); spring-back (all bends spring open slightly when released; overbend and correct)
TK4|Twisting|rotating stock along its long axis; creates decorative and functional spiral; must be uniform|heat section to be twisted evenly; grip one end in vise; grip other end with adjustable wrench or scrolling tongs; twist smoothly and evenly; can twist hot (easier) or warm (tighter, more controlled)|even rotation with steady force; count turns for consistency across multiple pieces; twist at consistent rate (jerky = uneven); thinner sections twist more easily; square stock shows twist more dramatically than round|uneven twist (one area tighter than another; heat wasn't uniform); cold twist (stock cooled before completing; results in cracks at corners of square stock); twisted off-axis (stock wasn't straight before twisting; spiral is wobby); over-twist (metal shears at corners in square stock)
TK5|Scrolling|creating curved spiral or scroll from straight stock; decorative; many variations (C-scroll, S-scroll, volute, snail)|taper end first (scrolls look best starting from a taper); heat tapered end; start curl over anvil horn tip or bending fork; continue forming progressively larger radius; use scrolling tongs or jig for consistency|controlled curving; each section bent slightly more than previous (increasing radius); scroll should be in one plane (not corkscrew); template or jig ensures consistency across multiples|flat scroll (not in single plane → corkscrew effect; forge on flat face to correct); uneven spacing (use jig or template); no taper (blunt end looks clunky; always taper scrolled ends); kink at start (first bend too sharp; use rounded horn tip or mandrel)
TK6|Punching|making holes by driving punch tool through hot metal; displaces metal rather than removing it (unlike drilling); metal displaced forms boss around hole|heat stock to full forging temperature; place on anvil face over pritchel hole; center punch; drive with firm blows until bulge appears on back side; flip stock; drive from other side to complete hole; drift to final size|centered, straight blows; punch perpendicular to surface; punch from both sides (cleaner hole, less distortion); cool punch frequently (absorbs heat from stock; softens and deforms if too hot)|off-center hole (punch not aligned); punching onto solid anvil face (slug can't escape; tool sticks; mushrooms stock); overheating punch (softens, deforms, sticks in hole); uneven hole (punch tilted during driving); tearing instead of clean breakthrough (stock too cold or punch too blunt)
TK7|Splitting|separating metal into two sections using hot cut or chisel; may be partial (fork) or complete (two pieces)|mark split line; heat; place hot cut on line; hammer to drive cut; work from both sides for deep splits; for forks: split, then open and shape tines; for cutting off: cut 3/4 through, then snap over anvil edge|controlled depth; even both sides; don't cut on anvil face (damages both); sacrificial plate or cutting hardy for through-cuts; leave slight web at bottom of split (prevents tearing) when making forks|cutting into anvil face (damages face, damages tool); uneven split (chisel wandered; stock rotated during cutting); tearing beyond intended split (went too deep; leave web); cut-off pieces flying dangerously (secure before final cut or snap; hot metal is dangerous projectile)
TK8|Shouldering|creating defined step or transition between two cross-sections; clean square shoulder between thick and thin sections|use fuller or anvil edge; heat stock; lay across anvil edge or place fuller at shoulder location; hammer to create step; work from all sides (rotate 90° for square stock); refine with flatter|defined, clean transition; all four sides (for square) or all around (for round); fuller creates initial groove, then draw out the thinner section from the groove; shoulder should be perpendicular to stock axis|rounded shoulder (not using fuller or edge to define; just drawing out without defined start point produces taper, not shoulder); unequal sides (not rotating and working all four faces); shoulder not perpendicular (stock was at angle to edge); shoulder at wrong location (measure before heating)
TK9|Forge Welding|joining two pieces of iron/steel by heating to welding temperature and hammering together; oldest welding method; pre-dates electric and gas welding by millennia|clean both surfaces (wire brush); flux (borite ax: prevents oxidation at welding temp); heat to welding temperature (yellow-white, ~1200°C for mild steel; slightly lower for wrought iron); remove from fire; quickly brush off flux/scale; place together; strike firmly (first blows most critical → squeeze out flux and oxide, establish metal-to-metal contact); continue hammering to consolidate weld|first blow is critical (establishes initial bond; must be firm and centered); work quickly (temperature drops rapidly once out of fire); surfaces must be absolutely clean (trapped scale prevents weld); scarf joints (overlapping angle cuts) provide more surface area than butt joints|failed weld (cold shut: surfaces oxidized before contact; insufficient heat; insufficient flux; dirty surfaces; too slow getting from fire to anvil); burned steel (overheated beyond welding → grain destroyed → crumbles; white sparking = burning); delamination (weld looked good but separates under stress; incomplete bonding at center)|most difficult basic technique; requires: correct temperature judgment, speed, cleanliness, proper flux, correct joint design, confident hammer blows; practice extensively on mild steel before attempting on high-carbon
TK10|Riveting|joining two pieces by inserting metal pin (rivet) through aligned holes and heading (mushrooming) both ends|punch or drill holes in both pieces; align holes; insert rivet (slightly longer than total thickness + 1.5× rivet diameter for both heads); support one end on flat surface (anvil, bucking bar); head other end with ball peen or rivet set; buck and head alternately until tight|rivet length critical: too short = insufficient head; too long = bends over instead of heading; rivet should fill hole snugly; head formed by radial blows around perimeter, not single centered blow (centers and seats rivet first, then form head)|loose rivet (hole too large; rivet too short; not peened completely); cracked rivet (rivet too cold; carbon too high; blows too aggressive); misaligned holes (always punch/drill through both pieces together or use precise layout); rivet head off-center (not bucked properly; rivet not straight in hole)
TK11|Drifting|enlarging and truing a punched or drilled hole by driving a tapered tool (drift) through; makes hole precise size and shape|after punching initial hole, heat stock; insert drift from one side; hammer through; drift may be round, square, or shaped (eye drift for hammer/axe eyes); drift pushes metal outward (thickening around hole)|drift tapers gradually; lubricate with beeswax or oil (prevents sticking); alternate sides to keep hole centered; don't try to drift too much in one pass (metal may crack); drift cools rapidly in stock (reheat frequently)|cracked edges (stock too cold; drifting too aggressively); off-center hole (drift entered at angle; not alternating sides); stuck drift (insufficient lubrication; too tight; stock cooled around drift — reheat to remove); egg-shaped hole (drift not aligned with axis; uneven blows)
TK12|Planishing|smoothing the surface of forged work using light, overlapping blows with flat hammer or flatter tool; final surface refinement|stock at low red to black heat (cooler = harder surface = less deformation per blow = smoother finish); light, rapid, overlapping blows covering entire surface; flat face slightly crowned to prevent edge marks; flatter tool for very flat surfaces|very light blows (impression of each blow barely visible); consistent force; overlap each blow by 50% (no gaps); work systematically across surface; lower temperature = less depth of impression = smoother; too cold = work hardening (mild steel) or cracking (high carbon)|creating new hammer marks while trying to remove old ones (blows too heavy); not overlapping (leaves pattern of individual marks); cold planishing of high carbon (cracks); edge marks from hammer (face not properly crowned or angled); polishing a surface that hasn't been properly forged first (planishing is refinement, not correction)

# heat_treatment(id|name|definition|process|result|critical_variables)
HT1|Hardening|heating steel above critical temperature (austenite forms) then rapid cooling (quenching) to form martensite; transforms soft steel into hard (but brittle) steel|heat to critical temperature + ~30°C (non-magnetic test: magnet stops sticking at critical temp → heat slightly beyond); quench immediately in appropriate medium (water for W1, oil for O1); agitate work in quenchant for even cooling|steel transforms from soft pearlite to hard martensite; hardness depends on carbon content (higher C = harder); Rc 60-65 achievable with 0.80%+ C; AS-QUENCHED STEEL IS EXTREMELY BRITTLE → must temper immediately|carbon content (must be >0.30% to harden appreciably); critical temperature (correct: ~770-800°C for plain carbon); quench medium (must match steel: water too aggressive for oil-hardening steel → cracks); speed (from fire to quench must be seconds; hesitation → pearlite forms instead of martensite)
HT2|Tempering|reheating hardened steel to moderate temperature (150-350°C) then cooling to reduce brittleness while retaining useful hardness; trades some hardness for toughness|immediately after hardening (never leave as-quenched; handle like glass until tempered); clean surface to see oxide colors; heat gently and evenly; watch for temper colors; quench when desired color reached; or oven temper at specific temperature for precise results|reduces hardness somewhat but dramatically increases toughness; temper color indicates temperature: pale straw (220°C, files/razors), straw (230°C, knives), bronze (250°C, axes), purple (270°C, springs), blue (300°C, screwdrivers), gray (350°C+, structural)|temperature: determines hardness-toughness balance (lower temp = harder but more brittle; higher = tougher but softer); uniformity: uneven tempering → hard and soft spots → failure at soft spot under stress; timing: temper immediately (as-quenched martensite may spontaneously crack if left)
HT3|Normalizing|heating above critical temperature then cooling in still air; refines grain structure; relieves stress from forging; produces uniform fine pearlite|heat to ~50°C above critical (~815-845°C for medium carbon); hold briefly (soak); remove from fire; cool in still air (NOT quench); no wind, no water; air cooling only|uniform fine grain structure; relieves internal stress from forging (differential heating, hammering); prepares steel for final heat treatment; should be done between forging sessions and before hardening|critical temperature (must fully transform to austenite); still air (not accelerated cooling; draft may harden some steels); one normalization cycle per forging session recommended; multiple normalize cycles progressively refine grain
HT4|Annealing|heating above critical temperature then cooling as slowly as possible; produces softest possible condition; maximum ductility for machining or cold working|heat to critical + ~30°C; cool as slowly as possible: traditional method = bury in ash, vermiculite, or lime (insulates, slows cooling to hours); kiln anneal: reduce kiln temperature 20°C/hour; cool in kiln overnight|softest possible state (full annealing); maximum ductility; easiest to machine, file, or cold work; coarse grain (not ideal for final product; normalize after if forging will follow)|cooling rate must be very slow (furnace cooling ideal); too-fast cooling after anneal = partial hardening; annealing high carbon steel before filing or drilling makes it workable; do not anneal and then try to use as hardened tool (it's soft)
HT5|Case Hardening|adding carbon to surface of low-carbon steel by heating in carbon-rich environment; creates hard surface over tough core|pack steel in container surrounded by carbon source (charcoal powder, bone meal, leather scraps); seal container; heat to ~900°C for 1-4 hours (depth depends on time); remove and quench|hard exterior (high carbon surface hardens) over tough ductile core (low carbon interior remains soft); best of both worlds for specific applications; depth of case: ~0.5mm per hour at 900°C|temperature must be above critical for carbon diffusion; time determines case depth; carbon source must be in contact with all surfaces to be hardened; uneven packing → uneven case; post-process: quench from above critical temperature to harden the carburized surface
HT6|Differential Hardening|hardening only part of the piece; clay coating insulates sections that should remain soft; quench produces hard edge and soft spine (blade making)|coat spine and areas to remain soft with clay mixture (refractory clay + ash + water; some add charcoal powder); leave edge exposed; when clay is dry: heat to critical temperature; quench; exposed edge hardens; insulated areas cool slower → remain soft|hard cutting edge + tough flexible spine; visible temper line (hamon) where hard and soft zones meet; Japanese sword tradition (yakiba); functional: blade that holds edge but doesn't shatter|clay mixture must adhere and resist thermal shock; thickness determines cooling rate (thicker = slower = softer); hamon (temper line) is boundary between martensite and pearlite; extremely difficult to control precisely; master technique in blade smithing

# measurement(id|name|definition|method|notes)
ME1|Heat Color Reading|judging metal temperature by visible glow color; most critical measurement skill; must be developed through practice|work in subdued light (shade or indoor; sunlight washes out color); cherry red (~750°C); bright cherry (~800°C); orange (~900°C); yellow (~1050°C); white (~1200°C); above white: burning (sparking)|ambient light affects perception; fluorescent lighting masks colors; learn in consistent lighting; experienced smiths read color within ~25°C; beginners vary by ~100°C; practice by comparing color to pyrometer reading
ME2|Magnet Test|determining if steel has reached critical temperature (austenite transformation) for hardening; austenite is non-magnetic; ferrite/pearlite is magnetic|touch magnet to heated steel; if magnet sticks → below critical; if magnet doesn't stick → at or above critical (austenite formed); heat ~30°C beyond non-magnetic for complete transformation|only works for hardenable steel (>0.30% C); mild steel also becomes non-magnetic at critical but doesn't harden on quenching; magnet on wire (keep hand away from heat); test away from anvil and tongs (both magnetic)
ME3|Spark Test|identifying steel type by observing spark pattern when touched to grinding wheel; each carbon content produces distinctive spark pattern|grind sample against wheel; observe spark stream in dim light; low carbon: few sparks, long orange carriers, few forks; medium carbon: more sparks, moderate forking; high carbon: many sparks, abundant fine forking (starburst); stainless: short, dull orange; cast iron: short, dull red, no forks|requires practice and reference samples; grind known samples first to calibrate eye; works best in dim light; different alloys produce characteristic patterns; wrought iron: long smooth carriers, no forking; tool steel: dense bright forking; unknown steel: compare to known samples

ME5|Filing Test|testing hardness by attempting to file the surface; file skates over hard steel; bites into soft|after hardening: good file should skate over surface without cutting (Rc 55+); if file bites: hardening failed (not hard enough; didn't reach critical, wrong quenchant, wrong steel); after tempering: file should still skate but may bite slightly at higher tempers|hardened steel is harder than file (Rc 60-65 vs file Rc 62-65); well-hardened tool steel cannot be filed; if file cuts easily: not hardened; brass brush file first (scale can fool the test); comparison: file known hardened piece alongside unknown

# failure_modes(id|topic|mode|cause|consequence|prevention)
FL1|forging|cold cracking|working metal below minimum forging temperature; surface cools while interior still hot; differential stress|cracks on surface, especially at edges and corners; may propagate into deep fractures; piece may break during subsequent forging|return to fire when color drops below dull red; don't try to squeeze in one more blow; thinner sections cool faster (work those at higher initial heat); learn to read heat color accurately

# distinctions(id|side_a|side_b|key_asymmetry)
DI1|Wrought Iron|Steel|wrought iron: <0.08% C; fibrous (slag inclusions); extremely malleable; does not harden by quenching; welds easily at lower temperature; corrosion resistant; historical material (not commercially produced); steel: 0.05-1.5% C; homogeneous crystalline; can be hardened (>0.30% C); harder, stronger; modern material; all blacksmithing stock today is steel (mild or high carbon)
DI2|Mild Steel|High Carbon Steel|mild (<0.25% C): cannot be hardened; ductile; forgiving of temperature errors; wide working range; cheap; used for structural and decorative work; high carbon (>0.60% C): hardenable; narrower working range; more sensitive to overheating; more expensive; used for tools, blades, springs; different materials requiring different handling
DI3|Hot Working|Cold Working|hot working: forging above recrystallization temperature (~700°C+ for steel); grain refines; no work hardening; plastic deformation; forging range; cold working: shaping below recrystallization; work hardens (harder, more brittle); grain distorts; requires annealing to continue; bending cold = risk of cracking in medium/high carbon
DI4|Drawing Out|Upsetting|drawing out: making longer and thinner; cross peen stretches perpendicular to peen; most common operation; relatively easy; upsetting: making shorter and thicker; hammering on end; opposite direction of metal flow; most difficult basic operation; metal wants to buckle not compress; requires precise heat control (only heated section should deform)
DI5|Hardening|Tempering|hardening: heating to critical temperature + quenching → very hard but very brittle martensite; too brittle to use; tempering: reheating to moderate temperature (150-350°C) → reduces brittleness while retaining useful hardness; MUST temper after hardening; hardening without tempering = tool that shatters on first use
DI6|Forging|Casting|forging: shaping solid metal by hammering; grain flow follows shape → strongest; any carbon content of steel; casting: pouring molten metal into mold; grain random; may have porosity; cast iron only (2-4% C); steel can be cast industrially but not in blacksmith shop; forged parts stronger than cast in same material; blacksmithing is forging, not casting
DI7|Coal Forge|Gas Forge|coal: variable heat (localized or distributed); requires fire management skill; produces smoke, ash, clinker; can reach forge welding temperature easily; lower fuel cost; messier; gas (propane): consistent even heat; simple operation; clean (no smoke); limited by chamber size; struggles with forge welding temperature (some forges); higher fuel cost; easier for beginners; each has advantages
DI8|Scale|Decarburization|scale: iron oxide on surface from oxidation during heating; flakes off; wastes material (~3% per heat); visible and mechanical; decarburization: carbon loss from surface layer; invisible; surface becomes softer than interior; critical for tool steel (soft surface won't hold edge even if interior is hard); both increase with time in fire and temperature; minimize time at temperature
DI9|Normalizing|Annealing|normalizing: heat above critical, cool in still air; moderate cooling rate; fine grain; moderate hardness; between-session treatment; general purpose; annealing: heat above critical, cool as slowly as possible (insulated, furnace cool); slowest cooling; coarsest grain; softest possible; for machining or maximum ductility; both relieve stress but produce different grain size and hardness
DI10|Filing|Grinding|filing: hand tool; slow; controlled; quiet; traditional; develops feel for metal; leaves characteristic texture; limited stock removal rate; grinding: power tool (angle grinder); fast; aggressive; loud; modern; removes material quickly; creates heat (temper risk on hardened steel); safety concerns (disc failure, debris); filing teaches; grinding produces; both have place
