# foundations(id|concept|definition|significance)
FD1|adventure|narrative driven by physical action, dangerous situations, and exploration of unknown territory — plot is motion through space and risk|the oldest story mode — Gilgamesh, Odyssey, Sinbad — the human need to narrativize encounters with the dangerous unknown
FD2|heroism|protagonist acts with courage, skill, or moral conviction against forces that threaten self or others — action is the defining medium|the hero is known by what they DO, not what they think — interiority serves action, never replaces it
FD3|the quest|protagonist pursues a defined objective through a sequence of obstacles — the objective structures the entire narrative|the spine of adventure — without a quest object (person, place, thing, knowledge, return), adventure becomes random incident
FD4|peril|genuine threat to the protagonist's life, freedom, mission, or loved ones — must be credible and present|the engine of tension — without real danger, adventure becomes tourism; peril must cost something to overcome
FD5|agency|the protagonist acts, chooses, and causes outcomes — not merely a witness to events|distinguishes hero from victim — the protagonist's decisions drive the plot forward; passivity kills adventure
FD6|competence|the protagonist possesses or develops abilities that make survival and success plausible without being guaranteed|the reader's contract — the hero must be capable enough to plausibly succeed, fallible enough to plausibly fail
FD7|the unknown|territory, culture, creature, or situation the protagonist has not encountered before — novelty is structural, not decorative|adventure requires the unfamiliar — the familiar is safety, the unknown is where the story lives
FD8|return|the hero comes back changed — physically, psychologically, socially — the journey has consequences|without return, adventure is escapism; with return, it is transformation — the world or the hero is different now
FD9|physical engagement|the body matters — fatigue, hunger, injury, terrain, weather, disease, exertion are narrative elements, not background|distinguishes adventure from drawing-room fiction — the body is at stake, not merely the mind
FD10|stakes escalation|danger increases as the narrative progresses — early obstacles are survivable, later ones threaten everything|creates rising tension — the reader must feel that things are getting worse, not merely repeating
FD11|code|the hero operates by principles — honor, loyalty, duty, justice, mercy, or personal rule — tested by circumstances|the code is what makes heroism meaningful — action without principle is merely violence; the code defines what the hero will and won't do
FD12|wonder|encounter with the extraordinary — vast landscapes, ancient ruins, impossible creatures, overwhelming beauty or terror|adventure's emotional register beyond fear — the hero (and reader) are awed by what exists beyond the known world
FD13|camaraderie|bonds formed under shared danger — loyalty tested, trust built, sacrifice for companions|the fellowship dynamic — adventure is rarely solo; the group's dynamics provide internal tension alongside external threat
FD14|adversary|the force opposing the hero — may be human, natural, supernatural, institutional, or internal|the adversary defines the hero — the nature of the opposition reveals the nature of the heroism required

# hero_models(id|model|description|defining_quality|example_tradition|tension)
HM1|classical hero|semi-divine or exceptionally gifted from birth — strength, beauty, lineage — fate-bound, often tragic|excellence (arete) — the hero is simply better than ordinary humans|Greek (Achilles, Heracles, Odysseus), Mesopotamian (Gilgamesh), Indian (Arjuna)|greatness vs mortality — the hero cannot escape fate despite surpassing all others
HM2|chivalric hero|knight bound by code of honor, service to lord and lady, defense of the weak, quest for spiritual perfection|duty (honor under pressure) — the code matters more than survival|Arthurian (Lancelot, Gawain, Galahad), Charlemagne cycle (Roland, Oliver)|duty vs desire — the code demands what the heart resists; courtly love vs martial obligation
HM3|picaresque hero|low-born, cunning, amoral survivor who navigates a corrupt world through wit rather than strength|resourcefulness — survival in a hostile social order through intelligence and adaptability|Lazarillo de Tormes, Gil Blas, Tom Jones, Moll Flanders|individual vs society — the picaro exposes institutional hypocrisy by surviving despite it
HM4|romantic hero|exceptional individual in conflict with society or nature — passionate, defiant, self-determined, often doomed|individual will — the hero insists on personal truth against external constraint|Byron (Childe Harold, Manfred), Melville (Ahab), Brontë (Heathcliff), Dumas (Edmond Dantès)|self-determination vs destruction — the same force that makes the hero great destroys them
HM5|frontier hero|self-reliant individual on the boundary of civilization — woodsman, scout, plainsman, explorer|self-reliance — competence in wilderness, independence from social institutions|Cooper (Natty Bumppo), Grey (Lassiter), L'Amour (many protagonists), London (Buck/White Fang)|civilization vs wilderness — the frontier hero belongs to neither world fully
HM6|pulp hero|two-fisted, fast-acting, larger-than-life protagonist — solves problems through action, fights evil directly|decisive action — the hero acts while others hesitate, thinks with fists as much as brain|Doc Savage, The Shadow, Conan, Tarzan, Allan Quatermain, Flash Gordon|action vs reflection — the pulp hero must always be doing something; interiority slows the pace
HM7|reluctant hero|ordinary person drawn into extraordinary circumstances — does not seek adventure, adventure finds them|ordinariness — reader identification through the hero's normalcy and resistance to the call|Tolkien (Bilbo, Frodo), Hitchcock protagonists, many thriller protagonists|comfort vs necessity — the hero wants to go home but cannot until the task is done
HM8|anti-hero|morally compromised protagonist — pursues goals through questionable means, may lack conventional virtue|moral ambiguity — the hero's methods or motives are as troubling as the villain's|noir (Marlowe, Spade), modern thriller (Reacher, Bourne), grimdark (Abercrombie), Moorcock (Elric)|effectiveness vs morality — the anti-hero succeeds where the virtuous would fail, but at what cost
HM9|collective hero|the group is the protagonist — no single character carries the narrative; the unit acts, suffers, and triumphs together|solidarity — individual heroism matters less than collective action and mutual support|war novels (Band of Brothers, The Things They Carried), heist narratives, expedition narratives|individual vs collective — personal glory must be sacrificed for group survival
HM10|trickster hero|wins through cunning, deception, and rule-breaking rather than strength or virtue — often comic|wit — intelligence applied to survival and subversion of power structures|Odysseus (partially), Anansi, Coyote, Loki, Leiber (Gray Mouser), Vance (Cugel)|cleverness vs ethics — the trickster's wins are admirable but morally questionable; comedy masks amorality
HM11|survivor hero|defined not by victory but by endurance — the world is hostile, success is staying alive and human|endurance — the hero's triumph is refusing to break under unbearable pressure|Robinson Crusoe, Hatchet, The Road (McCarthy), Lord of the Flies, The Martian (Weir)|hope vs despair — the survivor must find reasons to continue when everything says stop
HM12|swashbuckler|skilled swordsman with charm, wit, and panache — fights with style as much as effectiveness|flair — the hero makes danger look elegant; combat is performance as much as violence|Dumas (d'Artagnan, Count of Monte Cristo), Sabatini (Captain Blood), Fraser (Donat)|style vs substance — the swashbuckler's charm can mask shallow characterization if not grounded

# journey_structures(id|structure|description|phases|example)
JS1|monomyth (Campbell)|hero's journey — departure, initiation, return — universal story pattern underlying adventure|ordinary world → call → refusal → threshold → trials → abyss → transformation → return → master of two worlds|Star Wars, Lord of the Rings, Odyssey — Campbell's pattern describes, does not prescribe
JS2|odyssey|long return journey through episodic encounters — the goal is to get home, obstacles are sequential and varied|departure → series of distinct challenges → longing for home → final confrontation → homecoming|Homer's Odyssey, Cold Mountain, O Brother Where Art Thou, The Martian
JS3|quest for the grail|protagonist seeks sacred or transformative object — the journey purifies or destroys the seeker|call → companions → trials that test worthiness → approach → achievement or failure → return transformed|Arthurian Grail quest, Indiana Jones, Tolkien (Ring quest — inverted: destroy, not obtain)
JS4|descent and return (katabasis)|hero journeys to underworld/hell/abyss and returns with knowledge, power, or rescued person|descent → confrontation with death/shadow → ordeal → acquisition → ascent → changed perspective|Orpheus, Dante's Inferno, Frodo in Mordor, Heart of Darkness (partial — no true return)
JS5|expedition|organized group ventures into unmapped territory — scientific, military, or commercial motive|preparation → departure → wilderness → discoveries → crises → attrition → arrival or retreat|Conrad (Heart of Darkness), Verne (Journey to Center of Earth), Crichton (Jurassic Park), Endurance (Shackleton)
JS6|chase/pursuit|protagonist pursues or is pursued across terrain — motion is constant, rest is impossible|trigger → flight/pursuit → obstacles → near-captures → final confrontation|The 39 Steps, The Fugitive, Mad Max: Fury Road, Doyle (Hound of the Baskervilles — partially)
JS7|siege/defense|hero must hold a position against overwhelming attack — endurance under pressure|threat approaches → preparation → first assault → attrition → crisis point → relief or fall|Helm's Deep, Alamo narratives, Zulu, Masada, Last of the Mohicans (fort defense)
JS8|rescue mission|hero must extract a person from captivity — penetration, retrieval, extraction|intelligence → infiltration → discovery → liberation → escape → pursuit|Count of Monte Cristo (self-rescue), The Great Escape, Taken, Saving Private Ryan
JS9|coming-of-age journey|young protagonist's first great adventure — the journey IS the maturation|innocence → departure → first failure → mentor/companion → growth → decisive action → transformed return|Treasure Island, Hatchet, Earthsea, Huck Finn, The Old Man and the Sea (inverted — aged)
JS10|picaresque journey|episodic, loosely connected adventures of a rogue navigating society — no overarching quest, the road IS the story|episode → crisis → escape → next episode — no transformation, the picaro endures unchanged|Don Quixote (inverted picaresque), Tom Jones, Huckleberry Finn (partially), Vance (Cugel)
JS11|revenge journey|hero pursues specific adversary across distance — vengeance drives the quest|wrong suffered → oath → pursuit → obstacles → confrontation → vengeance achieved (or renounced)|Count of Monte Cristo, True Grit, Moby-Dick (inverted — revenge destroys), Inigo Montoya
JS12|exploration/discovery|hero ventures into genuinely unknown territory — the discovery itself is the objective|departure from known → frontier → terra incognita → wonder/horror → mapping/understanding → return with knowledge|Verne (20,000 Leagues, Center of Earth), Doyle (Lost World), Crichton (Congo), real exploration narratives
JS13|survival ordeal|hero must endure environmental extremity — shipwreck, wilderness, disaster — with minimal resources|catastrophe → immediate survival → adaptation → long-term strategy → rescue or self-extraction|Robinson Crusoe, Hatchet, The Martian, Life of Pi, Lord of the Flies, Swiss Family Robinson

# adventure_types(id|type|description|setting|defining_element|exemplars)
AT1|sword-and-cloak/swashbuckling|historical or pseudo-historical swordplay adventure — duels, intrigue, daring escapes|Renaissance/Early Modern Europe (or analog)|skilled swordplay + personal honor + romance|Dumas (Three Musketeers, Monte Cristo), Sabatini (Captain Blood, Scaramouche), Fraser (Donat)
AT2|sea/naval adventure|ships, navigation, storms, piracy, naval combat, exotic ports — the ocean is both setting and antagonist|open ocean, island chains, ports|the ship as microcosm — captain's authority, crew dynamics, isolation at sea|Melville (Moby-Dick), O'Brian (Aubrey-Maturin), Forester (Hornblower), Stevenson (Treasure Island)
AT3|jungle/lost world|expedition into dense wilderness discovering unknown civilizations, prehistoric creatures, or ancient ruins|tropical jungle, uncharted interior, lost plateau|the unknown interior — what civilization hasn't reached yet|Doyle (Lost World), Burroughs (Tarzan, Pellucidar), Crichton (Jurassic Park, Congo), Haggard (King Solomon's Mines)
AT4|arctic/polar|survival in extreme cold — ice, isolation, scurvy, madness, the sublime indifference of frozen landscape|polar regions, ice caps, frozen seas|the environment IS the antagonist — cold, darkness, distance from all help|London (Call of the Wild, White Fang), Simmons (The Terror), real narratives (Shackleton, Scott, Franklin)
AT5|desert/wasteland|crossing arid expanse — thirst, heat, navigation, hostile peoples, mirages, ancient secrets beneath sand|Sahara, Arabian, American Southwest, post-apocalyptic waste|scarcity — water, shade, civilization are all absent; survival is the primary quest|Lawrence (Seven Pillars), Herbert (Dune — SF/adventure), McCarthy (Blood Meridian), Grey (Riders of the Purple Sage)
AT6|mountain/alpine|climbing, crossing, or surviving in high-altitude terrain — altitude, avalanche, exposure, vertical danger|Himalayas, Alps, Andes, fictional ranges|verticality — danger increases with height; the summit is both goal and threat|Krakauer (Into Thin Air), Simpson (Touching the Void), Whymper, Tolkien (Caradhras)
AT7|underground/subterranean|caves, mines, tunnels, lost underground civilizations — darkness, claustrophobia, geological hazard|cave systems, mine shafts, subterranean worlds|darkness and enclosure — the earth itself is hostile; disorientation without sky|Verne (Journey to Center of Earth), Tolkien (Moria), Burroughs (Pellucidar)
AT8|sky/aerial|flight, airships, aerial combat, exploration from above — the vertical dimension as frontier|open sky, airship gondola, cockpit, cloud formations|freedom and exposure — no ground beneath, no shelter, speed and altitude as tactical dimensions|Saint-Exupéry (Night Flight, Wind Sand and Stars), Biggles (Johns), pulp aviation adventure
AT9|espionage/thriller adventure|protagonist operates in hostile territory under cover — danger is human betrayal, not natural environment|cities, embassies, border crossings, safe houses|paranoia — trust is the scarce resource; every ally may be an enemy|Buchan (39 Steps), Le Carré (some), Fleming (Bond), Ludlum (Bourne), Deighton
AT10|western/frontier|individual or small group in lawless territory — self-reliance, justice without institutions, civilization's edge|American West (or analog frontier)|the law is absent — the hero must create or enforce justice personally|L'Amour (many), Grey (Riders of Purple Sage), McCarthy (Blood Meridian), McMurtry (Lonesome Dove), Portis (True Grit)
AT11|war adventure|individual heroism within larger military conflict — the war provides setting and stakes, the individual provides the story|battlefield, occupied territory, behind enemy lines, POW camp|duty under fire — the individual within the machine of war; heroism is both possible and insufficient|Cornwell (Sharpe), Fraser (Donat), O'Brian (partially), war narratives (Ambrose, Hastings)
AT12|treasure hunt|pursuit of valuable object — treasure map, coded clue, rival hunters — the prize drives the plot|varied — wherever the treasure is hidden|the prize — concrete, valuable, findable — and the competition to reach it first|Stevenson (Treasure Island), Cussler (Dirk Pitt), adventure films (Indiana Jones, National Treasure)
AT13|survival horror adventure|adventure in which the environment and creatures are actively hostile — survival is the only victory|isolated location — island, ship, Arctic station, jungle|hostile ecology — the setting wants to kill you and has the tools to do it|Crichton (Jurassic Park), Simmons (The Terror), Wells (Island of Dr. Moreau), King (some)
AT14|river journey|travel by river through changing landscape — the river dictates pace, direction, and encounters|river — from source to sea, or upstream against current|the current — the river has its own will; you go where it goes or fight it at great cost|Twain (Huckleberry Finn), Conrad (Heart of Darkness), Dickey (Deliverance), real narratives (Powell, Lewis & Clark)

# character_types(id|type|description|narrative_function|examples)
CH1|the leader|takes responsibility, makes decisions under pressure, carries the weight of others' survival|provides direction, embodies agency, bears consequences of choice|Aubrey (O'Brian), Aragorn (Tolkien), d'Artagnan (Dumas), Shackleton (real)
CH2|the scout/pathfinder|navigates, tracks, reads terrain and sign — the group's interface with the wilderness|provides competence in the unknown, enables movement, detects danger|Natty Bumppo (Cooper), Aragorn-as-Strider, native guides in expedition narratives
CH3|the loyal companion|follows the hero through all danger out of love, loyalty, or shared purpose — may sacrifice self|emotional anchor, source of dialogue, raises stakes through reader's attachment|Samwise (Tolkien), Friday (Crusoe), Porthos/Athos/Aramis (Dumas), Dr. Watson (Doyle)
CH4|the adversary/villain|the active opposition — may be human, natural, institutional, monstrous|defines the hero by opposition, provides the obstacle that makes heroism necessary|Long John Silver, Moriarty, Moby Dick (nature as adversary), Colonel Kurtz
CH5|the mentor|experienced figure who teaches, warns, or equips the hero before departing|transfers knowledge, raises stakes through sacrifice or absence, provides exposition naturally|Gandalf, Obi-Wan, Long John Silver (inverted — the villain mentors), Jim Hawkins' captain
CH6|the innocent/protected|character whose safety motivates the hero — child, loved one, vulnerable person|raises moral stakes, provides emotional motivation beyond self-interest, tests the code|children in adventure (Jim Hawkins IS this and transcends it), civilians in war adventure
CH7|the rival|competitive equal who opposes the hero's methods but may share goals — not evil, just antagonistic|provides conflict without villainy, may become ally, tests hero's competence and character|rival captains, competing treasure hunters, professional enemies who respect each other
CH8|the betrayer|trusted figure who changes allegiance — betrayal is a turning point in the narrative|shatters assumptions, forces adaptation, provides emotional devastation and plot reversal|Long John Silver (ambiguous), Saruman, Judas figures in war narratives
CH9|the local/native guide|person with knowledge of the territory who aids or hinders the expedition|bridges the known and unknown, provides cultural encounter, may embody colonial dynamics|Queequeg (Moby-Dick), native guides in exploration narratives, Chingachgook (Cooper)
CH10|the comic relief|character whose humor lightens tone without undermining danger — the joker under pressure|manages reader exhaustion, humanizes the group, provides contrast to grimness|Gimli (film), Porthos, Rincewind (Pratchett), Passepartout (Verne)
CH11|the specialist|character with specific technical skill essential to the mission — surgeon, engineer, linguist, demolitions|enables specific plot solutions, provides exposition through expertise, diversity of competence in group|ship's surgeon, explosives expert, translator in expedition, Scotty/Bones archetype

# conflict_patterns(id|pattern|description|escalation_dynamic|resolution)
CP1|hero vs nature|environment is the primary antagonist — storm, cold, heat, terrain, disease, starvation|conditions worsen; resources deplete; the body deteriorates; nature does not negotiate|survival through skill, endurance, and adaptation — or death; nature is not defeated, only survived
CP2|hero vs villain|personal opposition between protagonist and antagonist — may be physical, intellectual, or moral|encounters escalate — each meeting raises stakes; final confrontation is decisive|duel (physical or intellectual), exposure, capture, or moral victory; the personal element demands personal resolution
CP3|hero vs institution|the hero opposes a corrupt or incompetent system — military, colonial, legal, commercial|the institution's resources dwarf the individual; the hero uses mobility and conviction against bureaucratic mass|reform from within, destruction of the institution, escape from its reach, or martyrdom that inspires change
CP4|hero vs self|internal conflict — fear, temptation, despair, addiction, moral compromise — the hero's own weakness threatens the mission|the internal flaw grows under pressure; external challenges amplify internal struggle|self-mastery (or tragic failure); the hero chooses who to be at the crisis point
CP5|hero vs the unknown|the challenge is that the adversary/terrain/situation is not understood — information is the scarce resource|each encounter reveals more but also shows how much remains unknown; mystery deepens before resolving|understanding achieved through observation, experiment, and risk — knowing the enemy enables defeating it
CP6|hero vs time|a deadline drives the narrative — the bomb, the tide, the closing window, the approaching army|tension increases as deadline approaches; shortcuts create new dangers; the clock is always visible|the hero barely succeeds in time, or fails and faces consequences; the time pressure prevents optimal solutions
CP7|hero vs rival|competition with an equal — both pursue the same objective; only one can achieve it|rivals force each other to higher performance; mutual respect grows alongside opposition|the rival is defeated, converted to ally, or both lose to the larger threat; pure rivalry rarely survives shared danger
CP8|group vs attrition|the expedition is worn down by cumulative losses — members die, supplies deplete, morale breaks|each loss raises stakes for survivors; the group shrinks toward the minimum viable for success|the survivors achieve the objective at great cost, or the remnant retreats having failed; the cost is the point
CP9|betrayal and trust|a trusted member betrays the group — the real danger was inside all along|trust erodes; suspicion poisons collaboration; the betrayer is eventually exposed but damage is done|confrontation with the betrayer; group reconstitutes around renewed trust; or the betrayal succeeds and the hero must recover alone

# pacing(id|element|description|function|failure_if_wrong)
PA1|action sequence|extended physical conflict — chase, battle, duel, escape, storm — the set piece|releases accumulated tension, demonstrates competence, advances plot through physical events|too long = exhausting; too frequent = numbing; too short = unsatisfying
PA2|tension/dread|building sense of approaching danger — the reader knows something bad is coming|creates anticipation, engages reader's imagination (what the reader imagines is worse than what the author shows)|too long without payoff = frustration; too short = no buildup; absent = action feels random
PA3|recovery/respite|quiet period between crises — characters rest, plan, bond, reflect, mourn|prevents reader exhaustion, enables character development, makes next crisis hit harder by contrast|too long = momentum dies; absent = relentless pace causes numbness; must contain character work, not dead space
PA4|revelation/discovery|new information changes understanding — a map, a confession, a discovery, a decoded message|reorients the narrative, provides intellectual engagement alongside physical, justifies quest direction changes|too convenient = deus ex machina; too obscure = reader lost; must be earned through prior action or investigation
PA5|complication/reversal|plan fails, ally betrays, route blocked, new enemy appears — situation worsens unexpectedly|prevents predictability, raises stakes, forces protagonist to adapt, demonstrates resilience|too frequent = reader stops trusting any positive development; too rare = story feels easy
PA6|climactic convergence|all threads, conflicts, and characters converge in a single sustained sequence — the final confrontation|maximum tension, maximum payoff, all preparation pays off or fails spectacularly|must be prepared by all preceding narrative; unearned convergence is chaos, not climax
PA7|denouement/aftermath|post-climax settling — consequences shown, wounds tended, the new status quo established|provides closure, shows cost, enables the return phase, gives reader decompression|too long = anticlimax; too short = reader feels abandoned; absent = story just stops
PA8|cliffhanger|chapter or section ends at moment of maximum uncertainty — danger unresolved|compels reader to continue, creates urgency across structural breaks|overused = manipulative; underused in adventure = missed opportunity; false cliffhanger (immediately resolved) = cheating
PA9|parallel action|intercutting between simultaneous events in different locations — cross-cutting|builds tension by delaying resolution of each thread, shows scope of conflict|too many threads = confusion; timing must be coordinated — reader must understand simultaneity
PA10|ticking clock|deadline introduced — rescue before execution, escape before the tide, arrive before the enemy|converts spatial journey into temporal urgency — distance becomes time|arbitrary deadlines feel contrived; organic deadlines (tide, season, enemy march) feel natural

# prose_techniques(id|technique|description|function_in_adventure|example)
PT1|kinetic prose|sentence structure mirrors action — short sentences for speed, fragments for impact, longer for tension building|physical engagement — the reader's reading pace matches the character's experience|Cornwell (battle sequences), McCarthy (sparse, percussive), Fleming (action sequences)
PT2|environmental description as character|terrain, weather, sea state described with the specificity and emotional weight of a character|the setting IS the antagonist — it must be as vivid and present as any human adversary|Melville (the sea), McCarthy (the landscape), Conrad (the river/jungle), London (the cold)
PT3|technical specificity|correct terminology for ships, weapons, terrain, navigation, survival technique — precision builds credibility|competence fiction — the reader trusts the narrative because the details are right|O'Brian (naval detail), Cornwell (military), Krakauer (climbing), Weir (engineering in The Martian)
PT4|compression and ellipsis|skipping routine events — "three weeks passed without incident" — to maintain pace|adventure cannot narrate every day; compression keeps the reader at the interesting parts|routine in Treasure Island elided; weeks of sailing in O'Brian compressed to weather and incidents
PT5|in medias res|opening in the middle of action — backstory delivered later through dialogue, flashback, or inference|immediate engagement — the reader is in danger before they understand the context|many thrillers, Odyssey (classic example — starts near the end), Doyle (Holmes stories — case already underway)
PT6|unreliable reporter|first-person narrator whose account is colored by bias, fear, bravado, or limited perspective|creates gap between event and interpretation — reader must judge the narrator's reliability|Treasure Island (Jim Hawkins — youthful unreliability), Moby-Dick (Ishmael — philosophical digression), Gene Wolfe
PT7|sensory immersion|privileging physical sensation — what the body feels, smells, hears, tastes — over abstraction|grounds the reader in physical reality — adventure is a bodily experience|London (cold, hunger, exhaustion), O'Brian (ship motion, sea spray), McCarthy (heat, dust, blood)
PT8|dialogue under pressure|conversation during or between crises — characters reveal themselves through what they say when afraid, exhausted, or dying|efficient characterization — no leisure for expository dialogue; every word carries weight|Hemingway (sparse, understated), O'Brian (professional brevity), Cornwell (battlefield communication)
PT9|map and territory|the narrative follows a map — the reader tracks progress spatially, distance remaining creates tension|geographic progress = narrative progress; the reader knows where the hero is and how far they have to go|Treasure Island (the map IS the plot), Lord of the Rings, any quest with a mapped route
PT10|foreshadowing through environment|landscape, weather, animal behavior, local superstition hint at coming danger|organic tension — the world itself warns the attentive reader|storm clouds before the battle, vultures circling before the massacre, locals refusing to go further

# themes(id|theme|description|how_adventure_explores_it|exemplars)
TH1|courage|acting despite fear — not absence of fear but mastery of it|the hero is afraid and acts anyway — the reader sees the fear and the choice|Aubrey (facing battle), Bilbo (stepping out the door), Santiago (the old man vs the sea)
TH2|endurance|surviving what should destroy — physical, psychological, moral stamina|the adventure strips everything away and asks: what remains when comfort, safety, and hope are gone?|Shackleton (Endurance), Robinson Crusoe, The Road (McCarthy), The Martian
TH3|honor/code|personal principles tested by extreme circumstances — the hero must choose between survival and principle|the code is easy to follow in safety; adventure forces the choice that reveals whether the code is real|d'Artagnan (Musketeers), Sharpe (Cornwell), Aubrey (O'Brian), western heroes (L'Amour)
TH4|freedom|escape from constraint — literal (prison, captivity) or figurative (social, institutional, psychological)|adventure as liberation — the journey frees the hero from the prison of ordinary life or literal imprisonment|Monte Cristo, Huck Finn, Robinson Crusoe (ambiguous — freed by and imprisoned by the island)
TH5|nature's indifference|the natural world does not care about human survival, purpose, or justice — it simply IS|the storm does not know you exist; the cold has no malice; survival requires accepting nature's indifference|London (To Build a Fire — definitive), McCarthy (The Road), Melville (the whale), Krakauer
TH6|civilization vs wildness|the hero moves between ordered society and untamed territory — each reveals the other's limitations|the frontier is where civilization's rules break down and the hero must create their own|Cooper (Leatherstocking), Conrad (Heart of Darkness), Twain (Huck Finn), McCarthy, London
TH7|loyalty and betrayal|bonds tested under pressure — the companion who stands and the one who turns|adventure compresses years of relationship testing into weeks or days of crisis — loyalty is proven or disproven fast|Treasure Island (Silver's shifting loyalty), Dumas (friendship under fire), war narratives
TH8|the cost of victory|success requires sacrifice — the hero returns but diminished, grieving, or carrying scars|adventure that ends without cost is wish-fulfillment, not literature — the reader must feel what was paid|Lord of the Rings (Frodo can't go home), Moby-Dick (everyone dies except Ishmael), The Road
TH9|coming of age|the young hero's first encounter with real danger matures them — innocence lost, competence gained|the adventure IS the maturation — the boy who left is not the man who returns|Treasure Island (definitive), Huckleberry Finn, Earthsea, Hatchet, Ender's Game (SF)
TH10|colonial encounter|the hero enters territory belonging to others — the adventure narrative's complicity in colonialism|adventure literature historically celebrated exploration and conquest; modern treatments interrogate this|Conrad (Heart of Darkness — ambiguous critique), Haggard (celebratory), modern revisionism
TH11|mortality|the hero confronts death directly — their own, their companions', their enemies' — and must continue|adventure makes death present and physical — not abstract but a body on the ground, a ship going down|Hemingway (bullfight, war), O'Brian (naval casualties), McCarthy (pervasive death)
TH12|the sublime|encounter with vastness that exceeds human comprehension — oceanic, mountainous, cosmic, temporal|adventure takes the hero to places where human scale is humiliated by natural scale — the reader shares the awe|Melville (the sea), Krakauer (Everest), Verne (undersea, underground), Tolkien (mountains, ancient forests)
TH13|self-knowledge|the journey reveals the hero to themselves — strengths and weaknesses exposed by extremity|you don't know who you are until you're tested — adventure is the test|Heart of Darkness (Marlow discovers his own darkness), Treasure Island (Jim discovers his courage and limits)

# conventions(id|convention|description|function|risk)
CV1|the map|physical map included in the book or described in detail — the reader can follow the journey geographically|spatial orientation, anticipation, progress tracking|map that doesn't match narrative distances; map that spoils the route
CV2|the set piece|major action sequence crafted as a standalone spectacle — the battle, the storm, the duel, the escape|demonstrates the author's descriptive power, provides memorable moments, justifies the genre|set pieces that don't advance plot or character; set pieces that violate established rules
CV3|the companion's death|a member of the group dies — raising stakes, providing emotional devastation, proving the danger is real|the reader must believe anyone can die; the death must cost the group something irreplaceable|death for shock value without narrative purpose; death of undeveloped character (no emotional impact)
CV4|the narrow escape|hero survives by the smallest margin — skill, luck, or sacrifice barely suffices|maintains tension — the reader believes the hero could have died; demonstrates resourcefulness|too frequent = plot armor visible; too easy = no tension; too lucky = no skill demonstrated
CV5|the villain's speech|adversary explains motives, reveals plan, or offers the hero a choice before the final confrontation|provides antagonist depth, creates dramatic pause before climax, offers moral complexity|villain who talks when they should act (Bond villain syndrome); speech that exists only for exposition
CV6|first-person retrospective|narrator tells the story from a point after the events — "I remember when..."|creates dramatic irony (we know the narrator survived), enables reflection, justifies detailed recall|can reduce tension (narrator obviously survived); unreliable narration may frustrate
CV7|the exotic setting|detailed description of unfamiliar locale — architecture, food, customs, landscape — the reader travels vicariously|vicarious experience is half the pleasure of adventure fiction; the reader goes where they cannot go|exoticizing that reduces other cultures to spectacle; inaccuracy that reveals ignorance
CV8|the ticking deadline|specific time pressure introduced — "we have three days before the monsoon" or "the execution is at dawn"|converts distance into urgency; prevents leisurely problem-solving; forces suboptimal decisions|arbitrary deadlines feel contrived; deadlines introduced too late feel desperate
CV9|in medias res opening|story begins during action — explanation comes later|immediate hook — reader is in danger before they understand context|confusion if context is withheld too long; whiplash if opening action is abandoned for lengthy backstory
CV10|the mentor's parting gift|mentor dies or departs but leaves behind knowledge, weapon, or insight that proves critical later|connects early and late narrative, honors the mentor's role, provides earned plot resolution|gift that is too perfectly suited to the final problem (contrived); gift forgotten until needed (convenient)

# influential_works(id|work|author|year|contribution|legacy)
IW1|Odyssey|Homer|~8th c BCE|established the adventure template — the journey home through episodic trials, the hero's cunning, the faithful wife, the son's search|every return-journey adventure descends from this; Odysseus is the prototype of the resourceful hero
IW2|Treasure Island|Robert Louis Stevenson|1883|invented the treasure-hunt adventure — the map, the ship, the mutiny, the boy hero, the ambiguous villain (Long John Silver)|template for all treasure hunts; Silver is the prototype of the charismatic villain; Jim Hawkins is the prototype YA adventure hero
IW3|The Three Musketeers|Alexandre Dumas|1844|defined swashbuckling adventure — brotherhood, swordplay, intrigue, honor, wit, pace|"all for one, one for all" as the adventure fellowship motto; proved adventure could be comic, romantic, and thrilling simultaneously
IW4|Moby-Dick|Herman Melville|1851|fused adventure with philosophical depth — the obsessive quest, the sea as cosmos, the whale as unknowable other|proved adventure could contain the highest literary ambition; Ahab as the hero-villain who is both; the sea as ultimate setting
IW5|Heart of Darkness|Joseph Conrad|1899|the journey upriver as descent into the self — adventure structure carrying moral and colonial critique|every "journey into darkness" narrative descends from this; Kurtz as what the hero could become; colonialism as adventure's shadow
IW6|The Call of the Wild|Jack London|1903|non-human protagonist in extreme environment — survival as atavistic return, nature as ultimate reality|defined the survival-against-nature narrative; proved adventure prose could be both visceral and literary
IW7|The Lord of the Rings|J.R.R. Tolkien|1954-55|the quest-adventure elevated to mythic scale — the fellowship, the journey, the sacrifice, the return|integrated adventure structure with world-building depth; every epic quest adventure responds to this
IW8|Master and Commander (series)|Patrick O'Brian|1969-2004|the sea adventure as literary art — Aubrey-Maturin as the definitive adventure partnership; technical mastery + emotional depth|set the standard for historical adventure prose; proved genre fiction could sustain 20 volumes without declining
IW9|Blood Meridian|Cormac McCarthy|1985|the anti-adventure — the western frontier as apocalyptic violence without redemption; Judge Holden as war incarnate|deconstructed the frontier adventure myth; proved adventure's darkest extreme; prose of biblical intensity
IW10|The Martian|Andy Weir|2011|modern survival adventure driven by engineering competence — humor under pressure, problem-solving as heroism|revived the competence-hero; proved technical detail could be entertaining; Robinson Crusoe on Mars
IW11|Lonesome Dove|Larry McMurtry|1985|the epic western — friendship, aging, the last frontier, the cattle drive as odyssey|elegiac farewell to the frontier myth; proved the western could be literary and emotionally devastating
IW12|The Count of Monte Cristo|Alexandre Dumas|1844-46|the definitive revenge adventure — imprisonment, escape, reinvention, elaborate vengeance, mercy|the template for revenge narratives; proved adventure could sustain 1000+ pages through plot architecture alone

# rules(id|rule|domain|rationale|violation_consequence)
RL1|the hero must act — passivity is fatal to adventure|character|adventure IS action; a hero who is acted upon is not a hero but a victim; internal conflict must manifest as external choice|reader disengagement — the protagonist becomes a camera, not a character; the story happens around them
RL2|peril must have real cost — someone must bleed, lose, or die|stakes|danger that never damages is not danger — the reader learns to ignore it; plot armor destroys tension|the reader stops worrying — combat becomes choreography, not drama; victory feels hollow
RL3|competence must be demonstrated before it is relied upon|character|the hero's skills must be shown in lower-stakes situations before being used to resolve high-stakes ones|deus ex machina — hero suddenly reveals ability never established; reader feels cheated
RL4|the setting must be as vivid as any character — it must resist the hero|setting|adventure fiction is inseparable from place — the jungle, the ocean, the mountain must be felt physically|generic setting produces generic adventure — the story could happen anywhere, so it matters nowhere
RL5|the unknown must remain partially unknown — complete explanation kills wonder|tone|if every mystery is solved, every creature catalogued, every culture fully explained, the adventure world shrinks to a textbook|wonder dies, the exotic becomes mundane, the reader has no reason to imagine beyond the page
RL6|recovery must follow crisis — relentless action numbs|pacing|the human body and mind cannot sustain peak performance indefinitely — neither can the reader's attention|reader exhaustion, numbing, inability to distinguish important from routine action
RL7|the villain must be competent enough to threaten the hero genuinely|character|an incompetent villain makes the hero's victory meaningless — the greater the opposition, the greater the heroism|stakes collapse — the reader knows the villain will lose; confrontations become formalities
RL8|the companion's death must be earned and mourned — not discarded for plot convenience|character|characters the reader has invested in cannot be killed casually — the death must serve the story AND be felt|reader anger if character is wasted; reader indifference if undeveloped character dies; trust broken either way
RL9|technical detail must serve narrative, not replace it|prose|accuracy builds credibility but excessive detail displaces story — the reader needs enough to believe, not enough to build a ship|the narrative drowns in specification — the reader skims or abandons; the story becomes a manual
RL10|the return must show cost — the hero cannot come back unchanged|structure|adventure without consequence is wish-fulfillment; the return reveals what the journey took and gave|flat ending — the hero is the same person, the world is the same world; the journey was tourism, not transformation
RL11|every journey must have a destination, even if the destination changes — aimless wandering is not adventure|structure|the quest object provides direction; direction provides rising action; rising action provides climax|picaresque drift — incidents happen without accumulation; there is no climax because there is no goal
RL12|the code must be tested to destruction — easy adherence is not heroism|theme|honor that is never challenged is merely habit; the moment the code costs the hero everything is the moment it becomes real|the code is decorative — the hero never faces a situation where following it means losing something they love

# failure_modes(id|mode|cause|symptom|prevention)
FM1|travelogue adventure|beautiful settings, no conflict — the hero walks through a landscape without encountering resistance|reader bored — lovely prose, nothing happens; the postcards are nice but there's no story|every setting must contain or produce conflict; beauty without danger is nature writing, not adventure
FM2|invincible hero|protagonist faces danger but is never seriously threatened — skill or luck always prevails without cost|reader stops worrying; combat scenes become performative; tension evaporates|real injuries, real losses, real failures that the hero must recover from; RL2
FM3|villain decay|antagonist established as formidable but becomes progressively less threatening through incompetent execution|the reader downgrades the threat; the climactic confrontation feels unearned|maintain the villain's competence throughout; if the villain loses, it should be by the narrowest margin; RL7
FM4|exposition dump disguised as dialogue|characters explain things they both know for the reader's benefit — "as you know, we have three days until the monsoon"|immersion broken; characters feel like puppets; the author's hand visible|dramatize information through action, discovery, and conflict — not through conversation
FM5|consequence-free violence|extensive combat with no psychological or physical aftermath — characters kill and move on unchanged|violence becomes meaningless; the hero's moral standing is never questioned; the reader becomes numb|show aftermath — physical (wounds, exhaustion), psychological (guilt, nightmares), social (reputation, law)
FM6|colonial gaze|non-Western cultures depicted as exotic backdrop, childlike, dangerous, or static — existing only for the hero to encounter|morally offensive, historically inaccurate, artistically impoverished — reduces cultures to props|give all cultures interiority, agency, and complexity; acknowledge the hero's outsider status; question the encounter
FM7|momentum collapse|action stops for extended backstory, exposition, or character reflection at precisely the wrong moment|the reader was leaning forward and is forced to sit back; re-engaging costs more effort than maintaining would have|backstory through action, reflection through dialogue under pressure, exposition through discovery not lecture
FM8|MacGuffin emptiness|the quest object turns out to be meaningless — it doesn't matter what it is, only that people chase it|the entire journey's purpose collapses retroactively; the reader feels their investment was wasted|the quest object must matter — its nature must shape the story's meaning, not merely its plot
FM9|adventure without return|the story ends at the climax — no aftermath, no homecoming, no reckoning with what was lost and gained|the reader is denied closure; the transformation is incomplete; the cost is unaccounted|the return need not be long but must be present — even a paragraph showing the changed hero in the changed or unchanged world
FM10|competence porn|hero is so skilled that every challenge is easily solved — the pleasure is watching mastery, not worrying about outcome|no tension — admiration replaces anxiety; the story flatters rather than challenges|give the hero weaknesses that matter in this specific adventure; competence in one area, vulnerability in another
